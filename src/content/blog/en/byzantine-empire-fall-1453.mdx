## A Thousand-Year Empire

In 395 CE, the Roman Empire split into East and West. When the Western Roman Empire fell to Germanic tribes in 476 CE, the East lived on. And it kept living — for **another thousand years**.

Its capital was Constantinople (modern Istanbul). Its emperors called themselves "Emperor of the Romans" to the very end, though their official language shifted from Latin to Greek. This was the **Byzantine Empire**.

---

## Justinian: The Golden Age

In the 6th century, **Emperor Justinian I (r. 527–565)** brought the empire to its peak.

**Corpus Juris Civilis**: A comprehensive codification of Roman law that became the foundation of European legal systems — the roots of the continental legal tradition that still governs much of the world.

**Territorial reconquest**: Justinian retook Italy, North Africa, and parts of Spain, briefly making the Mediterranean once again a "Roman sea."

**Hagia Sophia**: Built 532–537, it remained the world's largest Christian cathedral for nearly a thousand years and stands to this day.

<Callout type="tip">
**Justinian's paradox**: His military campaigns brought a flash of imperial glory but drained the treasury. Within decades of his death, most reconquered territories were lost — setting up centuries of gradual decline.
</Callout>

---

## Greek Fire: The Secret Weapon

The Byzantine navy possessed a weapon that terrified enemies: **Greek Fire** — an incendiary substance that burned on water and couldn't be extinguished with it.

This weapon defeated two Arab fleets besieging Constantinople (678 and 718 CE). Had Constantinople fallen then, European history would have been radically different.

The exact composition of Greek Fire remains unknown — one of history's most successfully kept secrets.

---

## The Long Decline

The 7th century brought the Islamic conquests: Syria, Palestine, and Egypt were lost. In 1071, the Battle of Manzikert saw most of Anatolia fall to the Seljuk Turks. Most shockingly, the Fourth Crusade in 1204 — launched by fellow Christians — sacked Constantinople itself and established a "Latin Empire" for 57 years.

When Byzantium was restored in 1261, it was a shadow of itself — controlling little more than Constantinople and parts of Greece.

---

## 1453: The End of the Middle Ages

In April–May 1453, **Ottoman Sultan Mehmed II** besieged Constantinople with 100,000 troops. The Byzantine garrison numbered perhaps 7,000.

The ancient walls had held for a millennium. This time was different. Mehmed deployed massive bronze cannons — including the **Urban bombard**, casting stone balls weighing over 500 kg — that systematically breached the supposedly impregnable Theodosian Walls.

On May 29, Ottoman troops flooded through the gaps. The last emperor, Constantine XI, stripped off his imperial purple cloak and charged into the fighting. He was never seen again.

---

## Legacy: The Spark of the Renaissance

After the fall, thousands of Byzantine scholars fled to Italy, bringing Greek manuscripts and classical learning. This influx is widely credited with fueling the **Italian Renaissance**. The irony: Byzantine civilization's destruction helped birth European modernity.
