# The Gravity of Finance: Bond Pricing and Yields

When you buy a bond, what gets fixed is the contractual interest and the repayment terms. What an investor actually earns depends on the purchase price, on whether payments are made as promised, on reinvestment rates, and on the point of sale. That is why two bonds with the same face value and the same coupon can carry different yields — the difference is what you paid.

This chapter covers **fixed-rate bonds with no options**: no call, no put, no early redemption. The calculation assumes every contractual cash flow is paid and that there are no taxes or fees. Do not apply these results directly to floating-rate bonds, to bonds with material default risk, or to bonds with early-redemption terms. The examples are for learning, not a product recommendation.

We keep two problems apart. Set a required return and discount the cash flows, and you get a valuation. Start from a market price and solve for the discount rate that explains it, and you get a yield to maturity. Both use the same equation; what differs is what you put in and what you take out.

---

## 1. When Price and Yield Move in Opposite Directions

Discounting the same positive cash flows at the same point in time, a **higher** discount rate produces a **lower** present value. That is the inverse relationship between the price of a fixed-rate bond and its yield. Note what "the rate" means here: it is not merely the headline policy rate in the news, but **the discount rate at which that bond's cash flows are valued**. A change in how credit risk or liquidity is assessed changes the required return too.

Stable rates alone do not mean a bond trades at face value. In the simple model that values a bond on its coupon payment dates, it equals par when the discount rate quoted at the same periodicity as the coupon equals the coupon rate. As time passes the remaining payment schedule changes too, so keep this distinct from the "unchanged" row above.

## 2. Working Through One Bond End to End

Consider a hypothetical bond with a face value of ₩1,000, an annual coupon rate of 5%, and 2 years remaining to maturity. We are immediately after the most recent coupon was paid, and the next one arrives exactly one year from now. You receive ₩50 at the end of each year, and at the end of year 2 you receive the final ₩50 coupon together with the ₩1,000 principal. Accrued interest is currently 0.

```text
Now:      pay price P
+1 year:  receive 50
+2 years: receive 50 + principal 1,000

P = 50/(1+y) + 1,050/(1+y)²
(y is the annual effective yield)
```

| Discount rate y | PV of 50 in 1 year | PV of 1,050 in 2 years | Price P |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 3% | 48.54 | 989.73 | 1,038.27 |
| 5% | 47.62 | 952.38 | 1,000.00 |
| 10% | 45.45 | 867.77 | 913.22 |

The table rounds the intermediate values for display while the totals are computed from unrounded figures. Even though the ₩50 coupon never changes, raising the discount rate from 5% to 10% takes the price from ₩1,000 down to roughly ₩913.22.

Real bonds require you to check semiannual frequency, accrued interest, day-count conventions, and yield-quoting practice. Between payment dates, the settlement price includes accrued interest while the quoted clean price does not. When yields differ across maturities, a spot-rate curve is used to discount each payment at the rate matching its timing. The table above is simplified to annual payments and a single discount rate.

## 3. YTM Is the Discount Rate That Explains the Market Price

**Yield to maturity (YTM)** is the internal rate of return that makes the present value of the contractual cash flows equal to the current price. If the market price of the bond above is exactly 50/1.1 + 1,050/1.1², the YTM is 10% per year. Plugging in a rounded figure such as the displayed 913.22 gives approximately 10% as well.

A 10% YTM does not guarantee a 10% compound annual growth rate. That holds only if payments arrive as contracted, the bond is held to maturity, and coupons can be reinvested at the same yield — then the compound return on the maturity asset value matches the YTM. Early sale, default, a change in reinvestment rates, taxes and fees all change the realised return.

**Current yield** is the annual coupon divided by the current market price. In this example 50/913.22314 ≈ **5.48%**. The 5% obtained by dividing by face value is the coupon rate, and it differs from the 10% YTM. Current yield is not a measure that reflects both the price difference settled at maturity and when the money actually arrives.

## 4. The Trap in Comparing a Valuation to a Market Price

Take the YTM implied by the market price, discount the same cash flows at that YTM, and you land back on the original market price. **You cannot find an undervaluation this way.** You have simply re-derived the same price.

If, for this bond, you had separately decided that the required return is 8% per year, the valuation is 50/1.08 + 1,050/1.08² ≈ **₩946.50**. That is above the market price of ₩913.22, but the gap exists **only under the assumption that an 8% required return is appropriate**. It may equally be the result of leaving out risk the market priced in, so it should not be read straight away as a risk-free gain or a buy signal.

## 5. Does a Bond Always Converge to Par as Maturity Approaches?

<Callout type="note">
  A bond that repays its contractual principal in full returns the face principal at maturity. If a final coupon is also paid, the final payment is principal plus interest. When explaining convergence to par, keep the price excluding accrued interest distinct from the price on a payment date.
</Callout>

In a simple model with other conditions held constant, discount and premium bonds do tend to approach par as maturity nears. That does **not** mean the actual market price moves in one direction every day. A change in rates or credit risk changes the path, and if the bond defaults the face repayment itself may never happen. Early-redemption options are a separate item to review.

## Check Your Understanding

**Q. A 5% coupon rate, a current yield of about 5.48%, and a 10% YTM contradict each other. Do they?**

> No. They are respectively an interest rate measured against face value, an annual coupon measured against the market price, and a discount rate linking the price to the entire contractual cash flow.

**Q. I computed a price using the YTM derived from the market price and got the market price back. Does that prove the price is fair?**

> No. It confirms an identity in the calculation. Arguing that a bond is relatively over- or undervalued requires an independent valuation basis.

The next chapter covers duration and convexity, which give an **approximation** of how price responds to a change in yield. There is no formula that always guarantees exactly how many percent the price moves for a one-percentage-point move in yield.
