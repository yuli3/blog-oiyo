## The Myth That a Security Deposit Is Free Money

Imagine two apartments. Apartment A requires a $30,000 security deposit and no monthly rent — just utilities. Apartment B requires a $500 deposit and $1,500/month rent. A first-year renter might immediately prefer Apartment A: "The money comes back, so I'm not really spending it."

But here is what that reasoning misses: $30,000 invested in a high-yield savings account at 4.5% generates **$112/month in interest**. The moment you hand that money to a landlord as a deposit, you give up $112 every month — not temporarily, but for the full duration of the lease. The real monthly cost of Apartment A is at least $112, just from the opportunity cost of lost interest. If Apartment B costs $1,500/month but you keep your $30,000 invested, the comparison is much closer than it first appeared.

This is not a trick — it is **opportunity cost**, and it is the single most important concept in housing economics.

The general principle: when you lock up a large security deposit, you forgo the return that money could earn elsewhere. If the prevailing interest rate is 4%, a $30,000 deposit carries an opportunity cost of $1,200/year — or **$100/month**.

That means a deposit-heavy arrangement is economically equivalent to paying rent of $100/month — even though no monthly check is written.

## How to Find the Break-Even Point

Use these formulas to identify when each option is more cost-effective:

**True monthly cost of deposit-only arrangement** = (Security Deposit × Interest Rate) ÷ 12

**True monthly cost of rent arrangement** = (Rent Security Deposit × Interest Rate ÷ 12) + Monthly Rent

The two values are equal at the break-even point.

**Example**: Large deposit = $30,000, small deposit = $1,000, applicable rate = 4.5%
- Deposit arrangement monthly cost: $30,000 × 4.5% ÷ 12 = **$112.50/month**
- Rent arrangement monthly cost: $1,000 × 4.5% ÷ 12 + rent = $3.75 + rent

→ If monthly rent is **below $108.75**, the rent arrangement is cheaper. Above that, the deposit arrangement saves money.

<Callout>
This calculation assumes you have the security deposit in cash. If you borrow to fund the deposit, the interest on that loan adds directly to your monthly cost — potentially making the deposit arrangement significantly more expensive.
</Callout>

## Additional Risks to Consider with Large Deposits

Beyond the financial math, large security deposits carry risks that monthly rent does not:

1. **Landlord financial health**: if the landlord defaults or faces foreclosure, recovering a large deposit can be difficult or require litigation
2. **Rent escrow laws vary by jurisdiction**: understand your state or local protections for deposit recovery
3. **Inflation erodes the real value of the returned deposit**: $30,000 returned in two years is worth less in purchasing power than $30,000 today
4. **Move-in condition disputes**: large deposits create higher-stakes disputes about deductions for cleaning, repairs, and normal wear

## How to Use This Calculator Effectively

Enter the deposit amount and monthly rent you are actually comparing — not idealized numbers. Then set the interest rate to what you could realistically earn (current high-yield savings rates or short-term CD yields are a good proxy).

Compare the two "true monthly cost" figures the calculator produces. If the deposit arrangement's monthly cost is lower, the deposit is the better financial choice. If higher, monthly rent saves money.

If you need to borrow to fund the deposit, add the monthly loan interest cost to the deposit arrangement's figure. This often dramatically changes the comparison and is the step most people skip.

Finally, remember that the numbers are not the entire decision. Flexibility, credit risk to the landlord, your savings rate versus investment return expectations, and local market dynamics all matter. The calculator gives you the financial baseline; the rest is judgment.
