# Financial Economics — Risk-free rates differ across maturities

The risk-free rate introduced earlier covered one period. In practice, government-bond yields differ by maturity. The risk-free rate used in a firm's WACC is also an input to [financial management](/en/education-finance-ch12/). Here we read the yield curve through asset prices: what, exactly, does it say about expectations?

## 1. No-arbitrage and the expectations hypothesis are different claims

Begin with **zero-coupon bonds** that share credit, currency, and liquidity characteristics. Their prices define spot and forward rates at each maturity. No-arbitrage links the prices of an investment fixed today for one year to those of a two-year investment and a forward contract. But **next year's one-year spot rate has not been fixed yet**. No-arbitrage alone therefore does not equate the *expected returns* on a long bond and a strategy that rolls over short bonds. That conclusion requires an extra assumption, such as the zero term premium of the pure expectations hypothesis.

Under suitable maturity and compounding approximations, the pure expectations hypothesis reads an $n$-year yield as the average expected path of future short rates. More generally, **the long yield combines that expected path and a term premium**. The term premium compensates for interest-rate risk, but its sign can be positive or negative depending on definition, model, and date. A separate *liquidity-premium hypothesis* assumes positive compensation; that is not a universal sign rule for term premia. An upward-sloping curve alone does not prove that short rates are expected to rise.

## 2. A forward rate is not the future realized spot rate

A forward rate is a **price-derived rate** calculated from bond prices traded today. A particular no-arbitrage term-structure model can relate it to a risk-neutral measure, but the observed forward rate cannot simply be read as the real-world expectation of the future short rate. Even writing “forward rate = risk-neutral expectation” without specifying the measure, compounding, and convexity assumptions is too compressed. Unforeseen shocks also affect the realized rate. The gap between a forward and an ex-post spot rate contains both a risk premium and forecast error; a simple regression slope that does not separate them cannot decide the expectations hypothesis.

For example, suppose the approximately 4.01% forward rate above contains a **+1 percentage-point term premium defined on the forward rate**. Then the real-world expectation of next year's short rate is approximately 3.01%. A −1 percentage-point premium would instead imply approximately 5.01%. If the 1-point premium were defined on the *two-year yield*, the calculation would differ. Always state which rate bears the premium.

When monetary policy moves short rates, the long-rate response depends on both expectations and premia. Breaking a nominal rate into real rates and expected inflation is a different layer from the real exchange rate studied in international finance. This chapter concerns risk-free claims across maturities.

<Callout type="warning">
The term structure and bond duration in financial management answer different questions. Duration approximates how a bond's price changes when its yield changes. Here the question is how yields across maturities reflect expectations and premia. A single CAPM beta does not explain the government-bond curve.
</Callout>

Bank runs and maturity transformation translate steepening curves or surging short rates into bank earnings and losses. The curve's theory belongs here; intermediary failure belongs in the banking-crisis discussion.

## Check your understanding

**Problem.** Zero-coupon bonds with the same credit and liquidity conditions have a one-year spot rate of 2% and a two-year spot rate of 3%. What is the one-year forward rate starting in one year under annual compounding? If that forward rate contains a +1 percentage-point term premium, what is the approximate real-world expected future one-year rate?

**Solution.** The forward rate is $(1.03)^2/1.02-1\approx4.01\%$. The problem defines the **+1 percentage-point premium on the forward rate**, so the expected short rate is approximately $4.01-1=3.01\%$. A negative premium or a premium attached to the *two-year yield* would change the answer. The two observed spot rates alone do not separately identify the risk premium or an expectation of rate increases.
