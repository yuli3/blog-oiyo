## Emotion isn't only an obstacle

Emotion often looks like a problem at the negotiating table. But emotion is also important information. Anxiety can signal insufficient preparation or the risk of loss; anger can signal a feeling of not being respected. Rather than trying to eliminate emotion, a negotiator needs to read what it's telling them.

The real problem isn't the emotion itself — it's reacting to it instantly, without pause. Negotiators need the ability to stop for a moment, separate fact from interpretation, and steer the conversation back to the actual issue.

## Separate the person from the problem

| Mixing them together | Separating them out |
|------------------|--------------------|
| "That person is just stubborn" | "There's probably a reason this condition matters to them" |
| "They're disrespecting me" | "That explanation just came across as blunt" |
| "They have zero intention of budging" | "We just haven't found a tradeable term yet" |

Attack the person, and they go on the defensive. Look at the problem together, and negotiation becomes a shared process of solving something again.

## Language that protects the relationship

"That doesn't make any sense" lands worse than "That condition is hard to execute on the current timeline." "You're wrong" lands worse than "The data I have shows something different." Keep your tone soft, but don't blur your actual standards — that's the balance to aim for.

Protecting the relationship doesn't mean saying only pleasant things. It means being clear about your limits and standards while still respecting the other person.

## A short routine for when emotions rise

First, pause before responding — take a breath. Next, summarize the other person's point back to confirm you understood it. Finally, steer the conversation back toward standards or options. For example: "So the concern you raised is the delivery delay. Between a Friday report and a follow-up next week, which one matters more to you?"

## Key takeaways

- Emotion is important information in negotiation, but reacting to it in the moment is risky.
- Separating the person from the problem protects the relationship while still addressing the issue.
- Soft language and clear standards can coexist.
- The stronger the emotion, the more summarizing, confirming, and offering options helps.
