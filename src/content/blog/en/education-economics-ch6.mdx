# Microeconomics — Long-Run Competitive Equilibrium Means Zero Economic Profit, Not Zero Accounting Profit

The individual firm of Chapter 5 produced where $P=MC$ and shut down at prices below minimum AVC. This chapter looks at markets with many such firms. In the short run the number of firms is fixed; in the long run profit attracts entry and losses cause exit.

The demand curve facing an individual firm is horizontal at the market price, because charging even slightly more loses every customer. Hence $MR=P$.

## 2. Short-run industry supply is the horizontal sum of firms' supply

Suppose 100 firms each have $MC=2q$ (in 10,000 won) and $AVC=q$. Each firm's supply is $P=2q$, i.e. $q=P/2$ (above AVC). Adding quantities at the same price gives industry supply $Q=100\times P/2=50P$.

With demand $Q=200-10P$, $50P=200-10P$ gives $P=10/3\approx 3.33$ (about 33,300 won) and $Q\approx 166.7$. Each firm produces about 1.67, where AVC ≈ 1.67 < 3.33, so every firm produces.

## 3. Long run: entry pushes price to minimum average cost

If profit is positive, new firms enter, supply rises and the price falls. If there are losses, firms exit, supply falls and the price rises. Adjustment stops when the price equals every firm's minimum average cost.

<FormulaBox
  label="Long-run competitive equilibrium"

  description="Firms produce optimally at P = MC, economic profit is zero, and each firm operates at the scale with the lowest average cost."
/>

**Zero economic profit** is not zero accounting profit. ATC includes the opportunity cost of the owners' capital and time (normal profit). Even if the books show a profit, economic profit is zero when that profit equals what the resources would earn elsewhere.

Consider a **constant-cost industry** in which all firms have the same costs and input prices do not depend on the size of the industry. If minimum ATC is 40 won (at an output of 4 per firm) and market demand is $P=120-Q$, the long-run equilibrium is $P=40$ won, $Q=80$ and 20 firms.

Suppose demand rises to $P=140-Q$. If the short-run supply of the existing 20 firms is $P=40+(Q-80)/2$:

- **Short run**: $140-Q=40+(Q-80)/2$ gives $Q\approx 93.3$ and $P\approx 46.7$ won. Existing firms make a profit.
- **Long run**: profit attracts entry and the price returns to 40 won. Quantity is $140-40=100$ and there are 25 firms.

## 4. Why competition is efficient

In long-run competitive equilibrium two kinds of efficiency hold at once.

- **Allocative efficiency**: $P=MC$. The value consumers place on the last unit equals its cost, so there is no deadweight loss.
- **Productive efficiency**: $P=\min ATC$. Each firm produces at its cheapest scale.

The monopoly of Chapter 7 violates the first, and the monopolistic competition of Chapter 8 the second. But this is a partial-equilibrium result that assumes no externalities and no information problems; Chapter 10 relaxes those conditions.

<Callout type="warning">
Free entry does not merely mean that the law allows entry. If licences, locations, brands or network effects act as de facto barriers, profits can persist without attracting entry. And if firms' costs differ, low-cost firms earn positive profit (rent) even in the long run. Even agricultural markets, the textbook example of perfect competition, do not fully meet the conditions because of quality differences and information asymmetries.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

What is the optimal output of a competitive firm with $P=30$ won, $MC=2Q$ and $AVC=Q$? $30=2Q$ gives $Q=15$. AVC is 15 won < 30 won, so the firm produces. With a fixed cost of 100 won, ATC is $15+100/15\approx 21.7$ won, so profit is about 8.3 won per unit and $30\times 15-(100+225)=125$ won in total. This profit attracts entry, which lowers the price.
