# Marketing Strategy — Target Selection, Positioning, and Brand Evidence

A marketing plan must explain which customers it will serve, what alternative they would otherwise choose, and how the firm can deliver its promise profitably. This chapter uses a fictional service business to connect STP and the marketing mix to a decision. The numbers are teaching assumptions, not market research or a forecast.

## 1. STP: classify, choose, then make a promise

Segmentation groups customers by relevant differences in needs or responses. Targeting commits resources to selected groups. Positioning specifies why those customers should choose this offer rather than an alternative. These are different decisions; a demographic label alone does not establish a profitable segment. See the definitions in [OpenStax §5.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-marketing/pages/5-1-market-segmentation-and-consumer-markets) and [§5.5](https://openstax.org/books/principles-marketing/pages/5-5-selecting-target-markets).

A fictional repair-booking service considers urgent customers and planned-maintenance customers. Both groups want a working appliance, but one values rapid appointments and the other values predictable prices. Survey interest is not the same as paid demand. The service must check whether technicians can meet the promised appointment window before advertising it.

### a. A target-selection calculation

Assume one campaign per segment, one completed order per acquired customer, and no repeat orders. Contribution per order is price minus the variable cost of fulfilling that order. Campaign spending is separate.

]} />

<FormulaBox label="One-campaign contribution after acquisition spending" formula="Net campaign contribution = visits × purchase rate × contribution per order − campaign cost" description="Urgent: 1,000 × 0.08 × USD 40 − USD 2,000 = USD 1,200. Planned: 1,000 × 0.05 × USD 50 − USD 1,000 = USD 1,500. These are conditional calculations, not profit forecasts." />

Planned maintenance has fewer orders but higher campaign contribution under these assumptions. Neither total revenue nor the purchase rate alone selects the target. The urgent offer breaks even at 50 orders, or a 5% purchase rate; the planned offer breaks even at 20 orders, or 2%. If urgent appointments require overtime not included in the USD 40 contribution, the comparison must be recalculated. Fixed costs, capacity, cancellations, and future purchases are outside this simplified calculation.

## 2. Make the 4Ps support the same position

A position such as “a confirmed appointment and an itemized estimate before work starts” needs an operating design, not just a slogan. Product, price, place, and promotion should reinforce that promise.

<LectureTable title="A coherent marketing mix" headers={["Decision", "Implementation and check"]} rows={[
  ["Product", "Define the repair scope and appointment commitment; measure missed appointments and rework."],
  ["Price", "Specify what the estimate includes and how extra work is approved; examine realized contribution, not list price alone."],
  ["Place", "Offer booking where the selected customers can use it; confirm technician coverage before expanding."],
  ["Promotion", "Show the actual estimate and booking process; do not advertise a service level that operations cannot deliver."],
]} />

A discount may lift orders while reducing contribution. A broader service area may attract demand while increasing travel costs. These are trade-offs to test together. The customer-oriented 4C lens can help ask about customer solution, total cost, convenience, and communication, but it does not replace the firm's capacity and cost checks.

## 3. Brand equity is not a quality guarantee

Brand equity concerns the additional value associated with a brand rather than an otherwise comparable offer; [OpenStax §9.5](https://openstax.org/books/principles-marketing/pages/9-5-branding-and-brand-development) introduces this distinction. Recognition, preference, repeat purchase, and willingness to pay are related but different observations. A familiar name can signal expected quality; it does not prove that every order will meet the promise.

For this fictional service, compare appointment reliability and complaints alongside awareness. A price premium alone does not isolate a brand effect: service scope, availability, channel, and customer composition may differ. An experiment intended to estimate a brand effect should specify what varies, what remains comparable, and which outcome is measured. Do not call a change in survey preference a proven increase in lifetime profit.

## 4. Acquisition and retention: define the denominator

Customer acquisition cost (CAC) must state its spending boundary and count of newly acquired customers. In the fictional campaign, acquisition spending divided by new customers gives USD 25 for urgent service (USD 2,000/80) and USD 20 for planned service (USD 1,000/50). These are campaign-only CAC figures, excluding any additional acquisition staff or overhead.

There is no universal acquisition-to-retention cost ratio to apply here. Retention spending divided by all existing customers, by contacted customers, or by additional customers retained produces different measures. Without an incremental outcome and a comparable time horizon, those figures cannot establish that one policy is cheaper.

<Callout type="warning">
Repeat customers may differ from first-time customers before any retention campaign. A higher repeat-purchase rate does not by itself prove the campaign caused it. Compare an appropriate control, net contribution, and observation period; state attribution and data limitations.
</Callout>

## Review problem

### Problem

The urgent campaign's purchase rate falls to 6%. Keep all other fictional assumptions unchanged. Which campaign has higher contribution after campaign spending, and can this comparison establish which segment has greater lifetime value?

### Worked answer

Urgent orders become 60. Contribution after campaign spending is 60 × USD 40 − USD 2,000 = USD 400. Planned maintenance remains USD 1,500. Planned maintenance therefore leads by USD 1,100 for the modeled campaign. Lifetime value cannot be ranked because repeat purchases, future margins, retention spending, and timing are not given. Nor does this calculation establish that the two visit populations are interchangeable.
