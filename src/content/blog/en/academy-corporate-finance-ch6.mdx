## What a Dividend Is

A **dividend** is how a firm returns some or all of its profit to the shareholders who supplied the capital for its operations. From shareholders' perspective, it's part of their investment return; from the firm's perspective, it also carries the character of a cost of using equity capital.

The dividend decision is one of the three financial decisions introduced in Chapter 1. A firm can retain the cash it earns for reinvestment, or pay it out to shareholders as dividends.

```
Use of firm profit
= retained earnings + dividends
```

Dividend policy looks separate from investment and financing policy, but the three are connected in practice. Paying out more in dividends leaves less internal funding and can increase the need for external financing; paying out less leaves more room for internal investment.

---

## Metrics for Dividend Levels

Dividend levels are tracked with several metrics.

| Metric | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Dividend per share (DPS) | The dividend paid per share |
| Payout ratio | The share of net income paid out as dividends |
| Retention ratio | The share of net income kept within the firm |
| Dividend yield | Dividends relative to the current share price |
| Dividend rate | Dividends relative to par value |

Payout ratio and retention ratio are linked.

```
Retention ratio = 1 - payout ratio
```

A stock investor's total return splits into capital gains and dividend yield.

```
Stock return = share price appreciation + dividend yield
```

So dividend policy isn't simply a question of whether to pay cash — it's about the form in which shareholders receive their return.

## MM's Dividend Irrelevance Theory

Under the assumption of perfect capital markets, MM's dividend irrelevance theory holds that **dividend policy has no effect on firm value**.

The key assumptions are:

1. Perfect capital markets
2. Dividend income and capital gains are treated identically
3. Investors share homogeneous expectations
4. Investment policy is unaffected by dividend policy

Consider an unlevered firm that finances any additional capital needs entirely through new share issues. If the firm pays out more in dividends, retained earnings fall by the same amount, and future dividends it can pay fall too. The increase in current dividends is offset by the decrease in future dividends, so firm value doesn't change.

```
Perfect capital markets + fixed investment policy
=> changing dividend policy only shifts the timing of cash flow distribution
=> no effect on firm value
```

This theory isn't really a claim that dividends don't matter in the real world — it's a benchmark showing what market friction or preference difference would have to exist for dividend policy to affect firm value.

## The Low-Payout Preference Theory

The **low-payout preference theory** holds that investors may prefer capital gains to dividends. The classic reason is taxes: if the tax rate on dividend income is higher than the capital gains tax rate, investors may prefer returns in the form of share price appreciation over current dividends.

From this view, a firm may benefit shareholders more by retaining earnings for reinvestment, or by using share buybacks instead of dividends.

```
Heavier dividend tax burden
=> investors prefer capital gains over current dividends
=> a low-payout policy may be preferred
```

Low-payout preference also connects to the logic of growth firms. A firm with many profitable investment opportunities may create more shareholder value by keeping cash internally and reinvesting it, rather than paying it out.

---

## The High-Payout Preference Theory

The **high-payout preference theory** holds that investors prefer a certain current dividend over an uncertain future capital gain. This view is often explained through the "bird in hand" logic.

If investors value a current dividend as reliable income, firms that pay high dividends may see their share prices bid up. In mature firms with stable cash flows in particular, dividends are sometimes interpreted as a signal of the firm's cash-generating power and its shareholder-friendly stance.

```
Preference for the certainty of a current dividend
=> higher dividend firms may be valued more highly
```

That said, high payouts aren't automatically good. If a firm pays dividends at the expense of forgoing investment opportunities, long-term firm value can suffer.

## The Practical Tools of Dividend Policy

Firms have more tools for returning value to shareholders than cash dividends alone. Share buybacks, stock dividends, and stock splits are also part of the toolkit.

| Type | Meaning | Shares outstanding | EPS | Share price | Shareholder wealth |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cash dividend | Dividends paid in cash | Unchanged | Unchanged | Adjusts for ex-dividend | Theoretically unchanged |
| Share buyback | The firm repurchases its own shares | Decreases | Increases | Upward pressure | Theoretically unchanged |
| Stock dividend | Retained earnings converted into capital stock, distributed as shares | Increases | Decreases | Adjusts downward | Unchanged |
| Stock split | Par value reduced, share count increased | Increases | Decreases | Adjusts downward | Unchanged |

A share buyback can have an effect similar to returning cash to shareholders in place of a cash dividend. Stock dividends and stock splits don't directly increase shareholder wealth on their own, but they can be read as boosting share liquidity or signaling confidence in the firm's growth.

## What Actually Drives Dividend Policy

In practice, firms weigh several factors when setting dividend policy.

1. Current net income and the stability of that income
2. The firm's cash liquidity
3. Growth rate and investment opportunities
4. Debt repayment obligations
5. Control and governance considerations
6. Shareholder composition and investor preferences

Empirically, a few patterns are commonly observed.

1. There's a lag between changes in profit and changes in dividends.
2. Firms tend not to change dividend levels frequently.
3. Dividends follow a smoother path than earnings.
4. Dividend policy tends to move with the firm's life cycle.

Growth-stage firms tend to have high retention ratios and low dividends, while mature firms have more room for stable dividends or buybacks.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. A dividend is compensation returned to shareholders from firm profit, and it also carries the character of a cost of equity.
2. Stock returns are made up of share price appreciation and dividend yield.
3. MM's dividend irrelevance theory holds that, under perfect capital markets and fixed investment policy, dividend policy is unrelated to firm value.
4. The low-payout preference theory holds that investors may prefer capital gains over dividends, largely because of taxes.
5. The high-payout preference theory holds that investors prefer a certain current dividend over uncertain future profit.
