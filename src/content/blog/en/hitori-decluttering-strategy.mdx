### Introduction: Subtracting Well Is Harder Than Adding

When a problem arises, our instinct is to add something new — a new feature, a new hire, a new process. But more often than we expect, the solution lies not in addition but in **subtraction**.

The Japanese logic puzzle **Hitori (一人, meaning "alone")** is a perfect demonstration of this principle. The board is filled with numbers; your task is to shade out the duplicates and leave only what belongs. As you erase the redundant noise, the essential structure of the grid becomes sharply clear — a process that mirrors the deep cleaning every system, organization, or life eventually needs. Today, through Hitori, we explore the skill of keeping only what is essential.

<h2>2. The 'Subtraction Management' Principles Hitori Illustrates</h2>

<h3>① Controlling Information Overload</h3>
The Hitori board is a problem precisely because it has too much information. When the same number appears repeatedly, it becomes impossible to tell which instance is actually needed. In business reporting, data without rigorous filtering impairs decisions rather than enabling them. Only after ruthlessly eliminating duplicates does the truth surface.

<h3>② Mutual Exclusion: The Danger of Eliminating Too Much at Once</h3>
The rule that shaded cells cannot be adjacent is a warning against **simultaneous wholesale elimination**. You can't just shade everything — doing so breaks the system's continuity. Efficiency measures (shading) must be spaced appropriately so that the overall process remains connected and operational, not severed and dysfunctional.

<h3>③ Maintaining the Connectivity of What Remains</h3>
All unshaded cells must form a single connected group. Even after extensive pruning, the **communication lines** between all remaining parts must stay intact. In an organization, no matter how aggressively you streamline, the channels through which teams communicate must never be cut.

---

## 3. Three Questions for Productive Subtraction

1. **Does it absolutely need to stay?** Ask whether this metric, this meeting, this process is generating genuine value.
2. **Is it duplicated?** If two things serve the same function, one of them should be shaded — decisively, like Hitori.
3. **Is everything still connected?** After removing the unnecessary, verify that the overall flow still runs without obstruction.

## Conclusion: Becoming Richer by Having Less

As you work through a Hitori puzzle, you notice: the grid looks better, feels clearer, and reveals its logic more beautifully after the right numbers have been removed. The same is true in life — real value often only becomes visible once you've created some space around it.

Through today's game, have you identified the "duplicate worries" in your own life worth shading out? Here's to a day that's clear, essential, and exactly what it needs to be.
