## Five Hundred Years of the Republic

In 509 BCE, Romans expelled their king and established a republic. For five centuries, two elected consuls shared power in one-year terms — a deliberate system to prevent any single person from accumulating too much power.

By the 1st century BCE, this system was breaking under its own contradictions.

---

## The Cracks in the Foundation

Rome's conquests created a paradox. Soldiers returned from years of war to find their farms absorbed by vast slave-worked estates (*latifundia*) owned by the wealthy. Dispossessed peasants flooded into cities, becoming a volatile urban poor.

Meanwhile, successful generals acquired personal fortunes and, more dangerously, armies loyal to them personally rather than to the state. The Republic had no mechanism to handle this concentration of military power.

---

## The First Triumvirate

In 60 BCE, three powerful men struck a secret alliance — the **First Triumvirate**:

**Pompey**: Rome's greatest military hero, conqueror of the East
**Crassus**: Rome's wealthiest man
**Julius Caesar**: A brilliant politician and military commander with enormous ambition

The alliance allowed each man to bypass Senate opposition and achieve his goals. But power is inherently divisive.

---

## Crossing the Rubicon

From 58–50 BCE, Caesar conquered all of Gaul (modern France and Belgium). Far from Rome, he demonstrated military genius and won his army's absolute loyalty.

When the Gallic Wars ended, the Senate ordered Caesar to disband his army and return alone. Compliance would leave him legally vulnerable to his enemies' prosecution.

On January 10, 49 BCE, Caesar led his army across the **Rubicon river** — the boundary between Cisalpine Gaul and Italy proper. Leading an army into Italy was treason.

<Callout type="tip">
**"The die is cast" (Alea iacta est)**: Caesar's words as he crossed the Rubicon, according to ancient sources. "Crossing the Rubicon" remains an expression for any irreversible decision — choosing a path from which there is no return.
</Callout>

---

## Civil War and Dictatorship

Caesar defeated Pompey (who fled to Egypt and was murdered there) and became Rome's dictator. He reformed the calendar (the **Julian calendar** we still use, indirectly), redistributed land to the poor, and extended citizenship to provincial peoples. Effective — but contemptuous of republican norms.

On March 15, 44 BCE — the **Ides of March** — 23 senators stabbed Caesar to death. Among them was **Brutus**, whom Caesar had trusted and promoted.

"Et tu, Brute?" — Shakespeare's invention, but emotionally true to the historical moment.

---

## The End of the Republic

Caesar's assassination restored not the Republic but chaos. New civil wars erupted. Caesar's adopted heir **Octavian** methodically eliminated all rivals. In 27 BCE, the Senate granted him the title "**Augustus**" (the revered one). The Roman Empire had begun.

Five centuries of republican government ended. Men who killed Caesar to save the Republic created the conditions that destroyed it. Rome's lesson echoes across history: institutions depend on citizens willing to uphold them, not on paper constitutions alone.
