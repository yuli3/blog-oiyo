## The question of modern philosophy: what can I know for certain

Modern philosophy moved away from the medieval worldview and tried to place human reason and experience at the center of knowledge. Alongside the Scientific Revolution, the way we explained nature changed, and philosophers asked anew: "Where does certain knowledge come from?"

That question still matters today. In an age flooded with news, statistics, reviews, videos, and AI-generated answers, we need to work out what we can actually believe.

## Descartes: a starting point that cannot be doubted

René Descartes tried to doubt everything that could possibly be doubted, in search of certain knowledge. The senses can deceive us, and dreams can be confused with reality. But it's hard to deny that the very self doing the doubting exists.

That's why Descartes is famous for the statement "I think, therefore I am." This isn't simple self-assurance — it's the minimal certainty he found at the end of doubt.

| Descartes's method | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Methodological doubt | Provisionally setting aside anything that can be doubted |
| The cogito | Taking the existence of the thinking subject as the starting point of certainty |
| Reason-centered | Placing weight on clear and distinct ideas |

## Rationalism: reason as an essential ground of knowledge

Rationalism finds the core grounds of knowledge in reason. It holds that some knowledge, like mathematics, is necessarily true without relying on experience. Descartes, Spinoza, and Leibniz are its representative figures.

Rationalism emphasizes order and structure. It holds that observation alone can't reveal the laws of the world — reasoning is required. But leaning too heavily on reason alone risks missing the complexity and specificity of real experience.

## Hume: is knowledge beyond experience possible?

David Hume is a central figure in empiricism. He held that our knowledge comes from impressions and ideas — that is, from experience. He raised a particularly sharp problem regarding the relationship between cause and effect.

We believe the sun will rise tomorrow because it has risen every day in the past. But Hume pointed out that repetition in the past provides no logical guarantee that it will repeat in the future. What we call "cause" may actually be a habitual expectation formed by repeated experience.

## Empiricism: what we've seen and lived is the basis of knowledge

Empiricism finds the starting point of knowledge in sensory experience. Locke, Berkeley, and Hume are its representative figures. This tradition tends to see the human mind not as filled with innate truths, but as filled in through experience.

Empiricism has the strength of emphasizing observation and verification. But it also faces criticism for struggling to fully explain matters that demand universality and necessity, such as mathematics, logic, and moral norms.

## Comparing rationalism and empiricism

| Comparison | Rationalism | Empiricism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grounds of knowledge | Reason, inference | Sense, experience |
| Strength | Explains universal laws and logical structure | Strong on observation and verification |
| Weakness | Can drift from real experience | Struggles to explain necessary knowledge |
| Representative figures | Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz | Locke, Berkeley, Hume |

Good judgment today generally needs both. Looking at data alone isn't enough, and neither is trusting theory alone. You have to gather experience and then organize it with logic.

## Applying it today: telling kinds of evidence apart

When you hear a claim, ask yourself: "Is this based on experience, or on inference?" The number of reviews matters for a restaurant recommendation, while logical necessity matters for a mathematical proof. Different kinds of claims require different kinds of evidence.

Learning just this one distinction makes judging information far clearer.

## Key takeaways

- Modern philosophy sought new grounds for certain knowledge in human reason and experience.
- Descartes used methodological doubt to place the existence of the thinking subject as the starting point of certainty.
- Rationalism emphasizes reason and inference, while empiricism emphasizes sensory experience and observation.
- Hume sharply raised the limits of experiential knowledge through the problems of causation and induction.
- Good judgment is possible only when empirical grounds and logical structure are checked together.
