## Logical Writing Isn't Cold Writing

Writing logically doesn't mean stripping out emotion. It means laying a clear path of thought so the reader can follow how you arrived at a conclusion. Even moving writing loses persuasive power without logic, and a report with weak evidence reads as mere opinion.

The basic unit of logical writing is the claim and its evidence. The claim is the conclusion you want to make; the evidence is the reason readers should believe it.

## Separating Claims from Facts

A common problem in beginner writing is mixing facts, interpretation, and claims together.

| Sentence | Category |
| --- | --- |
| Last month's app churn rate was 38% | Fact |
| The churn rate is high because the signup process is long | Interpretation |
| We should cut the signup process to three steps or fewer | Claim |

All three matter, but they play different roles. Facts provide the foundation, interpretation explains what the facts mean, and claims propose a judgment or action going forward.

## What Makes a Claim Good

A good claim is clear and testable. "Our service needs to get better" is too broad. It needs to show what should improve and by what standard.

Good example:

> To raise the first-payment conversion rate among new signups, we should cut the signup process from its current five steps to three.

This sentence shows a subject, a purpose, and an action. People can agree or disagree with it, and you can check afterward whether it happened.

## Mixing Types of Evidence

A single piece of evidence is often not enough. You need a balanced mix of numbers, examples, principles, and authoritative sources.

| Evidence type | Strength | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Data | Looks objective | Can mislead without context |
| Examples | Easy to grasp quickly | Careful with overgeneralizing |
| Principles | Builds logical structure | Can be too abstract |
| Comparisons | Helps judge alternatives | Comparison criteria must be fair |

More evidence isn't automatically better writing. Evidence that connects directly to the claim is good evidence.

## Thinking Through Counterarguments First

Logical writing doesn't just push one claim forward. Thinking through likely counterarguments in advance makes the piece sturdier. Acknowledge and answer objections like "costs could rise," "some users prefer the current approach," or "the data window is short."

Addressing counterarguments doesn't make writing look weak. If anything, readers sense that the writer considered the issue from more angles.

## A Logic-Check Template

After drafting, try filling in this template like a fill-in-the-blank exercise.

```text
I argue that we should ___.
This is because ___.
For example, there is ___.
That said, one could object that ___.
Even so, ___, so the claim holds.
```

This frame is simple, but it quickly reveals how well your claim and evidence connect.

## Key Takeaways

- Logical writing lays a clear path of thought so readers can follow you to your conclusion.
- Facts, interpretation, and claims play different roles and should be written separately.
- A good claim has a clear subject, purpose, and action, and is testable.
- Data, examples, principles, and comparisons should connect directly to the claim.
- Addressing likely counterarguments raises a piece's persuasiveness and credibility.
