Financial statement analysis uses accounting numbers to assess a company's past performance and financial position and to forecast its future. Investors ask about firm value, creditors about the ability to repay, and managers about their position relative to competitors. This course covers how to **read** the financial statements learned in Principles of Accounting and Intermediate Accounting. For how the statements are prepared, see [Principles of Accounting chapter 4](/en/education-accounting-ch2/).

## 3. Common-size statements: vertical analysis

Dividing each income statement item by revenue, and each balance sheet item by total assets, allows comparison across companies or years of different size.

<FormulaBox
  label="Common-size income statement"
  formula="\text{Item percentage} = \frac{\text{Item amount}}{\text{Revenue}} \times 100\%"
  description="In 20X2 FA's cost of sales ratio is 70%, selling and administrative ratio 18%, operating margin 12% and net margin 8%. In 20X1 they were 72%, 18%, 10% and 6.2%."
/>

A 2-percentage-point fall in the cost of sales ratio explains the improved operating margin; the selling and administrative ratio is unchanged at 18%. Whether it comes from lower raw material prices, price increases or a change in product mix is checked through business analysis and the notes.

On the balance sheet side, inventories make up 20% and receivables 15% of total assets at the end of 20X2. Liabilities are 50% of the total, the same as in 20X1.

## 4. Trend analysis: horizontal analysis

Trend analysis looks at the year-on-year change in the same item.

<LectureTable
  title="Growth in FA's key items (20X1 → 20X2)"
  headers={["Item", "Growth"]}
  rows={[
    ["Revenue", "+20.0%"],
    ["Operating profit", "+44.0%"],
    ["Net profit", "+54.8%"],
    ["Trade receivables", "+25.0%"],
    ["Inventories", "+25.0%"],
    ["Total assets", "+11.1%"],
  ]}
/>

Profit grew faster than revenue. This is operating leverage: with fixed costs steady, revenue growth greatly increases profit ([Cost Accounting chapter 13](/en/academy-cost-accounting-ch8/)). Meanwhile, receivables and inventories grew faster (25%) than revenue (20%). That may signal slower collections or piling-up inventory, to be checked with the turnover analysis of [chapter 4](/en/academy-accounting-basics-ch11/).

<Callout type="warning">
A ratio without a point of comparison says little. Look at it alongside the same company's past (time series), competitors in the same industry (cross-section) and the industry average. Different accounting policies (depreciation method, inventory cost flow, lease treatment) also give the same ratio a different meaning. Do not mix consolidated and separate statements, or K-IFRS and Korean GAAP for non-public entities, in comparisons ([Principles of Accounting chapter 12](/en/academy-accounting-basics-ch9/)).
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

In FA's 20X2 common-size income statement the operating margin is 12%. If in 20X3 revenue rises by 10%, the cost of sales ratio stays at 70% and selling and administrative expenses remain ₩21.6 billion, what are operating profit and the operating margin?

Revenue in 20X3 is ₩132 billion and gross profit $1{,}320 \times 30\% = 396$ (₩39.6 billion). Operating profit is $396 - 216 = 180$ (₩18 billion), an operating margin of about 13.6%. Because selling and administrative expenses are fixed, operating profit grows faster (25%) than revenue (10%).
