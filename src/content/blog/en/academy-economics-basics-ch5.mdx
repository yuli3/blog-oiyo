# Introduction to Economics — Market Structure Is the Relationship Between Price and Marginal Cost, Not the Number of Firms

Memorizing the number of firms and entry barriers from a table is classification. What an intermediate review needs to state is whether $P$ equals $\mathrm{MC}$, and whether long-run profit disappears through entry.

## 1. Long-run equilibrium under perfect competition is P = MC = min ATC

In the short run, as in Chapter 4, firms produce where $P=\mathrm{MC}$ and may earn profits. With free entry, profits attract more supply and drive price down to $P=min \mathrm{ATC}$. Zero economic profit is not zero accounting profit: the opportunity cost of capital is already in ATC.

With demand $P=120-Q$ and long-run industry supply at $P=40$ (constant cost), the competitive quantity is 80 and the price 40 won. In every transaction $P=\mathrm{MC}$, so there is no deadweight loss.

## 2. A monopolist chooses quantity where MR = MC and reads price off demand

With linear demand $P=120-Q$, total revenue is $120Q-Q²$ and $\mathrm{MR}=120-2Q$. With $\mathrm{MC}=40$, $120-2Q=40$ gives $Q=40$ and $P$ = 80 won. Compared with competition, quantity is halved and price doubled.

<FormulaBox
  label="Deadweight loss (simple)"
  formula="DWL \approx (1/2)(P_m-MC)(Q_c-Q_m)"
  description="The triangle between the monopoly price and the competitive quantity. An approximation for linear demand and MC."
/>

$0.5×(80-40)×(80-40)$ = 800 won is the deadweight loss in this example. Consumer surplus transferred to the firm is not deadweight loss. Interview questions become tractable once you separate who gets the surplus from what disappears.

Price discrimination raises profit when consumer types have different elasticities. Perfect (first-degree) discrimination can reduce deadweight loss but requires information and the prevention of resale. The proposition "discrimination is always bad for consumers" is close to true only in some markets with third-degree discrimination.

## 3. Oligopoly combines the incentive to collude and the incentive to cheat

If two firms split the monopoly profit of 800 won, each gets 400 won. If one cuts its price slightly and takes the market, it gets 700 won and the other 50 won. The Nash equilibrium is to cheat. If the game is repeated for long enough and deviations can be punished, collusion can be an equilibrium. One-off tenders are not like that.

<Callout type="warning">
Do not declare agricultural products or spot equity markets "examples of perfect competition." Differences in quality, location, information or entry regulation are enough to move away from P = MC. Conversely, a single firm does not automatically earn monopoly profits: potential entry (contestability) and substitutes restrain prices.
</Callout>

Regulating price at $\mathrm{MC}$ is efficient but causes losses for a natural monopoly. Regulating it at $\mathrm{ATC}$ removes the losses but leaves a deadweight loss. When asked about public enterprises or utility regulation, state this trade-off first.

## Check your understanding

Demand is $P=100-Q$ and $\mathrm{MC}=20$. What are the competitive and monopoly quantities? Competitive $Q=80$. For the monopolist, $\mathrm{MR}=100-2Q=20$ gives $Q=40$ and $P$ = 60 won. The DWL is $0.5×40×40$ = 800 won.
