## Stress is not the enemy

"Don't stress" is an impossible instruction. Stress is the response your body and mind switch on to adapt to change — without it you couldn't sense danger, meet deadlines, or feel the thrill of a new challenge.

The endocrinologist **Hans Selye**, often called the father of stress research, divided stress into two kinds:

- **Eustress** — good tension that lifts motivation and focus: the excitement of a new project, the pre-game jitters, the concentration a deadline brings.
- **Distress** — bad stress that exceeds your coping resources and leads to burnout: a load that feels uncontrollable, endless, and without recovery time.

The same presentation is eustress if you have time and resources to prepare, and distress if it overwhelms you. What determines the character of stress is **not the event itself but the ratio of event to resources**. ([Eustress vs distress →](https://wiki.oiyo.net/en/meaning-of-eustress-distress/))

## Not what happened — what might happen

Interestingly, stress often grows less from what has already happened than from **anticipation of what might happen**. That's why the night before an exam is worse than the exam itself. Knowing this gives you room to notice — and break — the mental loop that keeps replaying an unarrived future.

## The Yerkes-Dodson law — why zero stress isn't the goal

In 1908, psychologists Yerkes and Dodson found that **arousal and performance follow an inverted-U curve**.

- **Too little arousal** → boredom, apathy. Repeating only easy tasks actually wears you down.
- **Optimal arousal** → the peak of focus and flow.
- **Too much arousal** → anxiety, freezing, poor judgment.

So "zero stress" can't be the goal — a state with no stimulation leads to apathy. The goal is to **reduce distress and keep a rhythm of eustress**. ([Yerkes-Dodson law →](https://wiki.oiyo.net/en/meaning-of-yerkes-dodson-law/))

## Burnout — distress that has piled up

The WHO defines burnout as "a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed." It has three signs:

1. **Energy depletion** — fatigue that rest doesn't restore
2. **Psychological distance** — cynicism and detachment toward work
3. **Reduced efficacy** — the sense that "nothing I do matters"

If burnout is accumulated distress, the opposite extreme is **boreout** — apathy from a total lack of stimulation. A healthy state is the sweet spot between the two. ([Burnout →](https://wiki.oiyo.net/en/meaning-of-burnout-syndrome/))

## Put a number on your stress load

To move beyond a vague "I'm exhausted lately," measurement helps. The **life-events stress scale** developed by Holmes and Rahe in 1967 sums the changes you've experienced over the past year (moving, a new job, marriage — good events count too) to estimate your stress load.

👉 [Try the Holmes-Rahe stress test](https://oiyo.net/en/stress-events/test)

If your score is high, read it not as a fault but as **a signal to secure more recovery time**. Next: relationships, one of the biggest sources of stress.
