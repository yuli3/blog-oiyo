## Getting Started After Passing the Exam

Passing the exam is the beginning, not the end. Before you can serve clients for compensation, you must complete the **registration process** with the applicable licensing authority.

### Registration Process

1. Obtain your official certification document from the issuing authority
2. **Register with the professional licensing body** (mandatory in most jurisdictions)
3. Submit your business address and contact information
4. Receive your registration certificate → authorized to begin practice

## Authorized Service Areas

The following service areas are typically within the exclusive scope of a licensed administrative agent's practice.

### 1. Document Preparation for Government Agency Filings

Prepare and file various applications, petitions, requests, and correspondence on behalf of individuals and organizations.

**Common document types**:
- Business license and permit applications
- Land use and zoning applications
- Government benefit program applications
- Formal requests and administrative complaints
- Agency registration filings

### 2. Immigration and Visa Services (Primary Revenue Source)

Among all service areas, immigration and visa work typically offers the highest volume of client demand and the best revenue-to-effort ratio.

**Key immigration filing categories**:
- Nonimmigrant visa extension and status change applications
- Naturalization (citizenship) applications
- Permanent residence (green card) petition documentation
- Employment authorization document (EAD) applications
- Family-based immigration filings

*Important*: Non-attorney immigration service providers must understand and strictly operate within the boundaries set by federal law and state unauthorized practice of law rules. In the US, representing clients before the immigration courts requires DOJ accreditation or a law license.

### 3. Administrative Appeals

When an agency takes an adverse action against a client, an administrative agent may represent the client in seeking review or reconsideration through administrative channels.

**Common cases**:
- Appeal of a denied business license or permit
- Request for reconsideration of a benefit denial
- Response to a notice of violation or proposed penalty
- Appeal of a government contract decision

## Office Setup

### Solo Practice vs. Group Practice

| Category | Solo Practice | Group Practice |
|----------|--------------|---------------|
| Structure | One practitioner | Two or more practitioners |
| Startup cost | Lower | Shared expenses |
| Service breadth | Depends on individual capacity | Specializations can be divided |
| Independence | High | Moderate |

### Office Location Strategy

- **Near immigration offices or courts**: High demand for visa and status change services
- **Industrial and commercial districts**: Employer clients with ongoing immigration and permitting needs
- **Dense government office corridors**: General administrative filing demand

## Fee Structure

Administrative agent fees are generally set by the individual practitioner. Typical ranges (highly variable by market and complexity):

| Service | Fee Range |
|---------|-----------|
| Simple visa extension (standard case) | $150–$400 |
| Naturalization application assistance | $500–$1,500 |
| Administrative appeal filing | $500–$2,000 |
| Business license application | $300–$1,000 |
| Permitting and land use application | $500–$3,000 |

### Growth Pathway

Even in the early stages, building a **defined specialization** matters more than maximizing fees in the short term.

1. **Entry phase**: Start with high-volume, straightforward filings to build competence and reputation
2. **Growth phase**: Develop expertise with a specific client community, visa category, or agency
3. **Established phase**: Build a book of recurring clients, develop cross-referral relationships with attorneys and CPAs

## Language Skills as a Competitive Advantage

In immigration practice, fluency in **Spanish, Mandarin, Vietnamese, Tagalog, or other languages** is a direct business advantage — not just a communication tool.

The ability to serve clients in their native language, explain complex administrative processes clearly, and build trust across a cultural gap is rare and valuable. Practitioners who can do this for underserved language communities will find strong demand and less direct competition.
