## 1. Ask What a Leadership Theory Explains

Leadership concerns influence toward collective work; holding a managerial title does not demonstrate that influence. A useful review connects each theory to a question and to evidence that could challenge the answer.

| Perspective | Question to investigate |
|---|---|
| Traits | Which personal characteristics are associated with leadership emergence or effectiveness, and in which setting? |
| Behaviors | What does the leader do to organize tasks and support people? |
| Contingency | How does the relationship between leadership and outcomes depend on the task, followers and situation? |

Trait research is not a certificate that leaders are born rather than developed. Kirkpatrick and Locke explicitly distinguish the presence of traits from their inherited or acquired origin and from successful leadership. Being noticed as a leader and helping a team perform are different outcomes. Confidence can make a person visible without establishing sound judgment. Associations do not by themselves identify whether selection, learning or the environment explains the result. [Kirkpatrick & Locke, 1991](https://homepages.se.edu/cvonbergen/files/2013/01/Leadership_Do-Traits-Matter.pdf).

Behavioral approaches distinguish task structuring from consideration for people. These are not necessarily opposite ends of one scale: a supervisor can clarify responsibilities and listen carefully. A contingency analysis asks what the work requires rather than selecting one universally best style. For a hypothetical emergency handover, explicit task allocation may matter immediately; during a process redesign, obtaining information from experienced staff may expose risks the manager cannot see. These are reasoning examples, not measured rankings of leadership styles.

## 2. Rewards and a Shared Direction Are Not Alternatives

Transactional leadership concerns exchanges and standards, including contingent rewards and attention to deviations. Transformational leadership concerns influence that changes how followers understand the purpose and possibilities of their work. Its familiar four components are idealized influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation and individualized consideration.

A leader can explain a meaningful service goal while defining measurable quality standards and coaching an individual. Calling the first action transformational does not make the reward system unnecessary. Nor does a contingent reward mean the organization must preserve its existing strategy. Judge and Piccolo's meta-analysis discusses their coexistence and the difficulty of separating their highly correlated effects; it does not establish a universally superior style for every outcome. [Judge & Piccolo, 2004](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Ronald-Piccolo/publication/8210096_Transformational_and_Transactional_Leadership_A_Meta-Analytic_Test_of_Their_Relative_Validity/links/0deec523c4fc647ad3000000/Transformational-and-Transactional-Leadership-A-Meta-Analytic-Test-of-Their-Relative-Validity.pdf).

Evaluate the conduct and outcome separately. A persuasive vision may encourage effort but can also discourage questioning if disagreement is treated as disloyalty. Individual consideration requires evidence about actual support, not a leader's self-description. Servant leadership similarly emphasizes service and follower development; using the label does not establish that employees have meaningful discretion or protection from retaliation.

When comparing teams, distinguish employee ratings of a leader from independently measured results. The same respondent rating both leadership and team success can introduce shared measurement influences. High-performing teams may also give their leaders better ratings. A positive association therefore needs a design and a plausible alternative explanation before becoming a causal claim.

## 3. Read Culture at More Than One Level

Organizational culture concerns shared patterns of meaning and assumptions learned within a group. An organization can contain different professional, departmental and national subcultures; one company label can hide those differences.

Schein's three levels distinguish what can be observed from what needs interpretation. His warning that artifacts are easy to observe but difficult to interpret matters when diagnosing a workplace. The following support department is fictional, not a case reported in the source. [Schein, 1984](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/coming-to-a-new-awareness-of-organizational-culture/); [MIT Sloan's explanation of the three levels](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/ideas-made-to-matter/5-enduring-management-ideas-mit-sloans-edgar-schein).

| Level | Observation and interpretive boundary |
|---|---|
| Artifacts | A public incident dashboard and weekly review meetings are visible. Their existence does not tell us whether staff feel safe reporting mistakes. |
| Espoused beliefs and values | Managers say “raise problems early.” This records the stated principle, not necessarily what happens under pressure. |
| Basic underlying assumptions | Staff may take for granted that reporting a mistake harms their prospects. That is a hypothesis requiring repeated evidence, not something proved by office design. |

Investigate decisions, incentives and accounts of critical incidents. If the dashboard rewards teams for having no recorded incidents, silence may reflect the measurement rule rather than genuinely reliable service. Interviewing only managers could miss that conflict. Protect confidentiality and avoid treating one employee's account as the experience of everyone.

Culture strength describes the degree of agreement and reinforcement, not whether the shared expectations are desirable. Consistent error reporting and consistent concealment can both be reinforced. A claim that a strong culture improves performance must specify which norms, whose outcomes, the time horizon and the evidence.

## 4. Use the Competing Values Framework as a Comparison

The framework organizes cultural orientations along internal versus external focus and flexibility versus stability/control. The resulting quadrants make competing priorities visible. [Whetten & Cameron, Figure 0.2](https://webuser.bus.umich.edu/cameronk/PDFs/Management%20Skills/MANAGEMENT%20SKILLS%20BOOK%20%286TH%20ED%29%20-%20INTRODUCTION.pdf).

| Orientation | Position and characteristic emphasis |
|---|---|
| Clan | Internal focus with flexibility: participation, development and cohesion |
| Adhocracy | External focus with flexibility: experimentation and adaptation |
| Hierarchy | Internal focus with stability/control: coordination and dependable processes |
| Market | External focus with stability/control: results and competitive positioning |

These are analytical orientations, not four mutually exclusive kinds of company. A product team may need experimentation while a payment operation needs reliable controls. Calling one “innovative” and the other “bureaucratic” does not resolve the trade-off. Ask where discretion belongs, which errors are tolerable and how information crosses team boundaries. Culture-strategy fit is a proposition to investigate, not proof that a particular quadrant guarantees success or failure.

## 5. Design and Evaluate Organizational Development

Organizational development is planned work on organizational functioning through diagnosis, intervention and learning. Team building, process consultation and survey feedback are possible interventions, not interchangeable treatments. A workflow bottleneck may require clearer decision rights rather than another workshop.

Lewin's unfreezing, movement and stabilization offer a way to think about changing an established pattern. Burnes's reappraisal connects the three steps with diagnosis, participation and action research, including trial and evaluation during movement. Stabilization means making the new practice workable and sustainable, not forbidding later adaptation. A stage label is not evidence that every change follows a fixed sequence. [Burnes, 2004](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-6486.2004.00463.x).

In applying this framework, investigate resistance as a possible signal of workload, capability, fairness or safety concerns. Manufacturing discomfort is not a prerequisite for informed participation.

For a fictional department of 80 employees, a pilot combines a new escalation process with manager coaching. Keep process adoption, employee experience and operating outcomes separate:

- Before the pilot, 45 of 60 survey respondents report that they can raise problems: 75%. Afterward, 32 of 40 respondents report this: 80%.
- The workforce stays at 80, but response coverage falls from 75% to 50%. The five-percentage-point increase among respondents is not a verified improvement for the entire workforce.
- Recorded errors fall from 500 in 10,000 cases to 360 in 12,000 cases: 5% to 3%, a two-percentage-point decrease or 40% relative reduction in the recorded rate.

The arithmetic is descriptive. Changes in case difficulty, staffing, reporting rules or who answers the survey may explain part of it. Fewer recorded errors could even reflect less reporting. Review case mix and audit a comparable sample using stable definitions. Where feasible and appropriate, compare another team or a longer pre-intervention trend; record concurrent changes and protect employee privacy. Do not turn a favorable before-and-after result into a certificate that the culture caused it.

## 6. Succession Readiness Is Specific to a Role

Succession planning connects leadership development to the ability to fill a business-critical role. It also covers specialist roles, not only executive posts. CIPD emphasizes identifying critical roles and providing practical experience relevant to future work. A list of admired employees is therefore not enough. [CIPD, Succession planning](https://www.cipd.org/uk/knowledge/factsheets/succession-planning-factsheet/).

For each role, define the tasks, decision authority and evidence needed for immediate performance. Current performance, potential to develop and readiness for that particular role answer different questions. A strong technical specialist need not want or be ready for a management role. “Ready in one year” is a forecast requiring a development plan and later review, not a guaranteed date. Course attendance records participation; it does not by itself establish readiness.

The following hypothetical allocation uses four critical roles and three candidates. It is not an assessment of actual employees.

| Role | Candidate and immediate readiness |
|---|---|
| A | P can perform the role now |
| B | P can perform the role now, but is the same person assigned as A's candidate |
| C | Q can perform the role now |
| D | R needs further development and is not an immediate replacement |

Three of four roles have an immediate candidate: 75% coverage. If A, B and C become vacant together and one person can fill only one role, P and Q can fill at most two vacancies. In this four-role scenario, simultaneous replacement capacity is 2/4 = 50%, not 75%. Counting P twice conceals the constraint; counting R as immediately ready changes the definition. Check candidate willingness and availability, overlapping assignments and the future demands of the roles before turning a coverage rate into a continuity claim.

Development can involve supervised assignments, feedback and relevant work experience. Internal development and external recruitment are alternatives to compare against the capability gap and available time, not guarantees of cultural continuity or quick success. For real operations, define who can access candidate information and how it will be maintained; the hypothetical exercise does not require collecting personal records.

A separate cost scenario assumes one role remains vacant for five months and creates an additional cost of USD 20,000 per month: 5 × USD 20,000 = USD 100,000. This is a conditional calculation, not an expected loss incorporating vacancy probability or an observed saving from training. Establish what is additional to the baseline and avoid counting the same delay both as lost revenue and lost profit. A ready candidate does not guarantee zero transition time.

### Succession Problem → Worked Answer

**Problem.** A manager reports 75% coverage, three candidates and 95% training completion, then claims that succession planning has eliminated a USD 100,000 vacancy risk. What follows from the scenario?

**Worked answer.** The coverage figure counts roles with an immediate candidate, not independent replacements. P and Q provide at most two simultaneous replacements; R is still developing. Training completion establishes neither readiness nor shorter vacancies. USD 100,000 is the assumed additional cost conditional on a five-month vacancy, not a probability-weighted expected loss. Observe role-specific readiness, actual transitions and incremental costs before claiming avoided loss, while preserving access boundaries for candidate information.

### Culture Problem → Worked Answer

**Problem.** The pilot manager says, “The culture is now transformational: staff confidence rose to 80%, and our new culture cut errors by 40%.” What can the evidence support, and what should be investigated next?

**Worked answer.** It supports an 80% positive response rate among the 40 people who answered, not among all 80 employees. It also supports a 40% relative reduction in the recorded error rate, from 5% to 3%; the count itself fell by 28%, from 500 to 360. None of those calculations classifies the culture as transformational or establishes causation. Check who responded, whether error definitions and reporting incentives changed, and whether the periods contain comparable cases. Observe how managers respond to an actual reported mistake. Those checks connect leadership behavior, shared assumptions and outcomes without treating them as the same variable.
