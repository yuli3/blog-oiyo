# Introduction to Economics — What a Central Bank Changes Is the Monetary Base, Not an Automatic Multiple of All Deposits

The functions of money (medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value) are definitions. An intermediate review asks **when credit creation falls short of the multiplier**, and what policy moves once it works through interest rates.

## 1. The simple multiplier is an upper bound with no cash or excess reserves

With a reserve ratio of 10% and all loans redeposited, the multiplier is 10: a monetary base of 1 trillion won becomes 10 trillion won of deposits. In reality, households hold cash, banks build up excess reserves and loan demand may be absent.

<FormulaBox
  label="Money multiplier (with leakages)"
  formula="M / H = (1+c) / (c+r+e)"
  description="c is the currency-deposit ratio, r the required reserve ratio and e the excess reserve ratio. As c or e rises, the multiplier falls towards 1."
/>

With $c=0.2$, $r=0.1$ and $e=0.1$, the multiplier is $1.2/0.4=3$, not the simple 10. This denominator is why, when banks hoard excess reserves in a crisis, expanding the monetary base does not raise M1.

A zero-coupon bond with a face value of 10,000 won maturing in one year is priced at $10000/1.02≈9,804$ won when the market rate is 2%, and 9,615 won when it is 4%. "Rates are rising" and "bondholders are losing money" are two sides of the same event.

## 2. The operating target is now closer to interest rates than quantities

Open market operations, reserve requirements and lending and deposit facilities are instruments. Many central banks target a short-term policy rate and adjust liquidity to it. Under an interest-rate target, the textbook first step — fixing the monetary base exogenously and reading M through the multiplier — no longer holds: the base becomes endogenous.

The equation of exchange $\mathrm{MV}=\mathrm{PY}$ is close to an identity. The quantity theory of money adds the **further assumptions** that $V$ is stable and $Y$ is at full employment, so that growth in $M$ raises $P$ proportionally. In the short run $Y$ and $V$ move too. "Printing money only raises prices" is a long-run, full-employment statement.

## 3. In a liquidity trap, bonds and money are near-perfect substitutes

When interest rates are at their floor, bond prices have no room to rise and people want to hold money. Changing the monetary base through open market operations does not move rates or spending. That is when fiscal policy or direct action on expectations enters the discussion. Memorizing the trap only as "at a zero rate, policy is powerless" misses the exchange-rate and credit channels.

<Callout type="warning">
Do not assume that inflation is always the result of too much money. Supply shocks (energy, exchange-rate pass-through) raise $P$ while reducing $Y$. Squeezing demand lowers both prices and output, and suppressing supply bottlenecks with monetary policy alone magnifies output losses.
</Callout>

The Bank of Korea's inflation target and financial stability may not both be met perfectly with a single instrument. That conflict belongs to later macroeconomics and to financial economics.

## Check your understanding

Lowering only the reserve ratio from 10% to 5% raises the simple multiplier from 10 to 20. If at the same time banks hold 5% in excess reserves, the multiplier falls back to $1/(0.05+0.05)=10$. Do not read institutional easing and actual credit expansion as the same number.
