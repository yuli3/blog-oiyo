## Why does composition matter?

The same location, camera, and lighting can still produce dramatically different results depending on **composition**. Composition is how you arrange your subject within the frame, and it's the key element that lets a photo tell a story and guide the viewer's eye.

Three goals of good composition:
1. **Lead the eye to the subject**
2. **Create balance and tension within the frame**
3. **Communicate the story effectively**

---

## The rule of thirds

This is the most basic — and most powerful — composition rule there is.

### The principle

Divide the frame into a 3×3 grid using **two horizontal and two vertical lines**. The lines and their intersection points are the key.

The two horizontal lines are where a horizon sits best, and the four dots mark the intersections.

### How to apply it

- Place the **key part of your subject** on one of the four intersection points
- Place a **horizon line** along the upper or lower third line
- Place **vertical subjects** (trees, towers, people) along a left or right third line

> **Key point**: putting the subject dead center tends to look static and flat. Placing it on an intersection point creates a sense of energy.

## Leading lines

This technique uses lines within the frame to **naturally guide the viewer's eye toward the subject**.

### Types of leading lines

| Line type | Feeling | Example |
|---------|------|------|
| **Horizontal** | Stability, calm, expansiveness | A horizon, the sea |
| **Vertical** | Strength, power, growth | Trees, buildings |
| **Diagonal** | Energy, tension, movement | Roads, stairs, slopes |
| **Curved** | Softness, elegance, flow | A river, a winding path, an S-curve |
| **Converging** | Perspective, depth | Railway tracks, hallways, tree-lined roads |

**Using converging lines**: when two lines meet at a single point (a vanishing point) in the distance, it creates a powerful sense of perspective and depth.

## Symmetry and pattern

### Symmetrical composition

Place your subject **symmetrically in the center** of the frame. Centering a subject is usually something to avoid, but a strong sense of symmetry can become the composition itself.

- Vertical symmetry: reflections (lakes, puddles), building facades
- Horizontal symmetry: sky and its reflection
- Radial symmetry: tunnels, flowers, snowflakes

### Pattern and breaking the pattern

A regular pattern (tiles, grids, repeating shapes) can make a powerful photo on its own. Adding a **pattern breaker** — an element that disrupts the pattern — creates an even more striking visual effect.

## Frame within frame

This technique uses a natural frame (a door, window, tree branch, or tunnel entrance) to **wrap the subject one more time**.

**Effects**:
- Naturally focuses attention on the subject
- Conveys depth and perspective
- Reinforces the sense of story (a feeling of looking in from outside)

**Good framing elements**: doorframes, windows, tree branches, tunnels, gaps between rocks, hands or arms, a person's body

---

## Foreground, midground, background

Structuring a photo into three layers creates a sense of **depth and dimension**.

| Camera → | Foreground | Midground | Background |
|---|---|---|---|
| Example | flowers | person | mountains |

- **Foreground**: the closest element to the camera — use interesting texture or pattern
- **Midground**: your main subject
- **Background**: provides context and setting

> **Tip**: adding an interesting element in the foreground can turn an ordinary photo into one with real dimension.

---

## Negative space

This is the deliberate technique of leaving **empty space** around your subject.

**Effects of negative space**:
- Makes the subject stand out more
- Conveys stillness, solitude, or vastness
- Achieves a minimalist aesthetic

**Gaze direction and negative space**: leaving more space in the direction a person or animal is looking, or moving, creates a natural sense of space.

- **✓ Good**: [Subject →] [    negative space    ]
- **✗ Bad**: [    negative space    ] [← Subject]

---

## Angle and height

The same subject can look completely different depending on **shooting height and angle**.

| Angle | Feeling | Good for |
|------|------|--------------|
| **Eye level** | Natural, on equal footing | Portraits, everyday snapshots |
| **Low angle** | Grandeur, strength, dominance | Buildings, heroic portraits, nature |
| **High angle** | Smallness, cuteness, an overseeing view | Children, food, terrain |
| **Top-down (bird's-eye)** | Emphasizes pattern, geometric | Food, flat-lay arrangements, crowds |
| **Dutch angle (tilted)** | Unease, tension, dynamism | Action, horror |

## Practice quiz — 5 questions

**Q1.** In the rule of thirds, where should you place the horizon in a landscape photo meant to emphasize the sky?
- ① Center of the frame
- ② Lower third line
- ③ Very bottom of the frame
- ④ Upper third line

**Answer**: ② *(placing the horizon on the lower third emphasizes the sky, which takes up 2/3 of the frame)*

---

**Q2.** Which type of leading line best conveys energy and tension?
- ① Horizontal  ② Vertical  ③ Diagonal  ④ Curved

**Answer**: ③

---

**Q3.** Which of the following is NOT a primary effect of frame within frame?
- ① Focusing attention on the subject
- ② Conveying depth
- ③ Automatically correcting exposure
- ④ Reinforcing a sense of story

**Answer**: ③

---

**Q4.** When a person is looking to the right, where should the natural negative space go?
- ① To the left of the person
- ② To the right of the person
- ③ No negative space — fill the frame
- ④ Above the person

**Answer**: ② *(leave space in the direction of the gaze)*

---

**Q5.** What is the main effect of shooting from a low angle?
- ① The subject looks small and cute
- ② The subject looks grand and powerful
- ③ Perspective disappears
- ④ The background blurs

**Answer**: ②
