## Wedding etiquette looks completely different from country to country

The impulse to celebrate is universal, but the amount you give, how you give it, what you wear, and what to avoid can vary enormously depending on where the wedding is. If you've been invited to a wedding abroad, it's worth doing your homework beforehand. Here's what guests should know, country by country.

---

## Korea

```
Gift money: cash in a white envelope. Custom favors amounts
       that don't divide evenly by 10 (50,000 / 70,000 /
       100,000 / 150,000 won), scaled to how close you are
       and whether a meal is included.
Envelope: marked "祝結婚" ("Congratulations on the marriage")
       on the front, with the giver's name and affiliation
       on the back.
Dress: neat formal or semi-formal wear. Avoid white, which
       could be mistaken for competing with the bride.
Order of events: registration/gift table → the ceremony →
       traditional post-ceremony bows (pyebaek) and photos
       → a meal (often buffet-style).
```

- 50,000 won is the common baseline these days; close friends and family often give 100,000 won or more.
- Guests avoid outdressing the bride or wearing anything too revealing.

---

## Japan

```
Goshūgi (ご祝儀): given in crisp, brand-new bills — using new
       bills signals that you prepared for the celebration in
       advance.
Amount: 30,000 yen is standard for a friend. Odd numbers are
       preferred (an odd number "can't be split evenly," which
       symbolizes a bond that can't be broken). Even numbers
       and the number 9 are avoided (4 sounds like "death,"
       9 sounds like "suffering").
Envelope: a decorative envelope with an ornate knot (shūgi-
       bukuro), carried wrapped in a small cloth (fukusa).
Dress: white is strictly off-limits (it's the bride's color).
       Men typically wear a black formal suit with a bright
       tie or accessory.
```

- In Japan, **using crisp new bills is the polite move** — the exact opposite of funeral etiquette.
- Some weddings use a flat "membership fee" system (kaihi-sei), where guests simply pay a set, predetermined amount.

---

## China

```
Hóngbāo (红包): gift money given in a red envelope — red
       symbolizes celebration and good fortune.
Amount: numbers like 8 (which sounds like "prosperity") and
       6 (which suggests things going smoothly) are considered
       lucky. The number 4 (which sounds like "death") is
       strictly avoided.
Dress: guests generally avoid red, since it's reserved for
       the bride and her family. White and black are also
       avoided, since they evoke mourning.
```

- Customary amounts vary widely by region.
- The celebration typically centers on a large banquet (xǐjiǔ).

---

## The US and the broader West

```
Gifts: cash is uncommon — buying from the couple's gift
       registry (their curated wish list) is the standard.
RSVP: replying to confirm attendance is essential — it
       directly determines the headcount and meal planning.
Dress: follow the dress code stated on the invitation
       (black tie, cocktail, semi-formal, etc.).
Taboo: guests should never wear white — it's reserved
       exclusively for the bride.
```

- **A registry gift, not cash, is the default** — cash gifts are mainly seen among certain close-knit communities or immediate family.
- Failing to RSVP is considered a real breach of etiquette.

---

## Europe

Customs vary a lot by country, but registry gifts are common across the UK, France, and much of Western Europe, and some couples instead ask guests to contribute to a honeymoon fund. Dress codes are usually followed strictly, and avoiding white is a near-universal rule.

---

## India

```
Gift money: given in cash, with the amount ending in 1
       (101, 501, or 1,001 rupees) — an amount that doesn't
       end in a round zero is considered auspicious, symbolizing
       a new beginning and continuity.
Dress: bright, colorful outfits are welcomed and expected.
       White (associated with mourning) and black are avoided.
Scale: celebrations often span several days, with multiple
       events (such as the mehndi and sangeet).
```

- Vibrant, traditional colors — reds and golds especially — are genuinely welcomed rather than seen as showing up the couple.

## Quick comparison

| Country/region | Gift | Bills | Key taboo |
|------|------|------|-----------|
| Korea | White envelope | No preference | Wearing white |
| Japan | Goshūgi, ~30,000 yen | **Crisp new bills** | Even numbers, 9, white |
| China | Hóngbāo (red envelope) | No preference | The number 4, white/black |
| US/West | Registry gift | (not cash) | Wearing white, skipping RSVP |
| India | Cash ending in 1 | No preference | White/black clothing |

## Universal ground rules

1. **Never outshine the bride** — avoid white, overly revealing outfits, or excessive flash (India being a notable exception).
2. RSVP promptly and accurately.
3. Follow the dress code and start time stated on the invitation.
4. If you're taking photos, don't let it disrupt the ceremony itself.

At its core, wedding etiquette is about **making sure the couple stays the center of attention**. The specific customs change from place to place, but that underlying principle holds everywhere.
