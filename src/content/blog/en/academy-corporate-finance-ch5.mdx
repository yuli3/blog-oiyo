## Capital Structure and the Cost of Capital

**Capital structure** is the combination of long-term debt and equity a firm uses. A firm can raise the funds it needs for investment through equity, or through debt such as bonds and loans.

**Cost of capital** is the required return a firm must pay to those who supply its capital. From an investor's perspective it's a required return; from the firm's perspective it's the cost of using that capital.

Cost of capital serves three functions.

1. It's the minimum return that justifies an investment — the benchmark for evaluating projects.
2. It's the criterion for choosing how to fund an investment.
3. It shapes the firm's major financial decisions around investment, financing, and dividends.

---

## The Cost of Capital by Source

Capital broadly splits into equity and debt.

| Category | Meaning | Typical estimation approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost of equity | The return shareholders require | CAPM, the Gordon growth model |
| Cost of debt | The interest rate creditors and lenders require | After-tax cost of borrowing |
| Weighted average cost of capital | The average cost across all financing sources | WACC |

The cost of equity can be estimated with CAPM.

```
re = rf + [E(rM) - rf] × β
```

If dividends are assumed to grow at a stable rate, the Gordon growth model also applies.

```
re = (D1 / P0) + g

D1: next period's dividend
P0: the current share price
g: the dividend growth rate
```

The cost of debt is usually measured after tax, because interest expense reduces the firm's corporate tax bill.

```
After-tax cost of debt = rd × (1 - Tc)
```

---

## WACC

The **weighted average cost of capital (WACC)** calculates the firm's overall cost of capital using the market-value weights of debt and equity.

```
WACC = [E / (D + E)] × re + [D / (D + E)] × rd × (1 - Tc)

E: the market value of equity
D: the market value of debt
re: the cost of equity
rd: the cost of debt
Tc: the corporate tax rate
```

If a project's risk resembles the firm's existing average risk, WACC can be used as the discount rate in capital budgeting. If the project's risk differs, a separate discount rate suited to that project is needed instead.

---

## The Central Questions of Capital Structure Policy

Capital structure policy tries to answer two questions.

1. Does capital structure affect firm value?
2. If it does, which mix of debt and equity maximizes firm value?

Traditional theory held that WACC changes with debt use and that an optimal capital structure exists. Modigliani and Miller (MM), by contrast, worked out the relationship between capital structure and firm value with strict rigor under the assumption of perfect capital markets.

---

## MM Proposition I Under Perfect Markets

Modigliani and Miller showed that, under the assumption of perfect capital markets, **firm value is independent of capital structure**.

```
MM Proposition I:
VL = VU

VL: the value of a levered firm (using debt)
VU: the value of an unlevered firm (using no debt)
```

The core idea here is the **principle of value conservation**. If the cash flow generated by a firm's operating assets is the same, how that cash flow is split between shareholders and creditors doesn't change the firm's total value.

---

## MM Proposition II

MM Proposition II explains how using debt changes the cost of equity.

```
re = ra + (ra - rd) × (D / E)

re: the cost of equity
ra: the return on the firm's overall assets
rd: the cost of debt
D / E: the debt-to-equity ratio
```

Debt is generally cheaper than equity. But using more debt increases shareholders' risk, which raises the cost of equity. Under perfect markets, this rising cost of equity exactly offsets the cheaper cost of debt, so WACC and firm value remain unchanged.

---

## Corporate Taxes and Modified MM Theory

Real markets have corporate taxes. Interest on debt is deductible, which reduces taxable income. This is what creates debt's **interest tax shield**.

```
VL = VU + PV(interest tax shield)
   = VU + Tc × D
```

Considering only corporate taxes, firm value increases as debt use increases. At the extreme, this implies 100% debt financing is optimal.

The modified MM Proposition II shows that even with corporate taxes, using debt still raises the cost of equity — but the tax effect moderates how much it rises compared with the perfect-market case.

```
re = ra + (ra - rd) × (D / E) × (1 - Tc)
```

The higher a firm's corporate tax rate, the larger the tax benefit from using debt.

---

## Bankruptcy Costs and the Optimal Capital Structure

Looking only at corporate taxes suggests using more debt is always favorable. But in reality, bankruptcy risk rises as debt increases. Higher bankruptcy risk means expected bankruptcy costs.

```
VL = VU + PV(interest tax shield) - PV(bankruptcy costs)
```

So debt raises firm value on one hand, and lowers it on the other.

| Effect of using debt | Direction of firm value |
| --- | --- |
| Interest tax shield | Increases |
| Bankruptcy probability and expected bankruptcy costs | Decreases |

An optimal capital structure can exist at the point where these two forces balance.

---

## Agency Costs and Capital Structure

Debt and equity create different sets of interests. So capital structure also affects agency problems.

| Category | Conflict of interest | Typical incentive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Agency cost of equity | Outside shareholders vs. management | Perks, complacency |
| Agency cost of debt | Shareholders vs. creditors | Risk-seeking, underinvestment, asset stripping |

Increasing debt reduces the free cash flow management can spend at its discretion, which can lower the agency cost of equity. But excessive debt increases shareholders' incentive to shift losses onto creditors, raising the agency cost of debt.

Accounting for agency costs, firm value can be written as:

```
Firm value
= value of the unlevered firm
 + interest tax shield
 - bankruptcy costs
 - total agency costs
```

The optimal capital structure is the debt ratio that maximizes firm value once tax benefits, bankruptcy costs, and agency costs are all accounted for.

---

## Mechanisms That Mitigate Agency Costs

Agency costs are an extension of the agency problem from Chapter 1. Firms can reduce these conflicts of interest through:

1. Competitive pressure in the market for managerial talent
2. Performance-linked compensation such as stock options
3. Proxy fights that create pressure to replace incompetent management
4. Shareholders exercising their right to elect board directors
5. Internal monitoring systems within the firm

Capital structure isn't simply a financing ratio — it's a decision that intertwines taxes, bankruptcy risk, managerial incentives, and creditor protection.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. Capital structure is the combination of long-term debt and equity; cost of capital is the return capital providers require.
2. WACC is the cost of equity and after-tax cost of debt, weighted by market value.
3. Under perfect markets, MM Proposition I holds that firm value is independent of capital structure.
4. With corporate taxes, the interest tax shield means using debt can increase firm value.
5. In practice, the optimal capital structure sits at the point where tax benefits, bankruptcy costs, and agency costs balance.
