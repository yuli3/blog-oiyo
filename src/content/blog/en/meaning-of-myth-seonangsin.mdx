Seonangsin (서낭신) is a guardian deity in Korean folk belief that protects the village. They are enshrined at village entrances, mountain passes, or boundary points in the form of a *Seonangdang* (shrine), *Seonangnamu* (sacred tree), or *dolmudeogi* (cairn/stone pile). Their names and characteristics vary by region—sometimes referred to as *Seonghwangsin* (城隍神), *Dangsansin* (堂山神), or *Seonangsin*—and these roles often overlap. The core essence lies in the perception that there is a sacred threshold between the village where people live and the outside world, and that a presence is needed to guard that threshold.

## Core Myth — Representative Anecdotes

Rather than a single, fixed mythological protagonist, Seonangsin is a guardian deity with a different face in every village. In some villages, an ancient tree serves as the seat of the Seonangsin, while in others, a cairn or a small shrine performs that role. People would place stones on the *Seonangdang* and pray for safety when leaving on a journey or returning home.

In village rituals, people prayed to Seonangsin for the well-being of the community, a bountiful harvest, the prevention of disease, and safety on the road. Depending on the tradition, Seonangsin may be perceived as a male general, a female deity, a divine couple, or an ancestral figure. Therefore, it is inaccurate to define Seonangsin by a single personality; they are a deified manifestation of each village's history, geography, and collective memory.

The fact that the site of the Seonangsin is usually located at a boundary is significant. Mountain passes, crossroads, and village entrances are places where strange forces enter and people depart. Seonangsin manages this anxiety. They are not a deity who completely blocks the inside from the outside, but a deity of the threshold who watches over the passage to ensure it is made safely.

## Reading Through Archetypes — A Jungian Perspective

From a Jungian perspective, Seonangsin is the archetype of the **Gatekeeper** and the **Guardian of the Community**. By guarding the boundary, they connect the community to the outside world, which an individual might find difficult to face alone. In a modern context, this resembles the creation of safe distinctions between inside and outside, such as school gates, apartment entrances, corporate security procedures, or old neighborhood markers.

The Seonangsin archetype demonstrates that boundaries do not necessarily mean exclusion. A good boundary enables passage. Thus, Seonangsin is a symbol that allows a community to engage with the outside world without losing its own center.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q. Are Seonangsin and Seonghwangsin the same entity?**

A. Depending on the region, the terms may overlap or be distinguished. While their primary function as village guardians is similar, one must consider the differences in their respective traditions.

**Q. What is the custom of placing stones on a Seonangdang?**

A. It is a folk custom of praying for safety on the road and the fulfillment of wishes. The act of placing a stone can be seen as a way of notifying the deity of the boundary that one is passing through.
