# International Trade — The Gains from Trade Come from Relative Costs, Not Absolute Productivity

This chapter does not bundle trade and exchange rates together. Exchange rates and the balance of payments belong to [International Finance](/en/education-economics-ch36/). Here we fix only why two countries specialize. Heckscher–Ohlin and economies of scale are in [chapter 2](/en/education-economics-ch34/).

## 1. Comparative advantage exists even without an absolute advantage

Output per hour of labour:

Korea has an absolute advantage in chips and is level with the foreign country in wheat. The foreign country has an absolute advantage in neither good. The opportunity cost of a chip is 0.5 wheat in Korea and 1 wheat abroad. Korea has a comparative advantage in chips, the foreign country in wheat.

<FormulaBox
  label="Joint output after specialization"
  formula="\text{Chips}: 3 \to 4, \quad \text{Wheat}: 2 \to 2"
  description="World output when each country spends 2 hours only on its comparative-advantage good. Korea's 2 hours on chips give 4 chips; the foreign country's 2 hours on wheat give 2 wheat. Chips rise by 1 with no loss of wheat."
/>

Autarky: if each country splits its hours one each, Korea makes 2 chips + 1 wheat and the foreign country 1 chip + 1 wheat, a total of 3 chips and 2 wheat. With complete specialization, Korea makes 4 chips and the foreign country 2 wheat: 4 chips and 2 wheat in total. Chips rose by one unit. If the relative price lies between the two opportunity costs, consumption rises in both countries.

## 2. National gains and individual gains are different statements

Specialization moves labour out of import-competing sectors. Short-run unemployment and regional shocks are not automatically included in the calculation of total gains. The welfare analysis of tariffs in chapter 3 makes that distribution visible.

<Callout type="warning">
"Exports are good, imports are bad" is the opposite of comparative advantage. Importing means leaving goods with a high opportunity cost to someone else. The proposition that depreciation can favour exporters is not proven in this chapter; it is a price channel of international finance.
</Callout>

Transport costs and tariffs narrow the opportunity-cost gap by that amount and shrink trade volumes. If the gap is smaller than transport costs, no trade takes place. Complete specialization is the result of linear labour technology and zero trade costs.

## Check your understanding

If Korea's chip productivity rises to 4 per hour while wheat stays at 1, the opportunity cost of a chip falls to 0.25 wheat and Korea's comparative advantage in chips grows. But if Korea becomes more productive in both goods, making 4 chips or 8 wheat per hour, the opportunity cost of a chip becomes 2 wheat, higher than abroad (1), and comparative advantage flips to wheat. Even when all absolute productivities rise, the direction of comparative advantage is set by the relative gap.
