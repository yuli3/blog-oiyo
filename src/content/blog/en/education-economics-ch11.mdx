# Macroeconomics — GDP Is Final Output Valued at Market Prices, Not Welfare

If [Introduction to Economics, Chapter 7](/en/academy-economics-basics-ch7/) fixed the definition of GDP, this chapter uses the national accounting identities as the starting point for macroeconomic models. The consumption function (Chapter 5), the investment function (Chapter 6) and IS-LM (Chapter 4) all add behavioural assumptions to these identities.

## 1. Three approaches measure the same circular flow in different places

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** is the market value of final goods and services produced within a country in a given period. The same value can be measured in three places.

Suppose a farmer sells wheat to a mill for 300 won, the mill sells flour to a bakery for 600 won, and the bakery sells bread to consumers for 1,000 won.

<LectureTable
  title="One economy's GDP measured three ways"
  headers={["Approach", "Calculation", "Value"]}
  rows={[
    ["Production (sum of value added)", "300 + (600−300) + (1,000−600)", "1,000"],
    ["Expenditure (final purchases)", "Consumers' purchases of bread", "1,000"],
    ["Income (sum of incomes)", "Wages, profits, interest and rent at each stage", "1,000"],
  ]}
/>

The value added at each stage becomes the income of the people who work there and of the owners of capital, and the final sale price is the sum of all the value added. Adding the 600 won of flour separately would count value already contained in the price of bread twice. That is why intermediate goods are excluded.

## 2. Saving finances investment and net exports

Rearranging the expenditure identity gives the relationship between saving and investment. With private saving $S_p=Y-T-C$ (disposable income minus consumption) and government saving $S_g=T-G$:

<FormulaBox
  label="Saving–investment identity"
  formula="S_p + S_g = I + (X - M)"
  description="National saving equals domestic investment plus net lending abroad (the current account). If the government deficit rises, either private saving rises, investment falls or the current account worsens."
/>

This is an identity and says nothing about causation. Whether a fiscal deficit reduces investment (crowding out), raises private saving (Ricardian equivalence) or worsens the current account (twin deficits) depends on behavioural assumptions, which are covered in [Public Finance, Chapter 6](/en/academy-public-finance-ch6/) and Chapter 4 of this course.

## 3. Nominal and real GDP, and the deflator

Consider an economy with two goods.

<LectureTable
  title="Nominal and real GDP"
  headers={["", "Apples", "Pears", "Nominal GDP", "Real GDP (year-1 prices)"]}
  rows={[
    ["Year 1", "10 × 1,000 won", "5 × 2,000 won", "20,000 won", "20,000 won"],
    ["Year 2", "12 × 1,200 won", "5 × 2,200 won", "25,400 won", "22,000 won"],
  ]}
/>

Nominal GDP rose by 27%, but real GDP, which measures only the change in quantities, rose by 10%. The rest is price increases.

<FormulaBox
  label="GDP deflator"
  formula="\text{Deflator} = \frac{\text{Nominal GDP}}{\text{Real GDP}}\times 100 = \frac{25{,}400}{22{,}000}\times 100 \approx 115.5"
  description="Nominal growth ≈ real growth + deflator inflation. If nominal growth is 10% and the deflator rises 5%, real growth is 1.10/1.05 − 1 ≈ 4.8%."
/>

Keeping base-year prices fixed for a long time overweights goods whose prices fall quickly (electronics). That is why many countries chain growth rates using prices from adjacent years (**chain weighting**). The difference between the deflator and the consumer price index (CPI) is discussed in Chapter 2.

## 4. GDP and GNI

GDP is based on **where** output is produced; gross national income (GNI) on **who** earns the income.

$$GNI = GDP + \text{net factor income from abroad}$$

A country with large returns on foreign investment has GNI above GDP; a country with much inward direct investment has the reverse. And when the terms of trade deteriorate (export prices rise less than import prices), the same real GDP buys less, so real GNI grows more slowly than real GDP.

<Callout type="warning">
GDP is not a measure of welfare. Household production (housework, care) and leisure are excluded, and spending to clean up pollution raises GDP. It also ignores distribution and the depletion of assets such as natural resources. Do not equate a higher growth rate with a better quality of life. That said, GDP per capita is strongly correlated with many welfare indicators such as life expectancy and education and remains the most useful single indicator.
</Callout>

**Potential GDP** is the level of output that can be sustained without accelerating inflation. The sign and causes of the gap between actual and potential GDP (the output gap) are covered in AD-AS (Chapter 8) and business cycles (Chapter 10).

## Check your understanding

Nominal GDP rises from 100 trillion to 110 trillion won and the deflator from 100 to 105. What is real growth? $1.10/1.05-1\approx 4.8\%$. If in the same year the government spent 10 trillion won on civil servants' salaries and 10 trillion won on unemployment benefits, only the 10 trillion won of salaries enters G.
