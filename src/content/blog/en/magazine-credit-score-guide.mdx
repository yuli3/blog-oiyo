## Why Your Credit Score Matters

Your credit score is a numerical representation of **how reliably you repay borrowed money**.

A higher score means:
- Lower interest rates on loans (even a 1% difference can mean tens of thousands of dollars over a mortgage's life)
- Higher borrowing limits
- Better credit card terms and rewards
- Can influence apartment rental approvals and, in some states, insurance premiums

Ignoring your credit score means you may be paying high interest rates without understanding why.

---

## Credit Bureaus and Scoring Models

In the US, there are three major credit bureaus:

**Equifax, Experian, TransUnion** — each collects your credit data independently. Your reports from the three bureaus may differ slightly.

Two main scoring models interpret that data:

**FICO Score**: The most widely used by lenders (used in ~90% of lending decisions). Range: 300–850.  
**VantageScore**: Developed jointly by the three bureaus. Range: 300–850. Commonly shown in free consumer apps.

Different lenders may pull from different bureaus or use different scoring versions — which is why you might see slightly different numbers depending on where you check.

## How Your Credit Score Is Calculated

Your score breaks down into five factors.

### 1. Payment History (Most Important, ~35%)

**Have you paid on time?** Your history of on-time versus late payments is the single most influential factor.

- 30+ days late: significant score drop
- Even one missed payment can remain on your report for seven years — set up autopay

### 2. Credit Utilization (~30%)

**How much of your available credit are you using?** Lenders look at your balance relative to your credit limit.

- Using 70%+ of your limit → negative impact
- Ideal: keep utilization below 30%; below 10% is excellent

### 3. Length of Credit History (~15%)

**How long have you been using credit?** The average age of all your accounts matters.

- Keeping older accounts open is better than closing them
- Closing your oldest card can shorten your average credit age significantly

### 4. New Credit Inquiries (~10%)

**Hard inquiries** (from applying for new credit) temporarily lower your score.

- Multiple applications in a short window signals financial distress
- **Soft inquiries** (checking your own score, pre-approval checks) have no impact

### 5. Credit Mix (~10%)

Having different types of credit — revolving (credit cards) and installment (auto loans, student loans, mortgage) — managed responsibly adds a small benefit.

## How to Raise Your Credit Score

### Step 1: Set Up Autopay

Pay every bill on time, every month. Set autopay for at least the minimum payment — better yet, the full balance. A single missed payment can stay on your report for seven years.

### Step 2: Report On-Time Payments for Non-Credit Bills

**Experian Boost** (free): Links your bank account and credits you for on-time utility, phone, and streaming payments — often raises your score within days.

**UltraFICO**: Incorporates bank account activity into scoring. Worth exploring if you have limited credit history.

This is particularly effective for **young adults** building credit from scratch.

### Step 3: Use Credit Regularly and Pay in Full

Use a card for everyday spending and pay the balance in full each month. Doing nothing (no activity) can result in a thin or even declining credit profile over time.

### Step 4: Pay Down Small Balances

Even small outstanding balances across multiple accounts hurt your utilization ratio and debt level indicators. Clear high-interest small debts first.

### Step 5: Build Banking History

A long-standing relationship with a bank — direct deposit, savings activity, responsible credit use — can influence both your credit score and the bank's internal creditworthiness assessment.

## Behaviors That Hurt Your Score

- **Late payments**: Any amount, any account — don't miss due dates
- **Applying for many accounts at once**: Multiple hard inquiries signal urgency for credit
- **High credit utilization**: Stay below 30%; below 10% for best results
- **Closing old accounts**: Reduces average account age and total available credit
- **Maxing out cards**: Even if you pay in full at month-end, a high mid-cycle balance can be reported

## How to Check Your Credit Score for Free

**Free options:**
- **AnnualCreditReport.com**: Free full credit reports from all three bureaus (federally mandated, once per year; more often after 2023 rule changes)
- **Credit Karma**: Free VantageScore from Equifax and TransUnion, updated frequently
- **Experian free app**: Free FICO Score 8, updated monthly
- Most major credit cards now show your FICO score on the dashboard at no cost

Checking your own score is a **soft inquiry** and has zero impact on your score. Check it monthly.

## Credit Score Ranges (FICO)

| Score Range | Meaning |
|------------|---------|
| 800–850 | Exceptional (best rates, highest limits) |
| 740–799 | Very Good (excellent terms available) |
| 670–739 | Good (approved for most credit products) |
| 580–669 | Fair (higher rates, some restrictions) |
| Below 580 | Poor (limited options, secured cards recommended) |

## Credit Score and Loan Interest Rates

Borrowing the same amount with different credit scores can mean dramatically different interest rates.

**Example:** $300,000 mortgage, 30-year fixed

- Score 760+: ~6.5% rate → ~$1,896/month → total interest ~$382,000
- Score 660–679: ~7.2% rate → ~$2,040/month → total interest ~$434,000

A 0.7% rate difference = **over $52,000 more in interest** over 30 years. Managing your credit score is one of the highest-return financial habits you can build.

Meaningful improvement typically appears within 6 to 12 months of consistent good behavior.
