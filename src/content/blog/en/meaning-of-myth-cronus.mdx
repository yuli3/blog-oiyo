In Greek mythology, Cronus is the king of the Titans who overthrew Uranus, the father of Zeus, and a symbol of the structure in which those who gain power come to fear losing it again.

## Core Myth

At Gaia's request, Cronus castrates his father Uranus with a sickle and seizes the throne. Because Uranus had oppressed his children, Cronus's rebellion was an event that broke open a closed order. Yet after taking power, Cronus also hears a prophecy that he will fall at the hands of his own child, and he swallows each child as they are born.

Rhea hides the youngest child, Zeus, and gives Cronus a stone wrapped in swaddling cloth. Once grown, Zeus forces Cronus to disgorge the siblings he had swallowed, and after the Titan War he opens the age of the Olympian gods. Cronus's story condenses the paradox of the revolutionary who becomes a tyrant and the inevitability of generational succession.

The tradition of Cronus's Golden Age also needs to be remembered. As he became linked with the Roman Saturn, his age was sometimes imagined as a prosperous past with little labor or conflict. For that reason, Cronus carries not only the image of a tyrant but also nostalgia for a lost old order. But when longing for the Golden Age blocks growth in the present, that nostalgia turns again into a swallowing force.

## Reading as an Archetype

From a Jungian perspective, Cronus is an archetype that symbolizes the devouring father, the old order, and the pressure of time. In modern life, he can appear as authority that sees the growth of juniors or children as a threat, vested interests that block change within an organization, or an attitude trapped inside standards one has created oneself.

The shadow is control born from fear. It believes that eliminating the future can protect the present, but that action instead brings about downfall. The Cronus archetype asks the central question of power: "Am I raising the one who will come after me, or am I swallowing them?"

Cronus is also connected to the fear of time passing. When younger generations, new technologies, and different values appear, existing authority can see them either as evidence of growth or as a threat. The Cronian attitude appears when it chooses the latter. Mature authority prepares the next generation and knows when to step aside. Immature authority blocks possibility in order to keep its place. In the end, the swallowed children do not disappear; they return in the form of a stronger rebellion.

The myth of Cronus condenses the tension between parent and child, senior and junior, old system and new system. He ended the oppression he had suffered, but repeated the same fear in another form. Because of this repetition, Cronus becomes a powerful symbol that applies not only to individual psychology but also to organizations and history.

Reading him is also a way to ask whether I am passing the fear I inherited directly on to the next generation.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q. Is Cronus a god of time?**

A. Because of the name, he is often confused with Chronos, the god of time, but the mythological Titan Cronus belongs to a separate genealogy. In later periods, however, he was also combined with images of time.

**Q. Why did Cronus swallow his children?**

A. Because he feared the prophecy that his child would take power from him. This symbolizes the violence that appears when authority sees the future as a threat.
