## What Is Calculus?

Calculus is the mathematics of change and accumulation. Newton and Leibniz each built a system for it in the 17th century, and it is now the shared language of physics, engineering, economics and biology.

- **Differentiation** finds instantaneous rates of change.
- **Integration** finds areas and accumulated quantities.

The two are tied together by the **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus**: integrating a continuous function and then differentiating returns the original function. This chapter covers what that theorem rests on: functions, limits and continuity.

---

## A Review of Functions

A function $f:A\to B$ assigns exactly one element of $B$ to each element of $A$. Its codomain is specified as part of the function; the attained range $f(A)$ need not equal that codomain.

| Concept | Real-valued example and conditions |
| --- | --- |
| Domain | $f(x)=1/x$ is defined on $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$. |
| Codomain and range | For $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, $f(x)=x^2$, the codomain is $\mathbb{R}$ but the range is $[0,\infty)$. |
| Square root | The real function $\sqrt{x}$ has domain $[0,\infty)$. |
| Polynomial and rational | A polynomial $\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_kx^k$ or a ratio of polynomials; exclude inputs where the denominator is zero. |
| Trigonometric | $\sin,\cos$ are defined for all real inputs; $\tan$ requires $\cos x\ne0$. |
| Exponential and logarithmic | $a^x$ and $\log_a x$ with $a>0$, $a\ne1$; logarithms require $x>0$. |

## An Intuitive Understanding of the Limit

$\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L$ describes outputs approaching $L$ as inputs approach $a$. Here we consider finite limits of real functions, with $a$ an accumulation point of the domain. The value $f(a)$ need not be defined.

$$
\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}=x+1\quad(x\ne1),\qquad
\lim_{x\to1}\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}=2.
$$

Cancellation is valid only for $x\ne1$. It does not make the original expression defined at $x=1$.

### The epsilon–delta definition and a proof

For every $\varepsilon>0$, there must exist a $\delta>0$ such that every domain input $x$ satisfying $0<|x-a|<\delta$ also satisfies $|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon$. The choice of $\delta$ may depend on $\varepsilon$, but cannot be selected separately after seeing each qualifying $x$.

In the example, $a=1$ and $L=2$ give $|f(x)-2|=|x-1|$. For any $\varepsilon>0$, choose $\delta=\varepsilon$; the implication follows immediately. Checking a few nearby numerical inputs is not a proof covering all such inputs.

## Left-Hand and Right-Hand Limits

Write a right-hand approach as $\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)$ and a left-hand approach as $\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)$. When the domain permits approach from both sides, a two-sided finite limit exists exactly when the one-sided limits agree.

| Approach for $f(x)=\lvert x\rvert/x$ ($x\ne0$) | Function value |
| --- | --- |
| Toward $0$ from $x<0$ | $-1$ |
| Toward $0$ from $x>0$ | $1$ |

The values differ, so $\lim_{x\to0}|x|/x$ does not exist. This is the absolute value divided by $x$, not the absolute-value function itself. At a domain endpoint, continuity uses approach from within the domain.

---

## Properties of Limits

Suppose $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L$ and $\lim_{x\to a}g(x)=M$ both exist as finite values, and $c$ is constant.

| Operation | Result and condition |
| --- | --- |
| Sum or difference | $\lim(f\pm g)=L\pm M$ |
| Product | $\lim(fg)=LM$ |
| Quotient | $\lim(f/g)=L/M$, requiring $M\ne0$ |
| Constant multiple | $\lim(cf)=cL$ |
| Power | $\lim f^n=L^n$; here $n$ is a positive integer |

A zero denominator limit prevents use of the quotient law. The expression $0/0$ is not a limit value: further analysis is needed.

### The squeeze theorem

If $g(x)\le f(x)\le h(x)$ throughout a sufficiently small punctured neighborhood of $a$, and both bounding limits equal $L$, the middle limit also equals $L$.

$$
-|x|\le x\sin(1/x)\le |x|\quad(x\ne0).
$$

Both bounds follow from $|\sin(1/x)|\le1$. As $x\to0$, the bounds approach 0, so the middle limit is 0. Using $|x|$ ensures the bounds remain valid for negative inputs as well.

## Limits at Infinity and Asymptotes

If $f(x)$ approaches a finite value $L$ as $x$ grows without bound, we write $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=L$. For a rational function, divide numerator and denominator by the highest power in the denominator.

$$
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x^2+2x}{x^2-1}
=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3+2/x}{1-1/x^2}=\frac{3+0}{1-0}=3.
$$

- **Horizontal asymptote:** if $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=L$ or $\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)=L$, the line $y=L$ is a horizontal asymptote. Here it is $y=3$.
- **Vertical asymptote:** if $f(x)\to\pm\infty$ as $x\to a^+$ or $x\to a^-$, the line $x=a$ is a vertical asymptote. The function above has them at $x=1$ and $x=-1$, where the denominator is zero and the numerator is not.

Writing $f(x)\to\infty$ does not say the limit exists; it describes how the function diverges.

## Continuity

A function $f$ is **continuous** at a point $a$ of its domain when $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$. That one line contains three conditions.

1. $f(a)$ is defined.
2. $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)$ exists.
3. The two values are equal.

| Type of discontinuity | Condition | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Removable | The limit exists, but $f(a)$ is missing or differs from it | $\dfrac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ at $x=1$ |
| Jump | Both one-sided limits are finite but unequal | $\lvert x\rvert/x$ at $x=0$ |
| Infinite | At least one one-sided limit diverges to $\pm\infty$ | $1/x$ at $x=0$ |

Polynomial, trigonometric, exponential and logarithmic functions are continuous on their domains. Sums, differences, products and compositions of continuous functions are continuous, and a quotient is continuous wherever the denominator is nonzero.

---

## The Intermediate Value Theorem

If $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a,b]$ and $k$ lies between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then there is at least one $c\in(a,b)$ with $f(c)=k$.

A continuous function cannot skip values. If temperature is continuous in time and rises from 0°C in the morning to 15°C at noon, it passes through every temperature in between at least once.

**Example.** $f(x)=x^3-x-2$ is a polynomial, so it is continuous on $[1,2]$. Since $f(1)=-2<0$ and $f(2)=4>0$, some $c$ in $(1,2)$ satisfies $f(c)=0$. The theorem says only that a root **exists**; it gives neither its value nor how many there are.

Drop continuity and the theorem fails. $f(x)=1/x$ has $f(-1)=-1$ and $f(1)=1$, but it is not continuous on $[-1,1]$ and no $c$ gives $f(c)=0$.

## Check your understanding

**Problem.** Find $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2}\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}$ and classify the discontinuity of $g(x)=\dfrac{x^2-4}{x-2}$ at $x=2$. How should $g(2)$ be defined to make $g$ continuous?

**Solution.** For $x\ne2$, $\dfrac{x^2-4}{x-2}=x+2$, so the limit is 4. The limit exists but $g(2)$ is undefined, so the discontinuity is removable. Setting $g(2)=4$ gives $\lim_{x\to2}g(x)=g(2)$, and $g$ is continuous.
