# Macroeconomics — To Interpret Prices and Unemployment, Start with How They Are Measured

The two goals of macroeconomic policy are price stability and full employment. The Phillips curve, which links them as a trade-off, is covered in [Chapter 9](/en/education-economics-ch20/); this chapter first sets out what the two variables measure and where illusions arise.

## 1. The consumer price index is the price of a fixed basket

The **consumer price index (CPI)** fixes a representative household's consumption basket at a base date and measures how the cost of buying that basket changes.

The inflation rate is a **rate of change** in the price level, not the **level** itself. If the CPI goes from 110 to 120, inflation is $(120-110)/110\approx 9.1\%$. "The CPI is 120, so prices rose 20%" mistakes the cumulative rise since the base year for this year's inflation.

A fixed basket has a systematic **upward bias**.

- **Substitution bias**: when rice becomes more expensive people buy more bread, but the basket is fixed and overweights the dearer rice.
- **Quality bias**: if a smartphone at the same price gets better every year, the index records no price change. Without quality adjustment it misses the fall in the real price.
- **New-product bias**: new products enter the basket late, so the price declines early in their life are missed.

## 2. Nominal and real: the Fisher equation

<FormulaBox
  label="Fisher equation (approximation)"
  formula="i \approx r + \pi^e"
  description="The nominal interest rate i is the real rate r plus expected inflation π^e. The exact form is (1+i) = (1+r)(1+π^e)."
/>

If nominal wages rise 5% while the CPI rises 9.1%, real wages fall by $1.05/1.091-1\approx -3.8\%$.

**Expected** inflation is built into contract interest rates, so it redistributes little. **Unexpected** inflation transfers wealth from creditors to debtors. If you lend at a nominal 5% expecting 2% inflation and inflation turns out to be 6%, the real rate the creditor receives is −1%, not 3%. People holding fixed-rate loans as lenders, pensioners and others with incomes fixed in nominal terms lose out.

## 3. For the unemployment rate, the denominator matters

<FormulaBox
  label="Labour market indicators"
  formula="\text{Unemployment rate} = \frac{\text{Unemployed}}{\text{Labour force}},\quad \text{Employment rate} = \frac{\text{Employed}}{\text{Working-age population}}"
  description="Labour force = employed + unemployed. The unemployed are people able and willing to work who actively searched but have no job. People who have given up searching are classified as outside the labour force."
/>

Consider an economy with a working-age population of 100, of whom 57 are employed, 3 unemployed and 40 outside the labour force. The unemployment rate is $3/60=5\%$, the employment rate 57% and the participation rate 60%.

If one unemployed person gives up and becomes a **discouraged worker**, there are 2 unemployed in a labour force of 59 and the unemployment rate **falls** to about 3.4%. Nothing has improved in the labour market, yet the unemployment rate looks better. The employment rate (57%) is unchanged, so looking at both rates together avoids the illusion.

## 4. Three types of unemployment

<LectureTable
  title="Types of unemployment and responses"
  headers={["Type", "Cause", "Response"]}
  rows={[
    ["Frictional", "Search time when changing jobs or entering work", "Better matching of vacancies and job seekers"],
    ["Structural", "Mismatch of skills and jobs after technological or industrial change", "Retraining, support for relocation"],
    ["Cyclical", "Insufficient aggregate demand", "Monetary and fiscal policy"],
  ]}
/>

Frictional and structural unemployment remain even when the economy is strong. The unemployment rate made up of these two is the **natural rate of unemployment**. It is not zero, and it depends on institutions such as unemployment benefits and minimum wages and on how efficiently the labour market matches workers to jobs. How to compute the natural rate from flows into and out of unemployment is covered in [Chapter 20](/en/education-economics-ch31/).

The empirical relationship between cyclical unemployment and output is **Okun's law**: when unemployment is 1 percentage point above its natural rate, output is roughly 2% below potential, a relation observed in many countries. The coefficient is an empirical regularity that varies across countries and periods, not an identity.

<Callout type="warning">
Core inflation (excluding food and energy) is a tool for filtering out temporary price shocks to see the trend; it is not the definition of "true inflation". What households actually feel includes food and energy. And a low unemployment rate does not mean a healthy labour market if many people work part-time involuntarily or have stopped looking. Look also at broader unemployment measures (labour underutilisation indicators).
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

The CPI rises from 110 to 120 and nominal wages by 5%. What are inflation and the change in real wages? Inflation is about 9.1% and real wages fall about 3.8%. If in the same year one of the 3 unemployed gave up searching, the unemployment rate would fall from 5% to about 3.4%, but the employment rate would stay at 57%.
