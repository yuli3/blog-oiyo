# Macroeconomics — When Prices Move, a Point in IS-LM Becomes the Aggregate Demand Curve

The IS-LM model of [Chapter 4](/en/education-economics-ch14/) held prices fixed. When the price level rises, the real purchasing power of a given nominal money supply, $M/P$, falls, LM shifts left and output falls. Joining these combinations of price level and output gives the **aggregate demand (AD) curve**. Adding an **aggregate supply (AS) curve**, which shows how much firms produce at each price level, determines prices and output together.

## 1. Deriving the aggregate demand curve

In the economy of Chapter 4 (IS: $Y=410-10r$, money demand $0.5Y-20r$), set the nominal money supply at $M=180$ and leave the price level $P$ as a variable. LM becomes $180/P=0.5Y-20r$. Eliminating $r$ with IS gives:

<FormulaBox
  label="Aggregate demand curve"
  formula="Y = 328 + \frac{72}{P}"
  description="At P = 1, Y = 400 (the equilibrium of Chapter 4); at P = 1.2, Y = 388; at P = 0.8, Y = 418. A higher price level lowers real balances, raises the interest rate and reduces investment (the Keynes effect)."
/>

A **movement along** the AD curve is caused by a change in the price level; a **shift** of the AD curve itself is caused by government spending, taxes, the nominal money supply or expectations. If the nominal money supply rises by 10%, AD becomes $Y=328+79.2/P$, a curve that reaches output of 400 at a price level of 1.1.

## 2. Short-run and long-run aggregate supply

In the **long run** wages and prices fully adjust, so output stays at the potential output $Y^*$ set by labour, capital and technology. Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) is vertical at $Y^*$.

In the **short run**, because of nominal wage contracts, menu costs and imperfect information, prices do not adjust at once. When prices are higher than expected, firms produce more.

<FormulaBox
  label="Short-run aggregate supply"
  formula="P = P^e + \frac{1}{\alpha}\,(Y - Y^*)"
  description="P^e is the expected price level. Output exceeds potential only when actual prices exceed expectations. When expectations catch up with actual prices, SRAS shifts up and output returns to Y*."
/>

Take $Y^*=400$, $P^e=1$ and a slope $1/\alpha=0.002$.

## 3. The short and long run of a monetary expansion

If the nominal money supply rises by 10%:

- **Short run**: the new AD and the existing SRAS meet at $Y\approx 406.2$ and $P\approx 1.012$. Output rises a lot and prices a little.
- **Long run**: as people raise their expected price level to reflect higher prices, SRAS shifts up. When the expected price level reaches 1.1, the new equilibrium is $Y=400$, $P=1.1$. Output returns to potential and only prices have risen, by exactly 10%.

The property that nominal money cannot change real variables (output, employment) in the long run and changes only prices is **long-run monetary neutrality**. How large and how long-lasting the short-run effect is depends on the slope of SRAS and on how quickly expectations adjust. The adjustment path is examined further in [Chapter 15](/en/education-economics-ch26/).

## 4. Four shocks

<LectureTable
  title="Short- and long-run outcomes by shock"
  headers={["Shock", "Short run (Y, P)", "Long run"]}
  rows={[
    ["Aggregate demand rises (G↑, M↑)", "Y↑ P↑", "Y = Y*, P↑"],
    ["Aggregate demand falls", "Y↓ P↓", "Y = Y*, P↓ (a long slump if adjustment is slow)"],
    ["Adverse supply shock (oil price↑)", "Y↓ P↑ (stagflation)", "Back to the start if temporary; Y* falls if permanent"],
    ["Favourable supply shock (productivity↑)", "Y↑ P↓", "Y* rises"],
  ]}
/>

An adverse supply shock puts policymakers in a dilemma. Raising aggregate demand to protect output pushes prices up further; cutting aggregate demand to control prices deepens the recession. The oil shocks of the 1970s were such a case, and the policy options are covered in [Chapter 10](/en/education-economics-ch21/) and [Chapter 13](/en/education-economics-ch24/).

<Callout type="warning">
A vertical long-run aggregate supply does not mean "policy is powerless"; it means the **level** of output is independent of aggregate demand in the long run. Growth that raises potential output itself is the subject of [Chapters 11 and 12](/en/education-economics-ch22/). And ignoring short-run price stickiness and reading every recession through a vertical AS leads to misclassifying unemployment caused by weak demand as "natural". Conversely, ignoring long-run adjustment leads to the mistaken belief that expanding demand raises output forever.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

In this economy, what is the long-run equilibrium if the nominal money supply rises by 20%? In the long run $Y=400$ and AD is $Y=328+86.4/P$, so $86.4/P=72$ gives $P=1.2$. Prices rise by exactly 20%. Real balances $M/P=216/1.2=180$ are back to their original level, so interest rates and investment return to their original values as well.
