# Macroeconomics — Two Arrows Cannot Replace a Simultaneous Solution

[Chapter 4](/en/education-economics-ch14/) and [Chapter 7](/en/education-economics-ch18/) shifted curves to see signs and magnitudes. This chapter solves the same model as a system of equations and writes the equilibrium and comparative-static coefficients as general formulas. These formulas are needed to answer "how much" and to see where the numbers in policy simulations come from.

## 1. Writing the system of equations

Write IS and LM as follows.

$$\text{IS: } Y=\alpha-\beta r+\gamma G,\qquad \text{LM: } \frac{M}{P}=\lambda Y-\mu r$$

$\beta$ is the response of spending to the interest rate (including the multiplier), $\gamma$ the government spending multiplier, $\lambda$ the income sensitivity of money demand and $\mu$ its interest sensitivity. Collecting the endogenous variables $Y$ and $r$ on the left gives

<FormulaBox
  label="Matrix form"
  formula="\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \beta \\ \lambda & -\mu \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} Y \\ r \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} \alpha+\gamma G \\ M/P \end{pmatrix}"
  description="The determinant of the coefficient matrix is −(μ + βλ), which is not zero, so there is a unique solution. Solving systems of equations with matrices is covered in Economics Math Chapter 5."
/>

Solving with Cramer's rule gives

<FormulaBox
  label="Equilibrium output and interest rate"
  formula="Y^*=\frac{\mu(\alpha+\gamma G)+\beta\,M/P}{\mu+\beta\lambda},\qquad r^*=\frac{\lambda(\alpha+\gamma G)-M/P}{\mu+\beta\lambda}"
  description="The denominator μ + βλ captures the interaction between the two markets. The numerators show how the exogenous variables of the goods market and the money market are combined."
/>

## 2. Checking against earlier numbers

The Chapter 4 economy has $\alpha=260$, $\beta=10$, $\gamma=5$, $G=30$, $\lambda=0.5$, $\mu=20$ and $M/P=180$.

- $Y^*=\dfrac{20\times 410+10\times 180}{20+5}=\dfrac{10{,}000}{25}=400$
- $r^*=\dfrac{0.5\times 410-180}{25}=\dfrac{25}{25}=1\%$

This matches the graphical solution of Chapter 4.

## 3. Comparative-static coefficients

Differentiating the equilibrium formulas with respect to the exogenous variables gives the policy effects.

## 4. The interest-rate lower bound breaks the linear solution

If real balances are raised sharply to 220, the linear solution is $Y^*=(20\times 410+10\times 220)/25=416$ and $r^*=(205-220)/25=-0.6\%$. But the nominal interest rate cannot fall far below zero. The actual equilibrium is $Y=410$, set by IS at a zero interest rate; at a zero rate and output of 410 the required real balances are $0.5\times 410=205$, so any increase beyond 205 does not move output. Once the interest rate hits zero, LM becomes effectively flat and we enter the liquidity-trap region of Chapter 7.

<Callout type="warning">
A linear approximation holds only around one point. Where the structure of the model changes, as at the interest-rate lower bound, it is the regime that changes, not the coefficients. Policy simulations that omit this kink overstate the effect of monetary policy. Making the price level endogenous gives the aggregate demand curve of [Chapter 8](/en/education-economics-ch19/); extending to an open economy gives the Mundell-Fleming model of [International Finance Chapter 3](/en/education-international-finance-ch3/).
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

If $\mu$ falls from 20 to 5 (LM becomes steeper), what happens to the fiscal and monetary multipliers? The denominator $\mu+\beta\lambda$ becomes $5+5=10$. $dY/dG=5\times 5/10=2.5$ and $dY/d(M/P)=10/10=1$. The fiscal effect falls from 4 to 2.5 and the monetary effect rises from 0.4 to 1 — the same result as the table in Chapter 7.
