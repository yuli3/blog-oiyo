# Ch 39. The IS-LM Model: Policy Scenarios

When analyzing the effects of policy in macroeconomics, the **IS-LM model** is the first tool economists reach for. Formalized by British economist John Hicks as a diagram of Keynes's *General Theory*, this model shows how the real economy (goods market) and the financial economy (money market) are connected.

---

## 1. The IS Curve (Investment-Saving)

Represents equilibrium in the goods market. As income (Y) rises, saving increases, and for investment to remain in balance, the interest rate (r) must fall. The curve is therefore **downward-sloping**.

- **Shift factors**: Moved by fiscal policy — an increase in government spending (G) or a decrease in taxes (T).

---

## 2. The LM Curve (Liquidity Preference–Money Supply)

Represents equilibrium in the money market. As income (Y) rises, transaction demand for money increases, and with a fixed money supply, the interest rate (r) must rise to restore equilibrium. The curve is therefore **upward-sloping**.

- **Shift factors**: Moved by changes in the money supply (M) from the central bank.

<Callout type="info">
**Crowding-Out Effect**: When government spending (G) increases and shifts the IS curve to the right, income (Y) rises — but so does the interest rate (r). The higher interest rate suppresses private investment, partially offsetting the impact of the fiscal expansion. This is known as the **crowding-out effect**.
