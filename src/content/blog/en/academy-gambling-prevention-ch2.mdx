In Chapter 1, we saw that the house edge on baccarat's banker bet is **about 1.06%.** One dollar out of a hundred. That sounds like nothing.

This chapter calculates how that one dollar becomes your entire bankroll.

## What is expected value?

**Expected value (EV)** is the average amount you win or lose on a single bet. The math is simple.

<FormulaBox>
Expected value = (probability of winning × amount won) − (probability of losing × amount lost)
</FormulaBox>

Let's check it with a coin flip. Heads wins you $10; tails loses you $10. The expected value is:

(0.5 × $10) − (0.5 × $10) = **$0**

A game with an expected value of zero is called a **fair game.** Play it forever, and on average, you break even.

**Real-world gambling has no fair games.**

## The real expected value of baccarat's banker bet

Bet $100 on the banker:

- ~45.86% chance to win → you're paid $95 after a 5% commission
- ~44.62% chance to lose → you lose $100
- ~9.52% chance of a tie → your bet is returned

Work out the expected value and you get roughly **−$1.06.** In other words, **every $100 you wager, on average, $1.06 disappears.**

<HighlightBox>
The critical thing about that $1.06 is that **it's not "money you lose if you're unlucky."** It's **money you are, on average, guaranteed to lose.** You'll win some hands and lose others, but as the hands add up, the actual outcome gets pulled toward this average.
</HighlightBox>

## How many hands does it take to lose $1,000?

Bet $10 per hand, and on average $1.06 evaporates per hand. For a starting bankroll of $1,000 to hit zero:

<FormulaBox>
1,000 ÷ 1.06 ≈ 943 hands
</FormulaBox>

**About 943 hands.** Sound like a lot? Online gambling rounds take **less than 30 seconds each.** Play without stopping, and arithmetically that's under eight hours.

Here's the expected balance at each stage:

| Hands played | Expected balance |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | $1,000 |
| 10 | $989.40 |
| 50 | $947.00 |
| 100 | $894.00 |
| 200 | $788.00 |
| 500 | $470.00 |
| 943 | **$0** |

## Why people don't feel it

Given how clear the expected value is, why does gambling keep going? Because **over a short stretch, that line is invisible.**

After 100 hands, the expected loss is only about $10 — an amount that gets **completely buried in the swings of luck.** Some 100-hand sessions end up ahead; some end up behind. So it looks like "a game you could win."

But as more hands are played, luck's influence shrinks relative to the total, and **only the house edge remains.** That's the **Law of Large Numbers**, which we cover next chapter.

<Quiz>
**Think about it**

A friend says, "I won $500 at baccarat yesterday."

Does that disprove the house edge?

Answer: **No.** The house edge doesn't predict the outcome of a single hand, or even a single day. It only says where the numbers converge once enough hands are played. Winning $500 yesterday works as **the exact mechanism that keeps your friend playing** — and as long as they keep playing, the results will track the chart above.
</Quiz>

## Summary

1. Expected value isn't "the loss you take on a bad day" — it's a **loss that is, on average, guaranteed.**
2. On baccarat's banker bet, that's $1.06 per $100. A $1,000 bankroll is **mathematically drained in about 943 hands.**
3. Play briefly and you won't see it. Play long enough and you always will. **Getting you to play a lot of hands is the operator's entire business model.**
