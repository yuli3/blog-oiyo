# Macroeconomics — Before Being a Function of the Interest Rate, Investment Is a Comparison of Capital's Return and Cost

The IS curve of [Chapter 4](/en/education-economics-ch14/) sloped downward because investment responds to the interest rate. This chapter looks at the microeconomic basis for that response. Firms compare what one more unit of capital earns with what it costs — the same logic as net present value (NPV) in corporate finance, applied to macroeconomic variables. Investment is smaller than consumption in GDP but far more volatile, and it explains much of the business cycle.

## 1. The user cost of capital

The cost to a firm of using a machine for a year is not the price of the machine itself but the interest the money could have earned elsewhere plus the value the machine loses through wear over the year.

<FormulaBox
  label="User cost of capital"
  formula="uc = (r + \delta)\,P_k"
  description="r is the real interest rate, δ the depreciation rate and P_k the price of capital goods. For a 100-million-won machine with r = 5% and δ = 10%, the annual user cost is 15 million won. With corporate tax and investment tax credits, it is adjusted to an after-tax cost."
/>

A firm adds capital until the **value of the marginal product** of capital equals the user cost. Because the marginal product of capital diminishes, a lower user cost raises the target capital stock.

Suppose a firm's return on capital (the value of the marginal product per unit of capital divided by the price of capital goods) is $MPK=20\%-0.5\%\times K$, and $\delta=5\%$:

- $r=5\%$: $uc=10\%$ → $20-0.5K=10$ → target capital $K^*=20$
- $r=3\%$: $uc=8\%$ → $K^*=24$

If the current capital stock is 18, investment is 2 at an interest rate of 5% and 6 at 3%. A 2-point rate cut tripled investment. Adding up across all firms this relationship — lower rates raise the target capital stock and hence investment — gives the investment function $I(r)$.

Keynes expressed the same decision through the **marginal efficiency of capital (MEC)**: the discount rate that makes the present value of a project's expected returns equal to its cost. Firms invest if the MEC exceeds the market interest rate. The key is expectations. If entrepreneurs turn pessimistic, the MEC falls and investment collapses even if interest rates are unchanged. These swings in expectations, which Keynes called "animal spirits", **shift** the IS curve.

## 2. The accelerator

The target capital stock is also proportional to output. If each unit of output needs $v$ units of capital, $K^*=vY$ and net investment is $I=v\,\Delta Y$. Investment responds to the **change** in output, not its **level**. In an industry with $v=3$, a slowdown in output growth from 5% to 3% cuts net investment by 40% even though output is still growing. This is one reason investment is far more volatile than consumption.

## 3. Tobin's q

<FormulaBox
  label="Tobin's q"
  formula="q = \frac{\text{Market value of the firm}}{\text{Replacement cost of its capital}}"
  description="If q > 1, the market values each won of capital at more than a won, so buying and installing new capital adds value and firms invest. If q < 1, buying existing firms is cheaper than investing."
/>

Because q reflects the stock market's view of expected returns, it carries the same information as the user-cost model while being observable. It is also a channel through which rising share prices lead investment.

## 4. When investment becomes insensitive to interest rates

Residential and inventory investment follow the same logic: the house price relative to construction cost (the q of housing) and the user cost of holding inventories (interest plus storage) drive each.

<Callout type="warning">
Before concluding that "the investment function is wrong" because investment did not rise after a rate cut, check whether uncertainty, credit conditions or expectations shifted the investment function over the same period. Distinguishing a movement along a curve from a shift of the curve is the same identification problem as in [Microeconomics, Chapter 2](/en/education-economics-ch2/). The crowding-out effect of a fiscal expansion, which raises interest rates and lowers investment, works through the rise in the user cost described in this chapter.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

A machine costs 200 million won, the real interest rate is 4% and depreciation is 8%. What is the annual user cost? $(0.04+0.08)\times 200$ million = 24 million won. If the machine earns a net return of 20 million won a year, the firm does not invest. If the interest rate falls to 1%, the user cost becomes 18 million won and the firm invests.
