# Your Attachment Style: Tracing the Roots of Your Relationship Patterns

Humans need connection with others from the moment they're born. How an infant relates to their caregiver in those early years goes on to shape almost every close relationship in adulthood — romantic partnerships, friendships, even relationships at work. That's the central insight of **attachment theory**.

## The Scientific Origins of Attachment Theory

Attachment theory was formalized in the 1960s by British psychiatrist and psychologist **John Bowlby**. Bowlby argued that a child's early relationship with their caregiver builds an "internal working model" — a template that goes on to shape what they expect, and how they react, in every relationship that follows.

In 1987, psychologists **Cindy Hazan** and **Phillip Shaver** applied Bowlby's theory of childhood attachment to adult romantic relationships. They initially sorted adult attachment into three styles — secure, anxious, and avoidant — and later researchers added a fourth, **fearful-avoidant**, completing the four-style model used today.

## The 4 Adult Attachment Styles

**Secure**: You see both yourself and others in a positive light. Intimacy feels natural, and you carry a baseline trust that the relationship will hold even through conflict. Roughly 55–65% of adults fall into this style.

**Anxious**: You want deep connection but carry a strong fear of rejection or abandonment. Small shifts in a partner's behavior can trigger a strong reaction, and reassurance-seeking behavior can show up.

**Avoidant**: Independence comes first. Intimacy can feel like a threat to your freedom, so you tend to suppress emotional expression and protect your own space.

**Fearful-Avoidant**: You crave connection and fear it at the same time. Internally, the urge to "get closer" collides with the fear of "getting hurt." This style is often linked to past trauma.

## What Makes This Test Different

Most short attachment-style tests use forced two-option questions, which makes it hard to catch the subtler differences for people who sit right on the border between styles. This test applies a **1–5 Likert scale across 12 questions** and calculates an independent score for each of the four styles. That means you don't just get a single label — you also get a visual breakdown of how strongly each style shows up in you.

## How to Read Your Results

### Attachment Style Isn't Fixed

Here's an important misconception to clear up first: attachment style isn't an inborn trait — it's **a pattern shaped by relationship experience**. Which means it can change. Being anxious or avoidant isn't a label stamped on you; it's a language for understanding your current relationship pattern.

### Growth Directions by Style

For the **anxious** style, the most helpful practice is putting emotion into words. Instead of reassurance-seeking behavior (repeated texts, excessive questions), saying directly, "I'm feeling anxious right now — can we talk for a minute?" does far more to stabilize the relationship.

For the **avoidant** style, what matters most is explaining the meaning behind the distance you create. Recognizing that pulling away emotionally is a self-protective reflex — and saying clearly, "I need some time to myself right now" — reduces the hurt the other person feels.

For the **fearful-avoidant** style, working with a professional counselor tends to be the most effective path. If past trauma is involved, approaches like CBT or EMDR can help integrate the two competing internal voices.

Even the **secure** style shouldn't get complacent. Staying in a long-term relationship with an avoidant or anxious partner can wear a secure person down into a kind of "secure burnout." Ongoing self-care matters here too.

### Attachment Theory and Compatibility

The most stable pairing in attachment theory is **secure + almost any other style**. A secure partner can calm an anxious partner's fears and give an avoidant partner the safe space they need. The hardest pairing is **anxious + avoidant**, where the anxious partner wants more connection and the avoidant partner wants more space — pushing each other toward extremes in what's known as the pursue-withdraw pattern.
