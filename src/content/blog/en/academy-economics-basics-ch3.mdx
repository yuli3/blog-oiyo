# Introduction to Economics — Consumers Match Relative Prices at the Margin

Utility maximization is not "buying the bundle you like best." It is the point **on the budget line** where the rate at which you give up one good to get a little more of another equals the rate the market requires you to give up.

## 1. The first-order condition for an interior solution

The absolute value of the slope of an indifference curve is the marginal rate of substitution, $\mathrm{MRS} = MU_{x}/MU_{y}$. The absolute value of the slope of the budget line is $P_{x}/P_{y}$. Where the two are tangent, utility can no longer be raised by rearranging the bundle.

<FormulaBox
  label="Consumer equilibrium (interior solution)"
  formula="MRS = MUx / MUy = Px / Py"
  description="In terms of marginal utility per won, MUx/Px = MUy/Py. If one good gives more utility per won, shift spending towards it."
/>

With $MU_{x}=10$, $MU_{y}=4$, $P_{x}$ = 2,000 won and $P_{y}$ = 1,000 won, marginal utility per won is 1/200 and 1/250 respectively. The consumer should buy more X and less Y. With diminishing marginal utility, $MU_{x}$ falls and $MU_{y}$ rises as spending shifts, restoring the equality.

Perfect substitutes have straight-line indifference curves, so there is no tangency and only the cheaper good is bought (a corner solution). Perfect complements have right-angled curves, so they are bought only in fixed proportions. "MRS always equals the price ratio" is a statement about interior solutions, convex preferences and positive consumption.

## 2. A price change splits into substitution and income effects

When the price of X rises, two things happen at once: the relative price moves against X, so X falls (the **substitution effect**), and real income falls, so if the goods are normal, both fall (the **income effect**). For an inferior good, the income effect pushes X up. If that income effect outweighs substitution, the good is a Giffen good.

A downward-sloping observed demand curve does not mean there is no income effect. The Hicks decomposition compensates the consumer back to the original indifference curve; the Slutsky decomposition compensates so that the original bundle can still be bought. First check which compensation an exam question uses.

## 3. Utility numbers may be all about order

With ordinal utility, a monotonic transformation leaves preferences unchanged: $u$ and $\ln u$ have the same MRS. "Utility went from 10 to 20, so I am twice as happy" is wrong until you move to cardinal utility (risk, expected utility). The risk premium of financial economics is the next step.

<Callout type="warning">
Equalizing marginal utility per won does not end in an equality with discrete units, indivisible goods or quantity constraints. If only whole units can be bought, it becomes an inequality; with rationing, a shadow price appears. This is also why in-kind support can yield lower utility than cash.
</Callout>

Labour supply is a choice between leisure and consumption. The substitution effect of a wage rise increases work; the income effect increases leisure. A backward-bending labour supply curve follows the same grammar of income and substitution effects as this chapter.

## Check your understanding

The two goods have the same price and $MU_{x}=6$, $MU_{y}=6$, but the consumer buys only X. Is this an interior solution? MRS = 1 and the price ratio = 1, so the first-order condition holds. But if the consumer is already at a corner, whether that point is unique depends on the shape of preferences. With perfect substitutes, every point on the budget line may be equally good. The fact that the condition holds does not prove an interior tangency.
