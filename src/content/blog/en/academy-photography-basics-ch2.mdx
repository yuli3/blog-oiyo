## What is exposure?

**Exposure** is what determines how bright a photo looks. A photo that's too bright is **overexposed**; one that's too dark is **underexposed**.

The three factors that combine to produce correct exposure are known as the **exposure triangle**.

| Setting | What changes directly |
| --- | --- |
| Aperture | Incoming light and depth of field |
| Exposure time | Light-collection duration and motion rendering |
| ISO | Signal amplification/output brightness, not incoming light |

These three elements have to balance against each other to produce a photo with the brightness you want.

---

## Shutter speed

Shutter speed is the length of time the shutter stays open, which determines **how long light reaches the sensor**.

### How it's notated

| Exposure-time change | With other settings unchanged |
| --- | --- |
| 1/1000 s → 1/500 s | Twice the time and light; greater potential motion blur |
| 1/500 s → 1/250 s | One stop more light |
| 1 s → 2 s | Twice the time and light; movement accumulates |

### Shutter speed's two roles

**1. Controlling brightness**: the slower it is, the more light comes in → the brighter the photo

**2. Rendering motion**:

| Shutter speed | Effect | Good for |
|----------|------|--------------|
| 1/1000s or faster | Freezes the instant (subject stays sharp) | Sports, birds, water droplets |
| 1/250s | Freezes typical motion | Someone walking, a moving car |
| 1/60s | Slight blur possible | Portraits (the handheld limit) |
| 1/30s or slower | Motion blur | Waterfalls, light trails |
| Several seconds or more | Full light trails | Star trails, night scenes |

> **Avoiding handheld blur**: as a rule of thumb, use at least `1/(focal length)s`. With a 50mm lens, that means at least 1/50s.

## Aperture

The size of the light-passing opening inside the lens is expressed as an **f-number (f-stop)**.

### The counterintuitive relationship of f-numbers

A typical full-stop scale is f/1.4, f/2, f/2.8, f/4, f/5.6, f/8, f/11, f/16. Each step toward a larger number roughly halves incoming light. The f-number is focal length divided by entrance-pupil diameter; the exact full-stop ratio is $\sqrt{2}$.

> **Note**: the **smaller** the f-number, the **larger** the opening — it's an inverse relationship.

### Depth of field

Aperture's second role is determining the **range that stays in focus (depth of field)**.

| Aperture | Details and conditions |
| --- | --- |
| f/1.4–f/2.8 | Depth of field: **Shallow**; Background: Strong blur (bokeh); Use: Portraits, product photography |
| f/4–f/5.6 | Depth of field: Moderate; Background: Moderately blurred; Use: Everyday snapshots |
| f/8–f/11 | Depth of field: Favors deeper focus; Background: Tends to widen the acceptably sharp range; Use: Landscapes, architecture |
| f/16 and beyond | Depth of field: Favors deeper focus; Background: Diffraction can soften fine detail; Use: Starburst effects |

These are trends at unchanged focal length and subject distance. Aperture alone does not determine depth of field or guarantee a sharp background throughout.

## ISO (sensitivity)

In a digital camera, ISO relates to signal amplification and output brightness. At unchanged aperture, exposure time, and illumination, raising ISO does not increase the photons reaching the sensor.

ISO 100, 200, 400, 800, 1600, 3200, 6400 are full-stop settings. Noise at matched output brightness also depends on incoming light, read noise, amplification, and processing. Higher ISO alone does not universally create new noise.

### Choosing an ISO

| Situation | Recommended ISO |
|------|----------|
| Bright outdoors | ISO 100–200 |
| Overcast/shade | ISO 400 |
| Indoors (bright lighting) | ISO 800–1600 |
| Indoors (dim lighting) | ISO 3200–6400 |
| Night/concerts | ISO 6400 or higher |

> **Rule of thumb**: keep ISO as low as possible, and handle exposure first through shutter speed and aperture.

---

## How the exposure triangle interacts

The three elements are **linked**. If one changes, one or more remaining settings can compensate for brightness. Equal incoming light differs from matching output brightness through ISO.

### The concept of a stop

For optical exposure controlled by aperture or shutter time, one stop doubles or halves incoming light. An ISO stop changes an output setting; it does not add incoming light.

- **Shutter**: 1/500 → 1/250 (1 stop slower) → 2x the light
- **Aperture**: f/5.6 → f/4 (1 stop wider) → 2x the light
- **ISO**: ISO 200 → ISO 400 (1 stop up) → approximately twice output brightness, not twice incoming light

### Sample optimal settings by situation

**Portraits (outdoors, sunny)**
- **Shutter**: 1/250s (freezes natural movement)
- **Aperture**: f/2.8 (blurs the background)
- **ISO**: 100–200 (minimizes noise)

**Landscapes (using a tripod)**
- **Shutter**: 1/30s–several seconds (tripod required)
- **Aperture**: f/8–f/11 (overall sharpness)
- **ISO**: 100 (lowest noise)

**Sports/wildlife photography**
- **Shutter**: 1/1000s or faster (freezes motion)
- **Aperture**: f/4–f/5.6 (moderate background separation)
- **ISO**: 800–3200, depending on the situation

**Night cityscapes (tripod)**
- **Shutter**: 5–30 seconds (captures light trails)
- **Aperture**: f/8 (overall sharpness plus starburst)
- **ISO**: 100–400 (minimizes noise)

## Reading the histogram

A histogram is a graph showing the **brightness distribution** in a photo.

The horizontal axis runs from dark to bright; the vertical axis counts pixels. Values 0–255 describe an 8-bit output, not universal RAW sensor limits.

| Histogram shape | Meaning |
|----------------|------|
| Skewed left | May reflect a dark subject or intentional low-key image |
| Skewed right | May reflect a bright subject or intentional high-key image |
| Evenly spread | Broad tonal range; no guarantee of correct exposure |
| Clipped at right edge | Highlight clipping in the displayed output; inspect RAW data |
| Clipped at left edge | Shadow clipping |

> **Goal**: check important image detail. A light source or intentional silhouette may reasonably clip. Remaining RAW data may allow adjustment, but detail that was never recorded cannot be restored.

## Practice quiz — 5 questions

**Q1.** At unchanged lighting and aperture, what changes when 1/250 s at ISO 400 becomes 1/500 s at ISO 800?
- ① Both incoming light and output brightness double
- ② Incoming light halves; output brightness remains approximately similar
- ③ Neither incoming light nor brightness changes
- ④ ISO adds new photons

**Answer**: ②. Halving exposure time halves incoming light. ISO can compensate output brightness, not replace missing photons.

---

**Q2.** At unchanged focal length, subject distance, and background distance, which produces more background blur: f/1.8 or f/11?
- ① f/1.8  ② f/11  ③ Both the same  ④ Can't tell

**Answer**: ① *(a lower f-number means a shallower depth of field → more background blur)*

---

**Q3.** What matters when raising ISO to brighten an image captured with little incoming light?
- ① The photo gets darker
- ② The colors fade
- ③ Noise from limited light may become apparent; ISO alone cannot solve it
- ④ The focus goes soft

**Answer**: ③. Read noise, amplification, and processing also matter; ISO alone is not a universal causal explanation.

---

**Q4.** Which describes a one-stop change in optical exposure through aperture or shutter time?
- ① A 10x increase in shutter speed
- ② A 2x or 1/2x change in the amount of light
- ③ A 100-point increase in ISO
- ④ A 0.5 increase in aperture

**Answer**: ②

---

**Q5.** What should you first inspect when a histogram clips at the right edge?
- ① The photo is too dark
- ② There's a lot of noise
- ③ Clipping in important image areas and remaining RAW data
- ④ The shutter speed was too fast

**Answer**: ③. Clipping in the displayed output does not prove that all RAW information is lost. Information that was never recorded cannot be restored.
