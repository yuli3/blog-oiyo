## Corporate Income Tax — Book-Tax Differences

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{Taxable income calculation}: \\
&\text{Book income} (\text{GAAP net income}) \\
&\pm \text{Book-tax adjustments} (M-\frac{1}{\text{Schedule}} M-3) \\
&= \text{Taxable income} (\text{Form} 1120, \text{Line} 28) \\
&\text{Permanent differences} (\text{no deferred tax}): \\
&\text{Items included in book income but never in taxable income} \\
&(\text{or vice versa}), \text{AND vice versa} — \text{never reverse} \\
&\text{Examples}: \\
&+ \text{Tax-exempt interest on municipal bonds} (\text{book income}; \text{not taxable}) \\
&+ \text{Dividends Received Deduction} (\text{taxable income deduction}; \\
&\text{reduces book tax expense but is} a \text{permanent benefit}) \\
&− \text{Meals and entertainment} (50\% \text{disallowed permanently under TCJA}) \\
&− \text{Life insurance proceeds on key-person policies} (\text{book income}; \text{tax-exempt}) \\
&− \text{Fines and penalties} (\text{book expense}; \text{not tax-deductible}) \\
&\text{Temporary differences} (\text{create deferred taxes}; \text{reverse over time}): \\
&\text{Examples of taxable temporary differences} ( \to DTL): \\
&\text{Accelerated MACRS depreciation} > \text{straight-line book depreciation} \\
&\text{Installment sales recognized ratably for book}; \text{deferred for tax} \\
&\text{Examples of deductible temporary differences} ( \to DTA): \\
&\text{Warranty expense accrued for book}; \text{deductible when paid for tax} \\
&\text{Allowance for credit losses} (\text{CECL for book}; \text{specific charge-off for tax}) \\
&\text{Net operating loss} (NOL) \text{carryforward}: §172 \text{deduction}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Individual Income Tax Structure

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{Federal Individual Income Tax} (\text{Form} 1040): \\
&\text{Gross Income} (§61 — \text{all income from whatever source}) \\
&− \text{Above-the-line deductions} (\text{adjustments to income}): \\
&\text{Student loan interest}, \text{HSA contributions}, \\
&\text{self-employment tax deduction}, \text{alimony} (\text{pre}-2019) \\
&= \text{Adjusted Gross Income} (AGI) \\
&− \text{Greater of Standard Deduction or Itemized Deductions}: \\
&\text{Standard deduction} (2024): \$14,600 \text{single} / \$29,200 MFJ \\
&\text{Itemized} (\text{Schedule} A): \text{SALT cap} \$10,000; \text{mortgage interest}; \\
&\text{charitable contributions}; \text{medical expenses} >7.5\% \text{of AGI} \\
&− \text{QBI Deduction} (§199A): \text{up to} 20\% \text{of qualified business income} \\
&\text{for pass-through owners} (\frac{\text{subject to income limits}}{W}-2 \text{wage tests}) \\
&= \text{Taxable Income} \\
&\text{Tax Rates} (2024 \text{Married Filing Jointly}): \\
&10\%: \$0 – \$23,200 \\
&12\%: \$23,201 – \$94,300 \\
&22\%: \$94,301 – \$201,050 \\
&24\%: \$201,051 – \$383,900 \\
&32\%: \$383,901 – \$487,450 \\
&35\%: \$487,451 – \$731,200 \\
&37\%: \text{over} \$731,200 \\
&\text{Preferential rates} (\frac{\text{Net Long-Term Capital Gains}}{\text{Qualified Dividends}}): \\
&0\% / 15\% / 20\% \text{depending on taxable income bracket} \\
&\text{Retirement income}: \\
&\frac{\text{Traditional IRA}}{401}(k): \text{pretax contributions}; \text{ordinary income on distributions} \\
&\frac{\text{Roth IRA}}{\text{Roth}} 401(k): \text{after-tax contributions}; \text{qualified distributions tax-free}
\end{aligned}
$$

## US Sales and Use Tax

```
Sales Tax:
Consumption tax; imposed at the state and local level
(no federal sales tax in the US)

Taxable events:
Sale of tangible personal property
Selected services (varies by state)
Digital goods (increasingly taxable; varies by state)

Tax rates:
State rates: 0% (Oregon, Montana, New Hampshire, Delaware, Alaska)
  to 7.25% (California)
Combined state + local: up to ~10%+ in some jurisdictions

Exemptions (common across states):
Groceries and prescription drugs
Resale purchases (for resale → buyer provides resale certificate)
Manufacturing equipment (in many states)
Services (B2B services often exempt; varies widely)

Economic Nexus (South Dakota v. Wayfair, 2018):
Remote sellers with >$100,000 in sales OR >200 transactions
in a state must collect and remit that state's sales tax
Prior physical presence standard (Quill) overruled

Reporting:
Sellers file periodic sales tax returns (monthly/quarterly/annual)
  based on state requirements
Streamlined Sales and Use Tax Agreement (SSUTA):
  voluntary simplification framework adopted by 24 states
```

## Key Concept Cards

**Book-Tax Differences: Permanent vs. Temporary** ★★★★★
: Permanent differences (fines, tax-exempt income) → never reverse → no deferred tax. Temporary differences → reverse → create DTA or DTL.
*Memory hook: permanent = no deferred tax; temporary = deferred tax*

**Sales Tax = No Federal Component** ★★★★★
: The US has NO federal sales tax. Sales and use tax is exclusively state/local. Post-Wayfair: economic nexus means remote sellers must collect where they have significant sales activity.
*Memory hook: sales tax = state only; Wayfair = economic nexus*

**Individual Income Tax Rate Range** ★★★★☆
: Seven brackets, 10%–37% marginal rates (ordinary income). Long-term capital gains taxed at preferential 0%/15%/20% rates.
*Memory hook: 7 brackets, 10–37%; LTCG = 0/15/20*

## Practice Quiz

**Q. A corporation pays a $50,000 fine to the EPA. How is this treated for tax purposes?**
> Fines and penalties paid to a government agency are permanently non-deductible under IRC §162(f). The $50,000 book expense is added back on Schedule M-1 / M-3, increasing taxable income relative to book income. Because this is a permanent difference, there is no deferred tax consequence — only a higher current tax expense than what the GAAP effective tax rate would otherwise reflect.

**Q. What is the difference between sales tax zero-rating and a sales tax exemption?**
> In the US sales tax context: an **exemption** means no tax is collected on the transaction, and the seller incurs no sales tax (e.g., resale, certain groceries). A concept analogous to VAT "zero-rating" does not technically exist in US sales tax, but the comparable concept is "exempt with credit" — effectively, in states with a manufacturing exemption, the seller pays no tax on inputs and does not charge tax on exempt sales, but also does not receive a direct refund mechanism (unlike VAT). Note: This contrasts with the VAT zero-rate / exemption distinction (IFRS context) covered in international tax courses.
