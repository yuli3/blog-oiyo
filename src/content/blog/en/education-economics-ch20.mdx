# Macroeconomics — When Expectations Change, the Same Unemployment Rate Brings Different Inflation

In 1958 Phillips found in British data that wages rose faster when unemployment was low. In the 1960s this relationship was read as a policy menu: accept a little more inflation and unemployment could be lowered permanently. In the 1970s, when high inflation and high unemployment appeared together, the menu collapsed. The missing variable was **expectations**.

## 1. The expectations-augmented Phillips curve

<FormulaBox
  label="Expectations-augmented Phillips curve"
  formula="\pi = \pi^e - \beta\,(u - u^*) + \varepsilon"
  description="π^e is expected inflation, u* the natural rate of unemployment and ε a supply shock. When unemployment equals the natural rate, inflation equals expected inflation. This is the short-run aggregate supply curve of Chapter 8 rewritten in terms of unemployment and inflation."

Let $u^*=4\%$ and $\beta=0.5$. With expected inflation of 2%, inflation is 3% at 2% unemployment and 1% at 6% unemployment. If expected inflation rises to 5%, the whole short-run Phillips curve shifts up by 3 points, so inflation is 6% at 2% unemployment.

In the long run, when expectations equal actual inflation ($\pi=\pi^e$), unemployment returns to $u^*$. **The long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate.** Friedman and Phelps reached this conclusion in the late 1960s, before stagflation arrived.

## 2. Adaptive expectations and accelerating inflation

Suppose people take last year's inflation as this year's expectation ($\pi^e_t=\pi_{t-1}$). The Phillips curve becomes $\pi_t-\pi_{t-1}=-\beta(u_t-u^*)$.

If the government tries to hold unemployment at 3%, inflation rises by 0.5 points every year: 2%, 2.5%, 3%, and so on. To **keep** unemployment below the natural rate, inflation must keep **accelerating**. That is why the natural rate is also called the NAIRU (non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment).

Conversely, bringing inflation down from 5% to 2% (**disinflation**) requires holding unemployment above the natural rate. To get $\Delta\pi=-3$ we need $\sum(u-u^*)=3/0.5=6$: 6 point-years of excess unemployment. If by Okun's law 1 point of unemployment equals 2% of output, the cumulative output loss is 12% of GDP.

## 3. Rational expectations and credibility

**Rational expectations** is the hypothesis that people form expectations using all available information and their understanding of how the economy works, so they do not repeat systematic errors. They look not only at past inflation but also at what the central bank will do.

A strong conclusion follows. If the policy rule is known and prices are flexible, **anticipated** monetary policy is immediately reflected in expectations and changes only prices, not output (the **policy ineffectiveness proposition**). Only unanticipated policy affects output in the short run.

There is also an implication for disinflation. If people believe a central bank's commitment to tighten, expected inflation falls at once and the Phillips curve shifts down, so inflation can be lowered without large unemployment. The sacrifice ratio depends on credibility. Sargent interpreted in this light the rapid ends of several 1920s European hyperinflations through abrupt changes in fiscal and monetary regimes.

## 4. The Lucas critique

Lucas pointed out that macroeconomic relationships estimated from past data (such as the slope of the Phillips curve) change when policy changes, because people's expectations and behaviour respond to the policy regime. The stable Phillips curve of the 1960s breaking down in the 1970s is an example. His argument that policy should be evaluated with models built on preferences and technology that do not change with policy transformed macroeconomic methodology.

<Callout type="warning">
Rational expectations does not mean "people predict the future correctly" but "forecast errors are not systematic" — a hypothesis within a model. If gathering information is costly, people may pay less attention to inflation. And if prices and wages are sticky, even anticipated policy moves output in the short run. Today's central banks announce inflation targets and use forward guidance as ways of using expectations as a policy tool. When expectations are anchored near the target, demand shocks pass through less to prices.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

In an economy with $u^*=5\%$, $\beta=0.4$ and adaptive expectations, how much cumulative excess unemployment is needed to bring inflation from 6% to 2%? $\Delta\pi=-4$, so $\sum(u-u^*)=4/0.4=10$ point-years. Doing it over two years would require unemployment of 10% each year. If credibility were high and expectations fell to 2% at once, the cost would be much smaller.
