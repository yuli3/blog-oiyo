The previous six chapters covered why gambling is a losing game, and why knowing that isn't enough to make people stop.

This chapter covers only one thing: **what you can do right now.**

<HighlightBox>
**In Korea — Gambling Problem Helpline: 1336**

- **24/7, 365 days a year**, and **free**
- For **both the person struggling and their family**
- Online chat counseling (Netline): [netline.kcgp.or.kr](https://netline.kcgp.or.kr) — live chat and message boards, daily 09:00–21:30
- Operated by the Korea Center on Gambling Problems: [www.kcgp.or.kr](https://www.kcgp.or.kr)

**Outside Korea**: search for your country's national problem-gambling helpline — most countries maintain one, and it's typically free and confidential.
</HighlightBox>

## First — counseling is not a report

The most common worry is this: **"If I call, will my parents or school find out?"**

Counseling is not a punishment or reporting process. Confidentiality is protected, and reaching out doesn't lead to any penalty. **You decide** what you share and how much.

If a phone call feels like too much, **starting with online chat** is fine. Writing is often easier than talking.

## If it's you — step by step

**1. Cut off access today.**
Making it **physically harder** to gamble works far better than trying to resist through willpower alone.

- Delete gambling sites and apps, clear bookmarks and links
- Leave related group chats and channels
- Separate your payment methods — unlink the relevant account from debit cards and payment apps

**2. Write down the exact number.**
Not knowing how much you've lost is the most dangerous state to be in. The sunk-cost fallacy (Chapter 6) **gets stronger the vaguer the number is.** Simply writing it down and making it concrete drains the power out of "just a bit more and I'll break even."

**3. Don't carry it alone.**
This matters even more if debt is involved. If illegal predatory lending is part of the picture, **seek counseling before it gets worse** — your options shrink as time passes.

**4. Call your local helpline (in Korea, 1336).**
The moment you're wondering "is this even bad enough to call about?" is exactly the right moment to call.

<Callout>
**If you've tried to quit on your own and failed**, that's not a personal failure — it's **evidence that this problem isn't the kind willpower solves.** As Chapter 6 showed, gambling is designed to exploit the brain's own learning circuits. A designed problem needs designed help.
</Callout>

## If you're worried about a friend

**Do**

- Listen without judgment. "Are you crazy?" ends the conversation and pushes them to hide it further.
- Talk about specific things you've observed. Stick to facts, like "you've been talking about money a lot lately."
- **Look up your local helpline together.** Just dialing the number together lowers the barrier a lot.
- Tell one trusted adult — a teacher, counselor, or parent, whichever feels less intimidating to your friend.

**Don't**

- **Lend them money.** That's not help — it's fuel that extends the gambling.
- Pay off their debt for them. Same reason.
- Promise unconditional secrecy. If debt or predatory lending is involved, an adult needs to be brought in.
- Join in "just to try it." Even with good intentions, the outcome is still bringing someone in.

## If you're a parent or teacher

**Understanding comes before confronting.** Gambling problems grow the more they're hidden, and confrontation pushes people toward hiding.

- Get an **accurate** picture of the amount and any debt involved. Partial disclosure is common.
- Check whether an account or identity was lent out — that creates a **separate legal issue** (see Chapter 5).
- In Korea, the Korea Center on Gambling Problems can connect families to **regional centers** for in-person counseling and treatment programs.
- For students who've left school, a youth counseling center (in Korea, **1388**) is another resource.

## One last thing this series wants to leave you with

This series never said "gambling is bad." Instead, it did the math.

- A fixed share leaks out of every round (Chapter 1)
- That share compounds into a guaranteed loss over time (Chapter 2)
- In a simulation of 2,000 players, **zero** survived 10,000 hands (Chapter 3)
- No betting strategy changes this structure (Chapter 4)
- What teens actually encounter is illegal gambling, where even that math is the optimistic case (Chapter 5)
- And the reason it's still hard to stop isn't willpower — it's design (Chapter 6)

<HighlightBox>
**The only winning move in gambling is not to play.**

If you're already in, the next best move is **to get out as fast as possible.** And doing that with help behind you works far better than doing it alone.

**In Korea: 1336. 24/7. Free.**
</HighlightBox>
