Companies use derivatives to reduce exposure to exchange rates, interest rates and commodity prices. The problem arises when accounting fails to show that economic hedge. A derivative's fair value changes go to profit or loss, but if the hedged item stays at cost or has not yet been recognized, only one side of profit or loss moves. **Hedge accounting** under K-IFRS 1109 is an optional exception that removes this mismatch. The basics of foreign currency translation were covered in [chapter 6](/en/academy-advanced-accounting-ch6/).

## 1. Definition and measurement of derivatives

A derivative is a financial instrument with all three of the following features.

1. Its value changes in response to an underlying variable (interest rate, exchange rate, price index and so on).
2. It requires no or only a very small initial net investment.
3. It is settled at a future date.

Forwards, futures, options and swaps are typical. As a rule, derivatives are **measured at fair value with changes in profit or loss**. For an **embedded derivative** contained in another contract, if the host is a financial asset the whole contract is classified without separation; if the host is not a financial asset and the economic characteristics are not closely related, the derivative is separated and accounted for as a derivative.

## 2. Qualifying criteria for hedge accounting

Hedge accounting is applied only when all of the following criteria are met, and **prospectively from the date of designation**.

- The relationship consists of eligible hedging instruments and eligible hedged items.
- The risk management objective and strategy and the hedging relationship are **formally designated and documented**.
- The hedge effectiveness requirements are met: there is an **economic relationship** between the hedged item and the instrument, the **effect of credit risk** does not dominate the value changes, and the **hedge ratio** equals the ratio actually used for risk management.

The 80–125% effectiveness test of the former standard (IAS 39) was removed in IFRS 9. Instead, any ineffective portion is recognized in profit or loss.

## 4. Cash flow hedge: a forecast purchase

Company GG (functional currency won) is highly likely to buy USD 100,000 of raw materials in three months, as of 1 November 20X1. To protect itself from a rising exchange rate, on the same day it buys a three-month currency forward (buying dollars) at a forward rate of ₩1,300 and designates it as a cash flow hedge. On 31 December the one-month forward rate is ₩1,330, and on 31 January 20X2 the spot rate is ₩1,340. Ignore discounting.

When the raw materials are bought at the spot rate on 31 January, GG pays ₩134 million. Deducting the ₩4 million reserve from the cost of the raw materials (a **basis adjustment**) brings them to ₩130 million, equal to the cost locked in at the forward rate. For a forecast transaction that results in a non-financial asset, the reserve must be included directly in the asset's cost like this. The hedge effect then reaches profit or loss as the raw materials pass into cost of sales.

## 5. Fair value hedge: fixed-rate borrowings

Company HH has ₩1 billion of borrowings at a fixed 5% and, to hedge their interest rate risk (the fair value rises when market rates fall), enters into an interest rate swap receiving fixed and paying floating, designated as a fair value hedge. If market rates fall so that the fair value of the borrowings rises by ₩12 million and the swap's fair value rises by ₩11.5 million, HH increases the carrying amount of the borrowings by ₩12 million, recognizing a loss, and recognizes a ₩11.5 million gain on the swap. The ₩500,000 difference is the **ineffective portion** left in profit or loss. In effect, the borrowings are reported like a floating-rate liability.

<Callout type="warning">
Hedge accounting is optional and documentation comes first. A hedge cannot be designated after the fact, so for an undocumented economic hedge only the derivative's gains and losses show up in profit or loss, often in large amounts. Shipbuilders and shipping companies that use large currency forwards often report huge derivative losses in periods of sharp currency moves while their actual cash losses are small. Conversely, if the hedge is larger than the actual exposure, it becomes a speculative position that leads to real losses. Compare the notional amounts in the derivative notes with the size of the hedged exposure.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

In section 4, if GG had not applied hedge accounting, what would profit or loss in 20X1 and 20X2 and the cost of the raw materials be? Conversely, if the spot rate on 31 January had fallen to ₩1,280, what would the raw materials cost with hedge accounting?

Without hedge accounting the currency forward is at FVPL. Gains of ₩3 million in 20X1 and ₩1 million in 20X2 are recognized in profit or loss, and the raw materials cost ₩134 million at the spot rate. In the periods when the raw materials are sold, cost of sales is ₩4 million higher, so the timing of profit or loss is mismatched. If the spot rate falls to ₩1,280, the forward's fair value is $\text{USD }100{,}000 \times (1{,}280 - 1{,}300) = -200$ (₩10,000), i.e. −₩2 million. The reserve is −₩2 million and the raw materials cost $12{,}800 + 200 = 13{,}000$ (₩10,000), again fixed at the forward rate of ₩1,300. That is the price of giving up the benefit of a favourable rate move.
