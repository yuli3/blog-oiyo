# Macroeconomics — Which Policy Is Stronger Depends on the Slopes, Not the School

[Chapter 4](/en/education-economics-ch14/) shifted IS and LM to see the signs of policy effects. This chapter looks at the slopes that determine their **size**. The long-running debate between "fiscal policy is powerful" (Keynesians) and "only monetary policy moves output" (monetarists) was in effect an empirical debate about how sensitive money demand and investment are to the interest rate.

## 1. Writing the multipliers in terms of parameters

Write IS as $Y=\alpha-\beta r+\gamma G$ and LM as $M/P=\lambda Y-\mu r$. $\beta$ is the sensitivity of spending to the interest rate (including the multiplier), $\gamma$ the government spending multiplier, $\lambda$ the income sensitivity of money demand and $\mu$ its interest sensitivity. Substituting $r=(\lambda Y-M/P)/\mu$ from LM into IS:

<FormulaBox
  label="Policy multipliers in IS-LM"
  formula="\frac{dY}{dG}=\frac{\gamma}{1+\beta\lambda/\mu},\qquad \frac{dY}{d(M/P)}=\frac{\beta/\mu}{1+\beta\lambda/\mu}"
  description="The term βλ/μ in the denominator is the size of crowding out: as output rises, money demand rises by λ, the interest rate rises at the rate 1/μ, and spending falls by β."
/>

With the example of Chapter 4 ($\beta=10$, $\gamma=5$, $\lambda=0.5$, $\mu=20$), the fiscal multiplier is $5/1.25=4$ and the real-balance multiplier $0.5/1.25=0.4$. This matches Chapter 4, where 10 trillion won of G raised output by 40 trillion and 20 of real balances raised it by 8 trillion.

## 2. Three regions by the interest sensitivity of money demand

Changing only $\mu$ reverses the relative strength of the two policies.

**Liquidity trap** ($\mu\to\infty$): when interest rates are so low that people are willing to hold unlimited money instead of bonds, increasing the money supply cannot push rates lower. Monetary policy cannot move output, and fiscal policy causes no crowding out because rates do not rise. The Great Depression of the 1930s and the zero-interest-rate period after 2008 are often seen as close to this situation.

**Classical region** ($\mu\to 0$): if money demand does not respond to the interest rate, $M/P=\lambda Y$ ties output to real balances. A fiscal expansion only raises the interest rate and crowds out an equal amount of private spending (full crowding out). Monetary expansion becomes the only way to move output — the same structure as the quantity theory of money in [Chapter 3](/en/education-economics-ch13/).

The interest sensitivity of investment $\beta$ plays the same role. If $\beta\to 0$, spending does not change when rates change, so there is no crowding out (fiscal policy is strong), and monetary policy cannot raise spending even if it lowers rates (monetary policy is powerless).

## 3. The policy mix

<LectureTable
  title="Policy combinations by situation"
  headers={["Situation", "Primary instrument", "Reason"]}
  rows={[
    ["Recession + rates at the floor", "Fiscal expansion", "The interest-rate channel of monetary policy is blocked"],
    ["Recession + room to cut rates", "Monetary easing (possibly with fiscal support)", "Raises investment without crowding out"],
    ["Overheating + fiscal deficit", "Fiscal tightening + monetary easing", "Keeps output while lowering rates to shift the composition towards investment"],
    ["Supply shock (prices up, output down)", "IS-LM alone is not enough", "Prices move, so the AD-AS model of Chapter 8 is needed"],
  ]}
/>

Combining fiscal tightening with monetary easing moves IS left and LM right, lowering interest rates while keeping output roughly unchanged. At the same output the share of private investment rises relative to government spending, which favours capital accumulation in the long run.

<Callout type="warning">
Do not choose answers by school labels. What is needed is an empirical judgement about how responsive money demand and investment are to interest rates. And under interest-rate targeting, whether the central bank raises its rate in response to a fiscal expansion effectively determines the slope of LM. Even in a liquidity trap, a central bank can have an effect by committing to a future path of rates (forward guidance) or by buying long-term bonds to move long-term rates and expectations.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

With $\beta=10$, $\gamma=5$, $\lambda=0.5$ and $\mu=10$, what are the output effects of 10 trillion won of government spending and of 20 of real balances? The denominator is $1+10\times 0.5/10=1.5$. The fiscal effect is $5/1.5\times 10\approx 33.3$ trillion won and the monetary effect $(10/10)/1.5\times 20\approx 13.3$ trillion won. As $\mu$ falls from 20 to 10, the fiscal effect shrinks (40 → 33.3) and the monetary effect grows (8 → 13.3).
