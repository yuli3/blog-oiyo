# Microeconomics — A Monopolist Chooses the Quantity; Demand Sets the Price

A competitive firm takes the price as given. A monopolist faces the whole market demand, so selling one more unit means lowering the price of the units it was already selling. That is why marginal revenue is below price, and this gap drives every result about monopoly.

## 1. Marginal revenue is below price

With demand $P=120-Q$, total revenue is $TR=120Q-Q^2$ and marginal revenue is $MR=120-2Q$. For linear demand, MR is a straight line with the same intercept and twice the slope.

If cutting the price from 81 to 80 won raises sales from 39 to 40, the firm earns 80 won on the extra unit but loses 1 won on each of the 39 existing units, 39 won in total. Marginal revenue is 41 won — far below the price of 80 won.

## 2. Profit maximisation and deadweight loss

With a marginal cost of 40 won, $120-2Q=40$ gives $Q_m=40$, and the price read off the demand curve is $P_m=80$ won. A competitive market with the same demand and cost would have $P=MC=40$ won and $Q_c=80$.

Of the 2,400 fall in consumer surplus, 1,600 is **transferred** to the monopolist and 800 **disappears** altogether. From an efficiency standpoint, the cost of monopoly is the lost 800, not the transfer; the transfer is a question of distribution.

## 3. Measuring market power: the Lerner index

Substituting $MR=P(1-1/|\varepsilon|)$ from [Chapter 3](/en/education-economics-ch3/) into $MR=MC$ gives a pricing rule.

<FormulaBox
  label="Lerner index and the inverse-elasticity rule"
  formula="L=\frac{P-MC}{P}=\frac{1}{|\varepsilon|}"
  description="At the monopoly equilibrium (P = 80, Q = 40), |ε| = 80/40 = 2, so L = 0.5 = (80−40)/80. The more elastic demand is, the closer the price must stay to marginal cost."
/>

Two conclusions follow. A monopolist produces only where $|\varepsilon|>1$, on the elastic part of demand; on the inelastic part, raising the price increases revenue and lowers cost. And if close substitutes exist, even a monopolist cannot raise the price much.


### A. How much does a monopoly price rise when cost rises?

In a competitive market, a rise in marginal cost is shared out according to elasticities ([chapter 3](/en/education-economics-ch3/)). Under monopoly, the **shape** of the demand curve sets the pass-through. With linear demand $P=a-Q$, $MR=a-2Q=MC$ gives $P_m=(a+MC)/2$: a 1-won rise in marginal cost raises price by 0.5 won. With constant-elasticity demand $Q=AP^{-\varepsilon}$, the Lerner rule itself becomes the pricing formula.

<FormulaBox
  label="Cost pass-through under monopoly"
  formula="\text{Linear: }\frac{dP_m}{dMC}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad \text{Constant elasticity: }P_m=\frac{|\varepsilon|}{|\varepsilon|-1}\,MC\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{dP_m}{dMC}=\frac{|\varepsilon|}{|\varepsilon|-1}>1"
  description="With |ε|=2, price is set at twice marginal cost, so a 1-won rise in cost raises price by 2 won. Under monopoly, taxes or cost increases can be passed on by more than 100%."
/>

In the example above, if marginal cost rises from 40 to 60, $120-2Q=60$ gives $Q=30$ and $P=90$. Cost rose by 20 but price by only 10 — exactly the linear pass-through of 1/2.

## 4. Price discrimination

Charging different consumers different prices requires two things: the firm must be able to tell groups apart, and those who buy cheaply must not be able to resell to those who would pay more.

<LectureTable
  title="Three types of price discrimination"
  headers={["Type", "Method", "Result"]}
  rows={[
    ["First degree (perfect)", "Each consumer pays their willingness to pay", "Quantity at the competitive level; the firm captures all surplus"],
    ["Second degree", "Consumers self-select among quantity or version prices", "Bulk discounts, economy/business class"],
    ["Third degree", "Different prices for observable groups", "Student discounts, country pricing"],
  ]}
/>

An example of third-degree discrimination: group A has demand $P=100-Q$, group B has $P=60-Q$, and $MC=20$ won. Apply $MR=MC$ in each group.

- A: $100-2Q=20$ → $Q=40$, $P=60$ won. Elasticity at this point $|\varepsilon|=60/40=1.5$
- B: $60-2Q=20$ → $Q=20$, $P=40$ won. Elasticity at this point $|\varepsilon|=40/20=2$

The more elastic group B gets the lower price: the Lerner rule applied group by group.

First-degree discrimination eliminates deadweight loss, because the firm sells to everyone whose willingness to pay is at least marginal cost. Efficiency matches competition, but consumer surplus is zero — another reminder that efficiency and distribution are different questions.


### A. Does third-degree price discrimination raise welfare?

What if the firm had to charge the two groups a single price? Below 60, total demand is $Q=(100-P)+(60-P)=160-2P$, i.e. $P=80-Q/2$, and $MR=80-Q=20$ gives $Q=60$ and $P=50$ (both groups buy: A 50 units, B 10).

<LectureTable
  title="Single price versus third-degree discrimination (MC=20)"
  headers={["", "Single price 50", "Discrimination (A 60, B 40)"]}
  rows={[
    ["Total output", "60 (A 50, B 10)", "60 (A 40, B 20)"],
    ["Consumer surplus", "1,250 + 50 = 1,300", "800 + 200 = 1,000"],
    ["Profit", "30 × 60 = 1,800", "40×40 + 20×20 = 2,000"],
    ["Total surplus", "3,100", "3,000"],
  ]}
/>

Total output is 60 either way, yet total surplus is 100 lower under discrimination. Splitting the same quantity between the groups at **different prices** means an A consumer willing to pay 60 goes without while a B consumer willing to pay only 40 buys — a misallocation. In general, for third-degree discrimination to raise welfare, **total output must rise** (a necessary condition). With linear demands and every group already buying, output does not change, so discrimination lowers welfare. Conversely, if discrimination opens up a group that would not be served at all under a single price (a low-income country, say), output rises and welfare can rise too.
## 5. Natural monopoly and regulation

If average cost falls over the whole range of market demand, supply by a single firm is cheapest ([Chapter 5](/en/education-economics-ch5/)). Each way of regulating such a **natural monopoly** has a cost.

- **Marginal-cost pricing** ($P=MC$): efficient, but where average cost is falling $MC<AC$, so the firm makes a loss and needs a subsidy.
- **Average-cost pricing** ($P=AC$): no loss, but $P>MC$ leaves a deadweight loss and weakens incentives to cut costs.
- **Two-part tariff**: a fixed fee covers fixed costs and the usage charge equals marginal cost. Some consumers may not sign up because of the fee.

<Callout type="warning">
Being the only firm does not guarantee a monopoly price. If entry and exit are free and costless (a contestable market), the mere threat of entry keeps the price close to average cost. Conversely, several firms that collude can produce the monopoly outcome (Chapter 8). Market power is measured by the ability to keep price above marginal cost, not by the number of firms.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

**Problem 1.** For a monopolist facing demand $P=120-Q$ with $MC=20$, find output, price, deadweight loss and the Lerner index.

**Solution.** $120-2Q=20$ gives $Q=50$ and $P=70$. Competitive output solves $120-Q=20$, i.e. 100, so the deadweight loss is $\tfrac{1}{2}\times(70-20)\times(100-50)=1{,}250$. The Lerner index is $(70-20)/70\approx0.71$, the inverse of the elasticity at that point, $70/50=1.4$.

**Problem 2.** A drug company is the sole supplier facing demand with a constant price elasticity of $|\varepsilon|=3$. If marginal cost rises from 10,000 to 12,000 won, how does the price change?

**Solution.** With constant-elasticity demand, $P_m=\frac{|\varepsilon|}{|\varepsilon|-1}MC=1.5\,MC$. Price rises from 15,000 to 18,000 won, an increase of 3,000 — more than the 2,000 cost increase, a 150% pass-through. The Lerner index is $1/3$ in both cases.
