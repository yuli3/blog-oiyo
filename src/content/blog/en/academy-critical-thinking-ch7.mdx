## The basic structure of a good argument

A good argument isn't a piece of writing that shouts its conclusion. It's clear about what it's claiming, it has evidence to back that claim, and it deals honestly with anticipated counterarguments. Readers aren't persuaded by the conclusion alone — they're persuaded by the path that leads to it.

The basic structure is claim, evidence, explanation, counterargument, and rebuttal. This order isn't always fixed, but it's a useful checklist when preparing writing or a presentation to make sure you haven't skipped a piece.

## An argument-building table

| Element | Role | Sample question |
|------|------|-----------|
| Claim | States the conclusion | What are you trying to say? |
| Evidence | Reasons and data | Why do you see it that way? |
| Explanation | Links evidence to claim | How does the material support the conclusion? |
| Counterargument | Anticipated objection | What could be a problem here? |
| Rebuttal | Response to the counterargument | Why does the claim still hold up? |

If you list evidence without explanation, the reader has to make the connection themselves. Persuasiveness comes from the clarity of the connection, not the sheer amount of material.

## Don't be afraid of counterarguments

A counterargument isn't an enemy weakening your claim — it's a tool that makes your claim more precise. Good writing doesn't hide the points that are unfavorable to its own case. Instead, it explains why that counterargument isn't decisive, or under what conditions it would actually apply.

For example, the claim "online classes increase accessibility" invites the counterargument that they reduce focus. A rebuttal might point to conditions like live Q&A, short modular units, and feedback on learning records.

## A practical sentence template

"I hold that A. The reason is B. B supports A because of C. Of course, there's a concern that D. But given condition E, A remains persuasive." This template alone can organize the structure of a great deal of writing.

## Key takeaways

- A good argument has a claim, evidence, explanation, counterargument, and rebuttal.
- Explaining the link between evidence and claim is what creates persuasiveness.
- A counterargument can be a tool that sharpens a claim rather than weakening it.
- In practice, a simple sentence template is a good way to set the structure of an argument first.
