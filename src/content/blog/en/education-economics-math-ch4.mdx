# Mathematics for Economics — Once Time Is Added, a Static Optimum Becomes a Differential Equation

Comparative statics jumps from one point to another. Dynamics asks whether the path between them is actually followed and whether the system returns to its steady state. Macroeconomic growth theory uses this grammar.

## 1. Solutions and stability of first-order linear equations

If $\dot x = -a(x-x^*)$ with $a>0$, then $x(t)=x^*+(x_0-x^*)e^{-at}$, which goes to $x^*$ as $t\to\infty$. If $a<0$, it diverges. The sign decides stability.

In the Solow model, capital per worker $k$ follows $\dot k = s f(k) - (\delta+n)k$, and in the steady state $s f(k^*)=(\delta+n)k^*$. With $f(k)=\sqrt k$, $s=0.2$ and $\delta+n=0.1$, $0.2\sqrt k=0.1 k$ gives $\sqrt k=2$ and $k^*=4$. If $k_0=1$, saving outpaces depreciation and population dilution and $k$ rises; if $k_0=9$, it falls.

The golden rule is $f'(k_{gold})=\delta+n$. With the numbers above, $f'=1/(2\sqrt k)=0.1$ gives $\sqrt k=5$ and $k=25$. Since $k^*=4$ is below the golden rule, raising the saving rate can increase steady-state consumption. Conversely, with oversaving, saving must fall for steady-state consumption to rise.

## 2. A phase diagram is arrows, not coefficients

The steady state is the point that separates the range where $\dot k>0$ from the range where $\dot k<0$. A nice picture does not mean the coefficients have been read. Local stability requires the bracket in the linearization $\dot{\tilde k}=[sf'(k^*)-(\delta+n)]\tilde k$ to be negative. With diminishing returns, $f'(k^*)<f(k^*)/k^*$, so $sf'(k^*)<\delta+n$ holds automatically. In the example above, $sf'(4)=0.2×0.25=0.05<0.1$.

<Callout type="warning">
Stability in continuous time and in discrete time do not share the same sign condition. $x_{t+1}=bx_t$ requires $|b|<1$. $b=-1.2$ oscillates and diverges. Do not write a macro model quarterly and carry over continuous-time intuition unchanged.
</Callout>

Optimal control (the Hamiltonian) is the next threshold after this chapter. The Euler equation for consumption and saving, $u'(c_t)=\beta(1+r)u'(c_{t+1})$, is its first-order condition. It sits on a different layer from the introductory multiplier.

## Check your understanding

If the saving rate rises from 20% to 30%, what is the new $k^*$? $0.3\sqrt k=0.1k$ gives $\sqrt k=3$ and $k^*=9$. Consumption per worker $c=(1-s)f(k)$ is $0.8×2=1.6$ at $k=4$, drops to $0.7×2=1.4$ the moment the saving rate rises, and climbs to $0.7×3=2.1$ as $k$ approaches 9. Do not write "higher saving is always a gain" from the long-run level alone. The generations on the transition path pay the cost.
