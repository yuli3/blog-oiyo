Dokkaebi are among the most familiar supernatural beings in Korean folk tales. They are sometimes imagined simply as horned monsters, but the dokkaebi of traditional tales are not the same beings as Japanese oni. They are believed to dwell in objects such as old brooms, fire pokers, and pestles, and they are famous for scenes in which they wrestle people on night roads or produce wealth with a dokkaebi club. Dokkaebi are not merely frightening evil spirits; they are playful, changeable powers of the threshold.

## Core Myth — Representative Episodes

One scene that often appears in dokkaebi stories is wrestling with a dokkaebi encountered at night. A person defeats the dokkaebi by noticing that one of its legs is weak, or by using a change in time such as a rooster's crow. This story shows the structure of a folk tale that handles the fear of an unfamiliar night through wit and observation.

The dokkaebi club is another representative symbol. It is known through stories in which wealth appears with phrases like "Gold, come out, thump," but depending on the tradition, a dokkaebi's treasure can also bring disaster to a greedy person. Dokkaebi are beings that give fortune while also testing greed. Wealth gained too easily can turn into ridicule or punishment at any moment.

It is also important that dokkaebi are said to arise from objects. Long-used objects, abandoned tools, and things that contain the energy of fire and iron become dokkaebi. This reflects an imagination in which even human-made objects accumulate memory and power. Their appearance and personality differ by tradition, so they should be read as fluid beings of folk tales rather than fixed into a single image.

## Reading as an Archetype — Jungian Perspective

From Jung's perspective, the dokkaebi is a **trickster** archetype. He unsettles rules, reveals people's desires and blind spots, and sometimes gives unexpected good fortune. In modern terms, he resembles unpredictable opportunities, incidents that begin like jokes, and creative ideas that emerge from old tools.

The dokkaebi archetype helps us avoid rejecting energy outside of order. Yet that power always contains both mischief and danger. For this reason, the dokkaebi symbolizes "something fun but requiring caution" and "something that gives fortune while reflecting greed." When humans become too solemn, he shakes order through laughter.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q. Is a dokkaebi a ghost?**

A. Dokkaebi are sometimes spoken of in ways that overlap with ghosts, but in traditional tales they are often distinguished as playful and powerful beings born from old objects.

**Q. Are dokkaebi evil beings?**

A. It is difficult to say that they are necessarily evil. They frighten and test people, but they also give fortune, and their character varies by tradition.
