# Duration and Convexity: Measuring the Sensitivity

If Chapter 1 taught us that bond prices move when interest rates change, Chapter 2 teaches us **"Under which assumptions can we approximate the change?"** To manage a bond portfolio, we need tools that quantify this risk. These tools are <mark><strong>Duration</strong></mark> and <mark><strong>Convexity</strong></mark>.

---

## 1. What is Duration? (The First Derivative)

Duration is not just time; it is a measure of a bond's price sensitivity to interest rate changes.

The basic model assumes fixed contractual cash flows. Macaulay weights are present-value shares, not nominal payment shares. With a quoted annual yield y compounded m times per year, Dmod = DMac/(1+y/m); align payment frequency and yield units.

## 3. Beyond the Line: Convexity (The Second Derivative)

Duration assumes that the relationship between price and yield is a straight line. But in reality, it is a curve. <mark><strong>Convexity</strong></mark> measures this curvature.

Positive convexity of an option-free fixed-rate bond adds a positive second-order adjustment to the duration-only estimate. Comparisons require duration and other conditions to be held equal; this does not rank the performance of arbitrary bonds. Convexity is an approximation coefficient, not insurance against losses.


### A. Check the approximation against Chapter 1

Use the same option-free bond with annual payments: P(y)=50/(1+y)+1050/(1+y)². At y=0.10, P≈913.22314. Differentiating gives Dmod=−P′(y)/P≈1.77293 and C=P″(y)/P≈4.79414. Convexity must use the same yield and compounding convention.

<FormulaBox formula="\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{mod}\Delta y + \frac{1}{2}C(\Delta y)^2" />

| Yield change | Duration only | With convexity | Exact repricing |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| −100bp (Δy = −0.01) | +1.77293% | +1.79690% | +1.79720% |
| +100bp (Δy = +0.01) | −1.77293% | −1.74896% | −1.74925% |

Both estimates are Taylor approximations around the current valuation point. Adding convexity does not make them exact. Recheck errors for larger shocks or changed cash flows.

<Callout type="warning">
  **Negative Convexity**: Some bonds, like Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS) or Callable Bonds, can
  have "Negative Convexity." This means their price may not rise as much as expected when rates fall
  because the borrower might repay the debt early.
</Callout>

## 4. Wrapping Up: Mastering Risk

Duration neutrality offsets first-order sensitivity to a specified rate shock; it does not eliminate risk. Nonparallel curve shifts, credit spreads, option exercise and changed cash flows can still cause losses. Key rate duration separates maturity-specific shocks; option-bearing bonds require cash-flow revaluation under benchmark curve scenarios.
