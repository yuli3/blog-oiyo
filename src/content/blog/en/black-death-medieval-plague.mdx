## The Ships of Death

In October 1347, twelve Genoese trading ships docked at the Sicilian port of Messina. Most of the sailors were dead. Those still alive were covered in dark, pus-filled boils. Harbor authorities immediately sent the ships away — but it was too late.

The **Black Death** had arrived in Europe.

---

## Cause and Path

The Black Death was caused by **Yersinia pestis** bacteria, transmitted primarily by fleas on rats.

The bacterium originated in the Central Asian steppe and traveled west along Mongol trade routes (the Silk Road). In 1346, Mongol forces besieging the Crimean trading city of Caffa (modern Feodosiya) reportedly catapulted plague-infected corpses over the city walls. Fleeing merchants carried the disease to Mediterranean ports. Within four years, it had covered all of Europe.

<Callout type="tip">
**Three forms**: The disease took three manifestations. **Bubonic plague** (swollen lymph nodes; 30–60% fatal), **pneumonic plague** (infects the lungs; 90%+ fatal, spread person-to-person by cough), and **septicemic plague** (bloodstream infection; nearly 100% fatal). The 14th-century epidemic involved all three.
</Callout>

## The Scale of Death

Between 1347 and 1353, roughly **25 million people** died across Europe — approximately one-third of the population. Some regions lost 50–60%.

Florence lost more than half its people. In Avignon, Pope Clement VI sat between two large fires — burning continuously — believing the smoke would protect him from infection.

---

## Medieval Responses

People had no concept of germ theory. Dominant explanations included:

- **Divine punishment**: God's wrath for humanity's sins
- **Miasma theory**: Disease spread by "bad air" from rotting matter
- **Jewish conspiracy**: Widespread, baseless accusations that Jews had poisoned wells led to horrific pogroms across Europe

The **Flagellant movement** spread across Europe — groups of men flogging themselves publicly in processions, believing self-inflicted suffering would appease God.

---

## How the Plague Transformed Europe

The Black Death's long-term consequences were paradoxically transformative.

**Labor shortage and the end of serfdom**: With a third of the workforce dead, surviving peasants could negotiate for better wages and conditions. The feudal system's foundations cracked. Serfs began escaping to cities; lords had to offer incentives to keep workers. The old social order began dissolving.

**Church authority weakened**: Clergy died alongside everyone else; prayer didn't stop the plague. Faith in the Church's power to protect — and explain — the world was shaken.

**Art transformed**: The "Dance of Death" (*Danse Macabre*) genre emerged — skeletal figures leading people of all social classes to their graves. Death became an obsessive theme in European art.

---

## The Black Death Today

Yersinia pestis has never disappeared. Hundreds of cases are reported globally each year — mostly in Central Asia, Africa, and the American West. Modern antibiotics treat it effectively. The difference between the 14th century and today is knowledge and medicine — representing the most significant factor in millions of lives.
