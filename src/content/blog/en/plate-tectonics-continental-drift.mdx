## Once Considered Lunacy

In 1912, German meteorologist Alfred Wegener proposed that the continents had once been joined and have since drifted apart. His evidence: the coastlines of South America and Africa fit together like puzzle pieces, and the same fossil species appeared on both continents.

The scientific establishment rejected him. He couldn't explain the mechanism — what force could move whole continents? Wegener died in 1930 on a Greenland expedition, dismissed by peers.

Decades later, seafloor exploration changed everything.

---

## Seafloor Spreading: The Smoking Gun

In the 1950s–60s, oceanographic surveys discovered mid-ocean ridges — vast underwater mountain chains splitting ocean floors. The **Mid-Atlantic Ridge** runs the length of the Atlantic, north to south.

The crucial finding: rock gets older the farther you go from a ridge. New oceanic crust was continuously being created at ridges and spreading outward. Wegener's missing mechanism was revealed: **seafloor spreading**.

<Callout type="tip">
**Magnetic stripes**: Oceanic rock records the direction of Earth's magnetic field when it formed. The seafloor shows symmetric stripes of alternating magnetic polarity on both sides of ridges — like a tape recording of Earth's magnetic reversals, and definitive proof of spreading.
</Callout>

## The Core of Plate Tectonics

Earth's lithosphere (crust + upper mantle) is divided into about 15 major **tectonic plates**, floating on the more fluid asthenosphere. Driven by mantle convection, they move a few centimeters per year.

### Plate Boundary Types

**Divergent**: Plates move apart. New crust forms. The Atlantic Ocean widens by 2–3 cm per year.

**Convergent**: Plates collide. The denser plate subducts (dives) under the other. This creates the Himalayas (two continents colliding), the Andes (ocean floor diving under South America), and Japan's volcanic arc.

**Transform**: Plates slide horizontally past each other. California's **San Andreas Fault** is the classic example — source of frequent major earthquakes.

---

## How the Himalayas Formed

About 50 million years ago, the Indian subcontinent collided with Eurasia. Neither continental crust was dense enough to subduct, so both crumpled upward — creating the Himalayas and the Tibetan Plateau. India continues pushing north today; the Himalayas rise a few millimeters per year.

---

## Past and Future Earth

**Pangaea**: Around 335 million years ago, all continents were joined in a single supercontinent. They split and drifted to their current positions.

**The future**: At current rates, the Atlantic keeps widening, the Pacific shrinking. In about 250 million years, continents may merge again into a new supercontinent — sometimes called **Amasia**.

---

## The Power of Unification

Before plate tectonics, earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain ranges, and ocean distribution seemed unrelated. Plate tectonics explains all of them with one coherent framework. Wegener's ridiculed idea transformed Earth science — a reminder that paradigm shifts often begin with ideas considered absurd.
