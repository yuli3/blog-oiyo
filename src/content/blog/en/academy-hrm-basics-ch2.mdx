## 1. Job Analysis

**Definition**

- A systematic investigation of job content, requirements, and characteristics.

**Purpose**

- Establish hiring and selection criteria
- Determine training and development content
- Develop performance appraisal standards
- Design the compensation structure

**Methods**

- **Observation**: Direct observation of work being performed
- **Interview**: Conversations with job incumbents and supervisors
- **Questionnaire**: Standardized surveys (e.g., Position Analysis Questionnaire)
- **Critical Incident**: Recording effective and ineffective behaviors

---

## 2. Job Description and Job Specification

**Job Description**

- Describes the job itself
- Content, duties, responsibilities, authority, working conditions
- **Answers**: What is done?

**Job Specification**

- Describes the human requirements to perform the job
- Education, experience, skills, and abilities required
- **Answers**: Who can do it?

**Position vs Job vs Job Family**

- **Position**: A specific role held by one individual
- **Job**: A cluster of similar tasks and duties
- **Job Family**: A group of related jobs

## 3. Job Design Theories

**Scientific Management (Taylor):**

Job simplification and standardization

An emphasis on efficiency through defined tasks and methods

- **Risks to investigate**: repetitive work, loss of discretion and turnover; these are possible outcomes, not inevitable effects of standardization.

**Job Enlargement:**

**Horizontal expansion**: add similar tasks at the same level

May increase variety, but adding tasks can also increase workload without improving discretion.

**Job Enrichment:**

**Vertical expansion**: increase autonomy and responsibility

Based on Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory

**Job Characteristics Model (Hackman & Oldham):**

Skill Variety · Task Identity · Task Significance

Autonomy · Feedback

- MPS (Motivating Potential Score)

## 4. Job Evaluation

**Purpose**

- Determine the relative value of jobs within an organization
- Foundation for designing the pay structure

**Methods**

- **Ranking Method**: Order all jobs from highest to lowest (simple)
- **Classification**: Fit jobs into pre-defined grades or classes
- **Factor Comparison**: Compare jobs factor-by-factor against benchmark jobs
- **Point-Factor**: Assign points to defined compensable factors and combine them using documented weights. Quantification alone does not establish accuracy or fairness.

---

## 5. Distinctions for Applied Review

The description concerns the work. The specification concerns justified requirements for performing it; it is not a portrait of the current incumbent.

Enrichment changes discretion or responsibility; enlargement changes the range of tasks. Check actual decision rights and resources, not the new job title.

### A Numerical Score Still Contains Judgments

Point-factor evaluation makes assumptions inspectable; it does not remove them. Factors, levels and weights require evidence about the work. The ILO guide warns that weighting can introduce bias even after careful earlier analysis. Its sample weights are illustrations, not a universal recommended scheme. [ILO, *Promoting Equity*, chapter 7](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/wcmsp5/groups/public/%40ed_norm/%40declaration/documents/publication/wcms_122372.pdf).

Consider two fictional jobs rated from 1 to 5 on knowledge, responsibility and working conditions. These three factors simplify the exercise; they are not a complete pay-equity instrument.

| Job | Hypothetical ratings: knowledge / responsibility / conditions |
|---|---|
| A | 4 / 2 / 2 |
| B | 2 / 4 / 3 |

With weights of 60%, 30% and 10%, A scores 3.2 and B scores 2.7. With weights of 30%, 60% and 10%, A scores 2.6 and B scores 3.3. The ranking reverses without any change in the jobs. Apply each alternative consistently to both jobs: changing the rule for one job would prevent a meaningful comparison.

Document why a factor matters, what distinguishes adjacent levels and which evidence supports each rating. Check whether demands are omitted or counted twice. Review disagreements and provide a way to challenge missing evidence. An explicit score can support consistent decisions, but it is neither proof of fairness nor a direct salary entitlement. Market pay, individual performance and the employer's budget are separate questions.

## 6. Practice Quiz

**Q. What is the difference between job enrichment and job enlargement?**
**Worked answer.** Enlargement adds tasks at a similar level of responsibility; enrichment adds discretion or responsibility for planning and control. A fictional operator moving from two assembly steps to four may experience more variety, but also a heavier workload. An operator who can plan the sequence and resolve defined quality issues has more discretion, provided the authority, training and resources are real. Neither change guarantees motivation. Inspect workload, capability, employee preferences, safety constraints and feedback before deciding whether the redesign helped. Herzberg's distinction is a theoretical interpretation, not evidence that only one intervention can motivate every employee.

**Q. Why is job evaluation important for designing a pay structure?**
**Worked answer.** Job evaluation provides a structured comparison of the demands of jobs, not the merit of their incumbents. It can inform internal pay relationships, while external market comparisons require comparable roles and pay definitions. A score does not guarantee equitable pay. In the example above, A's lead depends on placing greater weight on knowledge; B leads when responsibility receives greater weight. Explain and review the choices instead of treating the larger number as objective truth.

## 7. From Job Evidence to a Usable Design

OPM defines job analysis through the connection between tasks and the competencies needed to perform them. That connection matters more than a long list of desirable personal qualities. Its guidance is written for the US federal context; the analytical principle can be used here without presenting federal procedures as universal legal obligations. [OPM, *What Is a Job Analysis?*](https://www.opm.gov/frequently-asked-questions/assessment-policy-faq/job-analysis/what-is-a-job-analysis/).

For a fictional customer-service job, “excellent communication” is too broad to guide selection or training. Record the task—explain a disputed charge using the account record—the conditions, the acceptable outcome and the authority to escalate. Then identify the necessary knowledge and skill, and decide which must exist at entry and which can be learned. An incumbent's current degree is not automatically a necessary qualification for the task.

| Evidence method | Useful contribution and limitation |
|---|---|
| Observation | Shows workflow and interruptions, but may miss rare incidents or reasoning that is not visible. |
| Interview | Explains decisions and exceptions, but recollection and self-presentation can distort the account. |
| Questionnaire | Supports comparisons across respondents, but unclear items can create consistent-looking errors. |
| Critical incidents | Identifies consequential behavior in specific situations, but memorable cases do not establish ordinary frequency. |

Compare evidence from incumbents, supervisors and work records rather than assuming one method is always best. Record the date and scope: an accurate description can become stale when technology or decision rights change.

The job description records duties, authority, working conditions and interfaces with other roles. The specification records justified requirements for performing the work. A position is a particular instance of a role; several positions may share a job profile, while a job family groups related work. Local naming conventions vary, so define the units before comparing headcounts or grades.

The job characteristics model links features of work with psychological states and individual responses, rather than promising identical effects for everyone. The original article's abstract identifies those interacting classes of variables. Skill variety, task identity and task significance concern the character and meaning of the work; autonomy concerns discretion; feedback concerns information about results. A motivating potential score (MPS) is a model-based diagnostic, not a probability that an employee will be motivated or an observed productivity measure. [Hackman & Oldham, 1976](https://doi.org/10.1016/0030-5073%2876%2990016-7).

After redesign, check whether discretion actually changed, whether employees have the skills to use it and whether quality or workload deteriorated. Compare stable measures over comparable periods and investigate concurrent changes. A favorable survey alone cannot prove that enrichment caused better performance.
