## Why 2048 Is Harder Than It Looks

2048 was built over a single weekend in 2013 by Gabriele Cirulli. The rules are simple: on a 4×4 grid, slide tiles in any direction to merge matching numbers, and reach the 2048 tile to win.

And yet, it's surprisingly hard. Most first attempts end with the board filling up before you get anywhere meaningful.

There are two reasons.

First, **no intuitive feel for exponential growth.** 2 → 4 → 8 → 16 → 32 → 64 → 128 → 256 → 512 → 1024 → 2048. That's 11 steps. And the number of tiles needed to produce each successive step grows geometrically as you progress.

Second, **failure to manage space.** Without knowing that large tiles must be anchored in a corner, tiles scatter toward the center and the board locks up mid-game.

## Play 2048

Use the arrow keys (↑↓←→) or swipe to slide all tiles in one direction and merge matching numbers. Make the 2048 tile to win.

## The Core Strategies

### Strategy 1: Big Tile in the Corner

The single most important rule. Keep your highest tile locked in one corner — the bottom-left, for example — for the entire game.

Why: A large tile in the center blocks movement in every direction. A large tile anchored in a corner becomes a stable reference point that makes systematic space management possible.

### Strategy 2: Limit Your Directions

Stick to 2–3 directions. If you primarily use left and down, tiles naturally accumulate in the bottom-left corner. Moving freely in all four directions scrambles the board and burns space rapidly.

### Strategy 3: Set Up Future Merges

The goal is not the current move — it's **creating the conditions for the next merge**. Even if you can merge two tiles right now, hold back if doing so will block a larger merge later.

<Callout>
High-level 2048 play is like chess. You're not thinking one move ahead — you're thinking three or four ahead. The key is calculating how each current merge shapes the grid configuration for future moves.
</Callout>

## The Intuition Behind Exponential Functions

Humans are comfortable with linear change but intuitively bad at exponential change. This is called **Exponential Growth Bias** — and it explains why 2048 is genuinely difficult.

"I just need to build one more 256 tile" feels reasonable until you realize how many grid spaces that requires. Your gut is calibrated for addition; the game runs on multiplication.

**COVID-19 early response failures**: When there were 100 confirmed cases doubling every two days, most people couldn't viscerally grasp that there would be 3,200 in ten days. Epidemiologists saw it; most others didn't.

**Startup growth expectations**: "If we grow 10% per month, how big will we be in a year?" The intuitive answer is roughly 120%. The correct answer is 214% — more than double what most people expect.

Playing 2048 repeatedly builds this intuition in your hands. The moment late in a game when you realize you don't have enough empty space to take the next step — that physical sensation of scarcity is the essence of compounding.

## Space Management: Protect Your Options

Once 2048's grid is more than 80% full, the game is essentially over. With almost no empty space, you can't execute the moves you need.

This maps directly onto financial and organizational management.

**The value of holding cash**: Over-investing leaves no capacity to act on good opportunities. Warren Buffett has always kept a minimum cash reserve at Berkshire Hathaway precisely for this reason — idle cash is option value.

**Calendar white space**: A 100% scheduled day means a single unexpected event cascades into a chain of missed commitments. Intentional slack is what gives a system resilience.

**Technical debt**: An overly complex codebase leaves no room for new features or bug fixes. A locked 2048 board.

## 2048 and Strategic Patience

The most important lesson 2048 teaches: **strategic patience.**

Rushing to merge tiles out of impatience produces a messy, unmanageable board. Even when your score looks low and progress feels slow, maintaining sound structure is the only path to the high tiles.

Think back to the first time you reached 1024. Those early 2s and 4s looked worthless. But staying disciplined with principles you didn't yet fully trust — and then watching a large tile suddenly appear — is exactly what compounding feels like.
