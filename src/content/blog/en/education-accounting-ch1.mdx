Principles of accounting cover the grammar shared by financial, cost and tax accounting. This chapter sets out the first line of that grammar: the accounting equation and double entry. Definitions follow the *Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting* as adopted in Korean IFRS (K-IFRS). The procedure for journalizing transactions and gathering them into a trial balance continues in [chapter 2](/en/academy-accounting-principles-ch2/).

## 1. Accounting records "accounting transactions"

What we call a transaction in daily life and what goes into the books differ in scope. An accounting transaction is **an event that increases or decreases the entity's assets, liabilities or equity and whose amount can be measured reliably**.

### A. What is and is not a transaction

- **Not a transaction:** receiving or placing an order for goods, signing a sale or lease contract, hiring an employee. A contract alone does not yet move any asset or liability.
- **A transaction:** inventory destroyed in a warehouse fire, cash stolen, a building losing value over time (depreciation). Even with no counterparty, a change in financial position is recorded.

Once a deposit changes hands under a contract, a transaction does exist, because cash and an advance payment (or advance received) move by the amount of the deposit.

### B. Definitions of the elements of the statement of financial position

The Conceptual Framework defines the three elements as follows.

The key to an asset is *control*. Even without legal ownership, if the entity can obtain the economic benefits and restrict others' access to them, it has an asset. A right-of-use asset under a lease is an example.

## 2. The accounting equation is a promise that balances always agree

The three elements are always linked by the following relation. Assets are financed by someone's claims, and those claims split into the creditors' share (liabilities) and the owners' share (equity).

<FormulaBox
  label="Expanded accounting equation"
  formula="\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Share capital} + (\text{Opening retained earnings} + \text{Revenue} - \text{Expenses} - \text{Dividends})"
  description="Revenue and expenses end up changing equity through retained earnings. Revenue increases equity; expenses decrease it."
/>

This expanded form links the income statement to the statement of financial position. Profit for the period, revenue minus expenses, flows into retained earnings and forms closing equity.

## 3. Every transaction moves two sides at once

A single transaction changes two items by the same amount, either on both sides of the equation or within one side. This is the **duality of transactions**, and the recording method that enters the same amount on the debit (left) and credit (right) side is **double-entry bookkeeping**.

<LectureTable
  title="The eight elements of a transaction"
  headers={["Debit (left)", "Credit (right)"]}
  rows={[
    ["Increase in assets", "Decrease in assets"],
    ["Decrease in liabilities", "Increase in liabilities"],

One of the four left-hand elements pairs with one of the four right-hand elements. Borrowing from a bank pairs "increase in assets (cash)" with "increase in liabilities (borrowings)"; providing a service on credit pairs "increase in assets (trade receivables)" with "revenue earned (sales)".

## 4. Tracing the equation through a month of transactions

Suppose a service company made the following six transactions in March.

<LectureTable
  title="March transactions and changes in the equation (unit: ₩10,000)"
  headers={["Transaction", "Assets", "Liabilities", "Equity", "Paired elements"]}
  rows={[
    ["① Shareholders contribute cash 1,000", "Cash +1,000", "", "Share capital +1,000", "Asset increase / equity increase"],
    ["② Borrow 400 from a bank", "Cash +400", "Borrowings +400", "", "Asset increase / liability increase"],
    ["③ Buy equipment 300 for cash", "Equipment +300, cash −300", "", "", "Asset increase / asset decrease"],
    ["④ Provide services 500 on credit", "Receivables +500", "", "Sales +500", "Asset increase / revenue earned"],
    ["⑤ Collect 200 of receivables", "Cash +200, receivables −200", "", "", "Asset increase / asset decrease"],
    ["⑥ Pay rent 100 in cash", "Cash −100", "", "Rent −100", "Expense incurred / asset decrease"],
  ]}
/>

At month-end, cash is 1,200 (= 1,000 + 400 − 300 + 200 − 100), receivables 300 and equipment 300, so total assets are ₩18 million. Liabilities are borrowings of ₩4 million, and equity is share capital of ₩10 million plus profit of ₩4 million (= 500 − 100), or ₩14 million. $1{,}800 = 400 + 1{,}400$, so the equation holds.

Transactions ③ and ⑤ only swap accounts within assets; the total is unchanged. Transaction ④ increased equity without a single won of cash coming in. These two types are where a cash-only book and double entry part ways.

## 5. The cash balance and profit are different numbers

If the same month is summarized only in a cash book, closing cash of ₩12 million is all there is. The receivables of ₩3 million and the equipment of ₩3 million disappear, and profit cannot be known. A ₩12 million rise in cash does not mean a ₩12 million profit either: ₩14 million came in from contributions and borrowing, while operations actually brought in ₩2 million and paid out ₩4 million.

### A. What double entry checks

Equal debit and credit totals mean that **the same amount was written on both sides**. If this balance breaks, there is a recording error somewhere.

### B. What double entry cannot check

Omitting a whole transaction, recording the same transaction twice or choosing the wrong account still leaves both totals equal. For example, if the ₩2 million collected in ⑤ is recorded as sales, the equation still holds, but sales are overstated by ₩2 million and receivables remain overstated by ₩2 million.

<Callout type="warning">
Retained earnings of ₩4 million do not mean ₩4 million sits in the bank. Those resources are also tied up in equipment and receivables. Conversely, an increase in cash does not mean a profit was made. That the equation balances is not evidence that the records are complete — only that the same amount was written on both sides.
</Callout>

## 6. The assumption and measurement bases behind the equation

### A. The going concern assumption

The only underlying assumption stated in the Conceptual Framework is **going concern**: financial statements are prepared on the basis that the entity will continue operating for the foreseeable future. That is why the ₩3 million of equipment is recorded not at what it would fetch if sold today, but at cost that is spread over the years of use as expense. If liquidation becomes certain, the assumption breaks and a different basis, revaluing assets at disposal value, applies.

### B. Historical cost and current value

Measurement bases fall into two broad groups. **Historical cost** is based on the consideration paid at acquisition; **current value** — fair value, value in use, current cost — reflects conditions at the measurement date. The equipment in section 4 was recorded at its historical cost of ₩3 million. Even if the market price of the same equipment rises to ₩3.5 million, the carrying amount does not change under the cost model. Which basis applies to which asset is set by the individual standards and is covered in detail in [Intermediate Accounting](/en/academy-intermediate-accounting-ch1/).

### C. Relevance and faithful representation

For financial information to be useful it must have two fundamental qualitative characteristics. It must be able to change decisions (**relevance**), and it must depict economic phenomena completely, neutrally and free from error (**faithful representation**). Recording receivables of ₩3 million is relevant, but leaving the full amount in place when collection is uncertain weakens faithful representation. This tension leads to the bad-debt estimate in [chapter 7](/en/academy-accounting-principles-ch7/).

## Check your understanding

In April, the company in section 4 (1) buys fixtures of ₩2 million on credit, (2) collects ₩1 million of receivables in cash, and (3) agrees to pay April salaries of ₩800,000 in May. What are total assets, liabilities and equity at the end of April, and how much cash is there?

(1) increases assets (fixtures) and liabilities (other payables) by ₩2 million each. (2) only turns receivables into cash within assets. (3) incurs an expense that reduces equity by ₩800,000 and increases liabilities (salaries payable) by ₩800,000. So assets are ₩20 million, liabilities ₩6.8 million and equity ₩13.2 million, and $2{,}000 = 680 + 1{,}320$. Cash is ₩13 million. The salaries have not been paid yet, but they are an April expense. This idea leads into accrual accounting and adjusting entries in [chapter 3](/en/education-accounting-ch3/).
