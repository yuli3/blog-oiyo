## 1. Pay design brings together competing reference points

Compensation comprises direct pay and benefits. Direct pay includes base pay and variable payments; benefits can include insurance, retirement contributions, and leave arrangements. The employer's cost, the employee's cash income, and the employee's value from a benefit are different quantities. An employer contribution is not automatically spendable income.

Three equity questions distinguish different comparisons:

| Perspective | Comparison | Potential tension |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Internal | Relative job value within the organization | A scarce skill may command a market premium beyond its internal job grade |
| External | Relevant labor-market opportunities | A market match may compress the gap between experienced employees and new hires |
| Individual | People performing comparable work | Differences require a defensible basis rather than a vague label such as “potential” |

These are management concepts, not legal findings. For U.S. legal analysis, EEOC explains that Equal Pay Act comparisons concern substantially equal job content, not matching titles; other anti-discrimination laws have different requirements. [EEOC: pay and compensation discrimination](https://www.eeoc.gov/equal-paycompensation-discrimination). A satisfactory market comparison does not prove that a pay difference is lawful.

Use [job evaluation](/en/academy-hrm-basics-ch2/) for internal job comparisons, and compare external pay with an appropriate occupation, location, level, and date. Do not mix base salary at one employer with total target compensation at another.

### A. Read a pay range without turning its midpoint into a rule

A **compa-ratio** divides an employee's base pay by the midpoint of the relevant range. A base salary of USD 63,000 in a range with a USD 70,000 midpoint gives 0.90. This describes a position in that range; it does not prove underpayment, entitlement to a raise, or lower performance.

Before using the ratio, check whether the range covers the right job, whether both values use the same annual or hourly basis, and whether the range is current. Entry-level experience, sustained contribution, scarcity, and the pay policy may matter. Those are possible considerations, not a justification invented after seeing the employee's salary.

## 2. Choose what earns pay, not just the payment's name

| Basis | What earns pay, and what must be defined? |
| --- | --- |
| Seniority | Service or step progression; define credited service and the progression rule. |
| Job-based | Evaluated responsibilities and demands; define job content, factors, grades, and review process. |
| Skill-based | Specified skills held or demonstrated; define relevant evidence, certification, and maintenance. |
| Performance | Defined individual, team, or organizational results; define measures, thresholds, attribution, and payment conditions. |

Skill-based and competency-based arrangements are related but need not be identical: a certified technical skill is not the same evidence as a broadly defined behavioral competency. A combined pay system can use several bases. Calling an arrangement “job-based” does not imply that individual progression or market premiums disappear.

Variable pay can change behavior when a credible relationship connects controllable actions with meaningful rewards. It can also reward the wrong outcome if an easy-to-count target omits quality, cooperation, or long-term work. A volume commission with no quality safeguard creates a possible tension; it does not establish that every employee will game the measure.

## 3. Equal target pay can mean different income risk

Consider two fictional annual cash-pay designs. Both have target compensation of USD 75,000. Assume base pay is unchanged and the incentive payout is a linear proportion of the target incentive. Exclude taxes, benefits, overtime, and all other payments.

| Quantity | Design A | Design B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Base pay | USD 60,000 | USD 45,000 |
| Target incentive | USD 15,000 | USD 30,000 |
| Incentive share of target pay | 20% | 40% |
| Cash pay at 50% incentive payout | USD 67,500 | USD 60,000 |
| Cash pay at zero incentive payout | USD 60,000 | USD 45,000 |

Design A at a half payout is 60,000 + 0.5 × 15,000 = USD 67,500. Design B is 45,000 + 0.5 × 30,000 = USD 60,000. The same target total therefore produces a USD 7,500 difference in that scenario.

A larger variable share is not simply “more motivation”: it transfers more income variability to the employee. Whether that is appropriate depends on control over results, payout rules, income stability, and other constraints. A threshold, cap, or nonlinear payout curve would change this example. None of the displayed totals is take-home pay.

## 4. Distinguish gainsharing, profit sharing, and ownership

**Gainsharing** distributes a defined operating improvement relative to an agreed baseline. **Profit sharing** links payment to defined organizational profit, which may reflect pricing, financing, and other factors outside an employee's control. **Ownership arrangements** create an interest whose value and liquidity can differ from a cash bonus. The labels do not specify vesting, payout, tax treatment, or risk.

For a simplified fictional gainsharing calculation, assume comparable output valued at USD 5 million, a baseline labor-cost ratio of 20%, and actual eligible labor cost of USD 800,000. Assume the baseline and actual cost use consistent definitions and that all relevant adjustments have already been made.

| Step | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Baseline labor allowance | 0.20 × USD 5,000,000 | USD 1,000,000 |
| Improvement pool | USD 1,000,000 − USD 800,000 | USD 200,000 |
| Employee share, assumed 50% | 0.50 × USD 200,000 | USD 100,000 |

The employee allocation is a hypothetical plan rule, not a standard 50% entitlement. Also, a cost-ratio improvement is not proof of higher physical productivity. Changes in prices, product mix, outsourcing, quality, or the accounting perimeter could change the ratio. Agree on adjustments, quality safeguards, and verification before interpreting the pool as employee-created savings.

## 5. Benefit choice does not automatically reduce cost

A benefit menu can serve differing needs, but choices remain constrained by eligibility, enrollment, employer budgets, provider arrangements, and applicable rules. A benefit valued by one employee may be of little use to another. A flexible plan is not automatically cheaper: administration, pricing, and the choices participants make also affect cost.

Distinguish a statutory protection from an employer-provided benefit and a tax-advantaged plan. In the U.S., for example, FMLA leave protection depends on both a covered employer and an eligible employee, including service, hours, and worksite conditions. It is not accurately summarized as “every employee at a 50-person employer receives leave.” FMLA leave may be unpaid or overlap with employer-provided paid leave. [DOL Fact Sheet 28: FMLA](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/28-fmla).

Do not transport U.S. benefit and tax labels into another country's employment system. Identify the jurisdiction and the actual plan rather than treating a generic benefits checklist as a complete compliance guide.

## 6. Salary, job title, and overtime exemption are not synonyms

This section concerns U.S. federal references. DOL explains that job titles do not establish exemption: the applicable duties and compensation requirements must be met. Different exemption categories have different tests. **Being paid a salary, or carrying a professional title, is not by itself enough.** State or local rules and contractual obligations may also matter. [DOL Fact Sheet 17A: exemption framework](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/17a-overtime).

For covered nonexempt employees, the federal rule generally requires at least one-and-a-half times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek; hours cannot simply be averaged across two weeks. [DOL: overtime pay](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/overtime). The regular rate is not necessarily the posted base hourly rate: which payments enter it depends on statutory inclusion and exclusion rules. [DOL Fact Sheet 56A: regular rate](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/56a-regular-rate).

Verify current requirements, coverage, and local rules for an actual payroll decision. This study chapter does not freeze changing salary thresholds or minimum-wage amounts as universal constants, classify a real employee, or calculate legal pay entitlements.

## Practice: compare a proposed compensation change

### Problem

Using the fictional pay-mix table, a manager says: “Design B costs no more at target, so it has no disadvantage for employees. We can also classify everyone as salaried to eliminate overtime costs.” Identify the valid comparison and the unsupported conclusions.

### Worked answer

The designs have the same target cash total under the stated assumptions. They do not provide the same guaranteed base or the same downside cash flow: at a half incentive payout, Design B pays USD 7,500 less; at zero payout, it pays USD 15,000 less.

That comparison does not establish the employer's total cost, employee preference, productivity effect, or fairness. Benefits, administration, and legal obligations were excluded from the example. Salary designation alone does not establish overtime exemption. Separate pay-policy choices, scenario calculations, and applicable legal tests before recommending a change.
