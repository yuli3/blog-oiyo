# Behavioral Economics — What to Believe, and What Standard to Design Policy By

Chapters 1–9 looked at where the assumptions of the standard model go wrong. The final chapter closes the course with two questions. How far can those findings be trusted? And if people's choices may not be good for them, what standard should policy use?

## 1. Replicability: what survived?

In the 2010s, a wave of studies re-tested famous findings in psychology and the social sciences on a large scale. When the Open Science Collaboration (2015) replicated 100 psychology studies, about 36% produced statistically significant results in the same direction as the originals. When Camerer et al. (2016) replicated 18 laboratory experiments from leading economics journals, 11 (about 61%) replicated.

**Ego depletion** is the hypothesis that willpower is a resource that runs down with use. Early studies reported that after a self-control task, persistence on the next task fell. In the preregistered replication by Hagger et al. (2016) across 23 laboratories (about 2,100 participants), the effect was almost zero. The widely cited study claiming that "judges grant fewer paroles just before meals" was also reanalysed, showing that the order of cases was not random — for instance, cases without lawyers were bunched at the end of sessions. This does not deny any relationship between fatigue and decision-making, but the "willpower fuel" model and the dramatic effect sizes have lost their support.

The lesson of the replication problem is methodological: give priority to results backed by preregistration, large samples, joint replication across laboratories and cross-checks against field data.

## 2. When choices do not reveal welfare

Standard welfare economics relies on **revealed preference**: if someone chose A, A is better. Behavioral economics cuts this link. A person with the present bias of Chapter 4 puts off exercise today, but yesterday the same person wanted to exercise today. Which of the two choices reveals welfare?

Whatever standard is chosen shapes the conclusion, so policy analysis must state which welfare standard it uses.

## 3. Internalities and corrective taxes

The externalities of Public Finance Chapter 1 were costs imposed on others. Costs that present bias leads people to impose on their own future selves are called **internalities**. Part of the cost of smoking and excessive sugar consumption is borne by one's future self. If the long-run self standard is accepted, then, as O'Donoghue and Rabin showed, corrective taxes on internalities also raise welfare.

<FormulaBox
  label="Corrective tax including internalities"
  formula="t^* \approx \text{marginal external cost} + \text{marginal internality}"
  description="The marginal internality is the future cost of one unit of consumption that the present self ignores. If biases differ across consumers, the same rate applies to both strongly and weakly biased people, so the weakly biased are overtaxed."
/>

If the future health cost of a can of soft drink is 200 won and consumers take only half of it into account, the marginal internality is 100 won. The problems are distribution and heterogeneity. A sugar tax falls more heavily on low-income households (it is regressive) and is an unnecessary tax for consumers with little bias. The case strengthens when the revenue is returned to low-income households, or when there is evidence that more strongly biased groups respond more to the tax.

## 4. Do markets correct biases or exploit them?

The intuition that competition eliminates biases does not always hold. Firms have an incentive to design contracts that exploit biases.

- **Gym fees** (Chapter 4): monthly memberships are highly profitable with naive present-biased customers.
- **Hidden add-on costs**: strategies that shroud add-on costs, like printers and ink or bank fees, can survive competition (Gabaix and Laibson). A firm that discloses add-on costs loses naive customers, while sophisticated customers already avoid the add-ons, so disclosure gains nothing.
- **Auto-renewing subscriptions**: designs that make cancelling a chore (the sludge of Chapter 9) exploit present bias and status quo bias.

Here is the role of regulation. Mandatory disclosure of add-on costs, simplified cancellation procedures and cooling-off periods (the right to withdraw) are institutions that reduce the profitability of contracts that exploit biases.

<Callout type="warning">
The conclusion "people are biased, so government should decide for them" does not follow from this course. Policy designers are people with the same biases, and the government failures of Public Finance Chapter 3 still operate. What behavioral economics offers is not conclusions but more accurate predictions. The starting point for applying it to policy is to weigh, with evidence, how much an intervention changes whose behaviour.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

A tax of 250 won is imposed on a good with a marginal external cost of 150 won and a future health cost of 100 won that consumers ignore. Is this rate right for an unbiased consumer (one who takes all future costs into account)? For that consumer the internality is 0, so the corrective tax should be 150 won. The extra 100 won only creates excess burden for them. When biases differ across people, a single rate can only target the average internality, and this excess burden is the price.
