Bari Gongju is one of the most widely known figures in Korean shamanic narrative. Her name itself carries the fate of an "abandoned princess," and she transforms from a daughter cast away by her parents into a divine being who guides the dead. She is transmitted under names and ritual contexts such as Baridegi, Bari Gongju, and the origin narrative of the Ogu-gut, with details varying by region and shamanic lineage. Yet at the center remains the same structure: abandonment, a long journey, the water of life, the salvation of parents, and guidance through the underworld.

## Core Myth — Representative Episode

Bari Gongju is born as the seventh daughter of a king and queen who had wished for a son, and she is abandoned. The discarded child is raised by others or survives through hardship; later, it is revealed that Bari Gongju alone can obtain the medicinal water or water of life needed to save her ailing parents. This reversal creates a structure in which the very person ignored by family and power ultimately becomes the one who owns the path that saves life.

Bari Gongju sets out for Seocheon, the Western Realm, the underworld, or a distant world of life. On the road she endures many trials, encounters a being who guards the water or a divine man, and bears labor and time in order to obtain the water of life. Depending on the transmission, the details of marriage, children, and discipline differ, but she is not a figure who saves her parents through an easy miracle. She is a savior who has endured a long rite of passage.

At last, Bari Gongju brings back the water of life, revives her parents, and takes on the role of guiding the dead to the underworld. For that reason, Bari Gongju is too large a figure to be read only as a tale of a filial daughter. She is a symbol of the shamanic road that connects the living and the dead, abandonment and salvation, women's suffering and deification.

## Reading as an Archetype — Jungian Perspective

From a Jungian perspective, Bari Gongju is an archetype of the **wounded healer** and the **underworld traveler**. Through her own experience of abandonment, she gains the power to help other beings pass through death and loss. In modern terms, she resembles the scene in which someone who was not recognized within the family displays deep responsiveness in care work, counseling, funerary practice, art, or social healing.

The Bari Gongju archetype does not romanticize pain. Abandonment is a wound, and the journey is severe. But the myth says that when such a wound is consciously passed through, it can be transformed into the ability to guide others through darkness. Bari Gongju, then, is not "the abandoned person" but "the one who learned the road and returned."

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q. Was Bari Gongju a real princess?**

A. Rather than a historical princess, she is a mythic figure in shamanic narrative. Her name and plot vary somewhat by regional transmission.

**Q. Why is Bari Gongju connected with the underworld?**

A. She crosses the boundary of death to obtain the water of life, and afterward she takes on the role of guiding the dead. This is why she is deeply connected with rites for guiding the dead.
