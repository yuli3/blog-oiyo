Tyr is a god in Norse mythology deeply connected with war, law, courage, and oaths. Today he is less widely known than Thor or Odin, but in the story of Fenrir's binding, Tyr shows a stronger ethical tension than almost anyone else. He takes part in a deception, yet bears its cost with his own body.

## Core Myth — Representative Episode

Tyr's representative episode is the binding of Fenrir. The gods know that the wolf Fenrir will one day become a great catastrophe, so they try to bind him, but Fenrir breaks every ordinary chain. When they finally bring Gleipnir, the magical fetter made by the dwarves, Fenrir suspects deception and demands that someone place a hand in his mouth as a guarantee.

At that moment, Tyr steps forward. The gods had no intention of freeing Fenrir, and Tyr offers his hand even though he knows this. After Fenrir is bound, he realizes the promise has been broken and bites off Tyr's hand. This scene is a harsh myth showing that preserving order can sometimes leave a moral wound.

## Reading as an Archetype — A Jungian Archetype View

Tyr is an archetype of the **just warrior** and the **self-sacrificing guardian of law**. He is remembered through responsibility rather than victory. In modern terms, he resembles a leader who bears the burden of a difficult organizational decision, a practitioner who accepts personal loss for the public good, or someone who knows the weight of a promise in their own body. Tyr shows that justice is not sustained by clean feelings alone, and that it can sometimes be accompanied by wounds and guilt.

Tyr's hand shows that law and trust are not abstract words, but can require actual loss. He is not a perfectly pure hero, but a figure who does not avoid responsibility within an imperfect order. For that reason, the Tyr archetype often comes to mind in scenes where the weight of a promise takes precedence over personal comfort, such as contracts, public office, organizational ethics, and communal safety.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q. Why did Tyr lose his hand?**

A. Because he offered his hand as a guarantee for the promise involved in binding Fenrir. When the gods did not keep the promise, Fenrir bit off that hand.

**Q. Is Tyr a just god, or a god who took part in deception?**

A. Both. That is why Tyr symbolizes not simple justice, but the ethical wounds that arise when order is preserved.
