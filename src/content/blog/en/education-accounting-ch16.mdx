This chapter applies the relevant cost principle of [chapter 1](/en/education-accounting-ch13/) to four typical short-term decisions. In all four the question is the same: **which revenues and costs change because of this decision?** How fixed costs are treated — especially common fixed costs shared with other products — decides the answer.

## 1. Special orders

Company BK's product sells for ₩50,000 per unit, variable cost is ₩30,000 and fixed costs are ₩20 million a year (₩20,000 per unit at a capacity of 10,000 units). A customer offers to buy 1,000 units at ₩35,000 each.

<LectureTable
  title="Whether to accept the special order (unit: ₩10,000)"
  headers={["Situation", "Incremental revenue", "Incremental cost", "Opportunity cost", "Incremental profit"]}
  rows={[
    ["Enough idle capacity (currently producing 8,000 units)", "3,500", "3,000", "0", "+500 → accept"],
    ["Full capacity (all 10,000 units sold normally)", "3,500", "3,000", "Contribution margin of 1,000 regular units: 2,000", "−1,500 → reject"],
  ]}
/>

With idle capacity, fixed costs are irrelevant to the order. A price below the ₩50,000 full unit cost still raises profit as long as it exceeds the ₩30,000 variable cost. At full capacity, the order displaces regular sales, so their contribution margin becomes an opportunity cost. Also consider qualitative factors such as the risk that existing customers learn of the discount and demand lower prices, and fair-trade issues.

## 2. Make or buy

BK makes 10,000 components itself. Per unit, costs are direct materials ₩10,000, direct labor ₩8,000, variable overhead ₩4,000 and fixed overhead ₩10,000 (of which a supervisor's salary of ₩3,000 is avoidable if production stops). An outside supplier offers them at ₩23,000 each.

<FormulaBox
  label="Relevant cost of making"
  formula="\text{Relevant cost} = 1 + 0.8 + 0.4 + \underbrace{0.3}_{\text{avoidable fixed}} = 2.5 > 2.3"
  description="In ₩10,000 per unit. The ₩7,000 of unavoidable fixed cost remains even if the part is bought, so it is irrelevant. Buying saves ₩2,000 per unit, ₩20 million in total."
/>

If the capacity freed by buying could be rented out for ₩3 million a year, that rent is also a benefit of buying (an opportunity cost of making). Conversely, the supplier's quality and delivery risks and the risk of leaking key technology are weighed outside the numbers.

## 3. Dropping a product line

Company BL's profit by product line is as follows. ₩6 million of common fixed costs was allocated in proportion to sales.

C appears to have an operating loss of ₩330,000, but dropping it eliminates only its ₩3 million contribution margin and ₩2 million of avoidable fixed costs, while its ₩1.33 million share of common fixed costs is reloaded onto A and B. Company profit **falls** to $550 - 100 = 450$ (₩4.5 million). The criterion is not operating profit after allocation but the **segment margin** (contribution margin − avoidable fixed costs). If C complements A and B by helping them sell, the loss from dropping it is even larger.

## 4. Product mix under a constrained resource

Machine time is limited to 1,000 hours a month. Product X earns a contribution margin of ₩40,000 per unit and needs 2 machine hours; product Y earns ₩30,000 per unit and needs 1 machine hour. Monthly demand is 400 units of X and 600 of Y.

<FormulaBox
  label="Contribution margin per unit of the constrained resource"
  formula="\text{X}: \frac{4}{2} = 2 \text{ per hour}, \qquad \text{Y}: \frac{3}{1} = 3 \text{ per hour}"
  description="In ₩10,000. X has the larger contribution margin per unit, but Y earns more per scarce machine hour. Make Y first."
/>

Make Y up to demand, 600 units (600 hours), and use the remaining 400 hours for 200 units of X. Contribution margin is $600 \times 3 + 200 \times 4 = 2{,}600$ (₩26 million). Making 400 units of X first (800 hours) and then 200 units of Y yields only $1{,}600 + 600 = 2{,}200$ (₩22 million). With several constraints, use linear programming. Expanding the capacity of the constrained resource (the bottleneck) is the fastest way to raise total profit.

<Callout type="warning">
Short-term decision analysis rests on the premise that "fixed costs do not change with this decision". If special orders are taken repeatedly or outsourcing keeps growing, fixed costs also become adjustable in the long run. And frequently accepting special orders at prices near variable cost undermines the whole pricing structure. Do not carry the conclusions of short-term analysis over into long-term pricing policy.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

Company BM makes a component for product Z at ₩15,000 per unit (direct materials 6,000, direct labor 4,000, variable overhead 2,000, allocated fixed overhead 3,000). Fixed overhead does not fall if component production stops. An outside supplier offers ₩13,000 per unit, and buying would free space to make another product earning ₩4 million a year in additional contribution margin. With annual requirements of 5,000 units, should BM buy?

The relevant cost of making is the variable cost of ₩12,000, ₩1,000 less than the ₩13,000 purchase price, so ignoring the space, making is ₩5 million a year better. Adding the ₩4 million of contribution margin gained by buying, buying is still ₩1 million worse. Making remains ₩1 million a year better.
