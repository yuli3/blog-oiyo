# Behavioral Economics — A More Precise Claim than "People Are Irrational"

Summarizing behavioral economics as "people are irrational" leaves almost nothing usable. A more precise summary is this: the standard economic model makes specific assumptions about preferences, beliefs and the choice process, and some of them fail systematically, in predictable directions. Knowing the direction of the failure lets us fix the model, and the fixed model yields different predictions and policies from the standard one.

## 1. Three assumptions of the standard model

The consumer of microeconomics chooses as follows.

<FormulaBox
  label="Choice in the standard model"
  formula="\max_{x}\; \sum_{t=0}^{T} \delta^{t} \sum_{s} p_s\, u(x_{t,s})"
  description="It contains utility over outcomes u (preferences), probabilities p_s of states s (beliefs), exponential discounting δ^t, and the assumption that this problem is solved exactly (the choice process)."
/>

This expression contains at least three assumptions.

- **Preferences**: utility depends only on final outcomes (levels of wealth, amounts consumed), people care only about their own outcomes, and they discount time consistently.
- **Beliefs**: probabilities are updated correctly by Bayes' rule.
- **Choice process**: given their preferences and beliefs, people compute the optimum exactly and choose the same answer however the problem is presented.

The main findings of behavioral economics correspond to each of these three assumptions.

## 2. Bounded rationality

Simon argued that people do not optimize but **satisfice**: instead of comparing every alternative, they stop when a good-enough option appears, because gathering information and calculating are costly. From this viewpoint, heuristics (simple rules of judgement) are not defects but rational responses to limited cognitive resources.

What interests behavioral economics is where heuristics go wrong **systematically**. Random errors cancel out on average, but systematic errors accumulate across whole markets and, because they are predictable, can be built into models.

## 3. Where does the evidence come from?

For example, the evidence on automatic enrolment in retirement plans (Chapter 9) came from an observational study comparing new employees' participation rates before and after one firm changed its plan. With large stakes and real saving as the outcome, it is more persuasive than a laboratory experiment. Conversely, measuring pure inequity aversion is better done with laboratory games (Chapter 7) free of reputation and repeated dealings.

## 4. Why departures do not mean abandoning the standard model

The standard model is still the benchmark. Good behavioral models include the standard model as a special case: the β-δ model of Chapter 4 returns to the standard model when $\beta=1$, and the inequity aversion model of Chapter 7 when the inequity coefficients are zero. This allows the size of a departure to be measured by a single parameter and tested with data.

One must also ask whether markets correct biases. In repeated decisions with quick feedback (daily grocery shopping), learning reduces bias. In rare decisions with slow feedback (retirement saving, buying a home, marriage), biases persist. That is why the findings of behavioral economics matter most for policy in the latter domain.

<Callout type="warning">
Some famous early findings in behavioral economics turned out to be small or failed to replicate in large-scale replication studies (Chapter 10). Distinguish results confirmed repeatedly across methods, such as loss aversion, present bias and default effects, from results resting on a single laboratory study. This course states the strength of the evidence alongside each concept.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

How would you rewrite the claim "buying lottery tickets is irrational" in terms of the three assumptions of the standard model? Buying lottery tickets could be explained by risk-loving preferences (preferences), overestimating the chance of winning (beliefs), or a decision process that overweights small probabilities (the probability weighting of Chapter 3). Predictions and policies differ depending on which assumption fails. If people overestimate probabilities, providing information helps; if probability weighting is the cause, the prediction is that information alone will not change behaviour much.
