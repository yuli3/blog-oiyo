## 1. Labor relations concerns authority as well as cooperation

A company can offer generous benefits and still have unresolved conflicts over scheduling, discipline, representation, and who makes decisions. Labor relations studies the institutions through which employees and employers express these interests, negotiate rules, and contest their application. It is not another name for an employee-engagement program.

Three perspectives help expose different assumptions. A **unitarist** view emphasizes shared organizational purposes; a **pluralist** view recognizes distinct interests and treats negotiation as a way to manage conflict; a **radical** view foregrounds structural inequalities in the employment relationship. These are analytical perspectives, not instructions to label every disagreement as disloyalty or every cooperative arrangement as deception.

Identify the actors before selecting a procedure: the employees covered by the issue, their representative, the employer with decision-making authority, and the relevant public institutions. A government regulator and a mediator do not have interchangeable roles. Also distinguish a proposed change to employment rules from a complaint about how an existing rule was applied.

### A. Define the jurisdiction before using the legal framework

The legal discussion below concerns U.S. collective bargaining under the NLRA, not a universal employment-law system. Public-sector, Railway Labor Act, and other excluded relationships require separate jurisdictional analysis; check the statutory employer and employee definitions in [29 U.S.C. §152](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/29/152). Do not substitute these rules for Korean law or assume that every English-speaking workplace follows U.S. law.

For a covered relationship, section 8(d) describes good-faith bargaining over wages, hours, and other employment terms, and a written agreement if requested. It does **not** require acceptance of a proposal or a concession. [29 U.S.C. §158(d), official statutory text](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/29/158).

## 2. A bargaining subject is not classified by its label

“Management prerogative” is not a synonym for an illegal bargaining subject. The question is what the proposal changes, who it affects, and which legal rule governs it.

| Classification | Central distinction | Mistake to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mandatory | Within the legally required scope of bargaining | Treating the duty to bargain as a duty to accept the other party's terms |
| Permissive | Lawful discussion by mutual choice, outside the mandatory scope | Treating “not compulsory” as “prohibited” |
| Illegal | A term contrary to applicable law | Assuming mutual consent can make an unlawful term enforceable |

These categories are summarized in [NLRB guidance on union bargaining obligations](https://www.nlrb.gov/about-nlrb/rights-we-protect/whats-law/unions). A permissive proposal cannot be insisted on to impasse as a condition of agreement. Operational decisions also need issue-specific analysis: separate the decision itself from proposed changes to employees' pay, hours, or other conditions. Do not infer the classification from the word “restructuring” alone.

A useful review memo therefore records the actual proposal, affected employees, existing contractual language, and the authority used for classification. A list of topic names cannot replace that analysis.

## 3. Bargaining and grievance handling solve different problems

**Bargaining** concerns an agreement to be reached or changed. A **grievance** concerns the interpretation or application of an existing agreement. For example, negotiating a new overtime allocation rule differs from alleging that management ignored the current allocation rule. Sending both issues through the same checklist can obscure whether the parties are negotiating future rights or resolving existing ones.

The following is a comparison of tools, not an automatic escalation ladder.

| Tool | Who determines the outcome? | Question to resolve first |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct negotiation | The parties reach an agreement | Which issue and authority are actually at the table? |
| Mediation | The parties; a neutral assists | What barriers prevent an acceptable settlement? |
| Arbitration | An arbitrator within the applicable authority and scope | Does the agreement or law provide for this dispute and remedy? |
| Strike or lockout | Economic pressure, not a neutral decision | What legal and contractual restrictions apply? |

FMCS explains that a mediator cannot impose settlement terms. Grievance mediation can supplement a contractual grievance procedure by mutual agreement; it is not a mandatory step in every dispute. [FMCS FAQs: mediation and grievance procedures](https://www.fmcs.gov/resources/faqs/).

An FMCS arbitrator panel is a selection service: **FMCS does not itself issue arbitration awards**, and the arbitrators are not its employees. The parties select under their agreement. [FMCS arbitration program](https://www.fmcs.gov/services/arbitration/). Consequently, “mediation failed” does not by itself establish that either party may compel arbitration, and an arbitration award is not a routine permission to strike.

## 4. Evaluate proposals using a shared cost base

A percentage wage proposal is incomplete without a payroll base, covered population, effective date, and duration. Distinguish recurring base-pay changes from one-time payments; compare nominal pay with inflation separately; include benefits and scheduling where relevant. The following deliberately simplified case illustrates the calculation, not a negotiated market rate.

Suppose a bargaining unit has 180 employees, each with annual base pay of USD 50,000. The union proposes a 7% increase, and management proposes 2%, both effective for the full next year. Assume unchanged employment, hours, and benefits, and exclude payroll taxes and overtime effects.

| Quantity | Calculation | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Existing annual base payroll | 180 × USD 50,000 | USD 9,000,000 |
| Additional annual pay at 7% | USD 9,000,000 × 0.07 | USD 630,000 |
| Additional annual pay at 2% | USD 9,000,000 × 0.02 | USD 180,000 |
| Difference between proposals | USD 630,000 − USD 180,000 | USD 450,000 |

The USD 450,000 is a **first-year base-pay difference**, not the total agreement cost. A multiyear increase compounds if successive raises apply to the revised base. An agreement with staggered effective dates needs a different first-year calculation.

Now assume four days of interrupted operations, with USD 80,000 of sales per day that cannot be recovered later. The sales shortfall is USD 320,000. This is not USD 320,000 of lost profit: avoided variable costs, continuing fixed costs, recovery orders, and penalties affect the economic impact. It is also not evidence that either wage offer is fair or that the stoppage is lawful.

## 5. Notice periods are not agreement durations

For modification or termination of an existing agreement, FMCS describes notice to the other party at least 60 days before expiration and notice to FMCS and applicable state agencies within 30 days after that notice. Healthcare institutions have extended periods of 90 and 60 days. Other situations have separate requirements. [FMCS required notices](https://www.fmcs.gov/services/collectivebargaining/collective-bargaining-mediation/).

These periods concern **procedure and recipients**, not a universal contract length. Check the actual agreement, jurisdiction, relevant exceptions, and current law. This chapter is a study framework, not a filing calendar or an assessment of an actual dispute.

## Practice: distinguish what the numbers establish

### Problem

Using the case above, a manager says: “The union's demand costs USD 630,000, so accepting our USD 180,000 proposal instead saves USD 450,000. A four-day stoppage costs only USD 320,000, therefore a stoppage is rational and legally safe.” Identify the valid calculations and the invalid conclusions.

### Worked answer

The two payroll increments and their USD 450,000 difference are correct under the stated assumptions. The USD 320,000 sales shortfall is also arithmetically correct under the non-recovery assumption.

The comparison uses unlike measures: a recurring annual payroll difference versus a one-time sales shortfall. It omits profit margins, other stoppage costs, the agreement's duration, and possible settlement alternatives. Even a corrected economic comparison would not establish legal permissibility. A defensible memo separates calculation, scenario assumptions, and legal analysis rather than treating one number as the answer to all three questions.
