# Behavioral Economics — Today's Self and Tomorrow's Self Make Different Plans

Discounting the future is not in itself irrational. The problem is how we discount. With the exponential discounting of the standard model, the plan made today is one we still want to follow tomorrow. Real people plan patiently for the distant future and then reverse their plans when that future becomes "now."

## 1. Exponential discounting is time-consistent

<FormulaBox
  label="Exponential discounting"
  formula="U_0 = u_0 + \delta u_1 + \delta^2 u_2 + \cdots"
  description="The discount rate between any two periods is the same, δ. The trade-off between 'in 10 days vs in 11 days' seen today equals that between 'today vs tomorrow' seen 10 days from now, so plans are never reversed."
/>

## 2. Quasi-hyperbolic discounting and preference reversals

Laibson's **β-δ model** adds an extra discount $\beta<1$ between "now" and "later."

<FormulaBox
  label="Quasi-hyperbolic (β-δ) discounting"
  formula="U_0 = u_0 + \beta\left(\delta u_1 + \delta^2 u_2 + \cdots\right)"
  description="With β = 1 it reduces to exponential discounting. Empirical estimates vary across studies, but the finding that β is below 1 has been confirmed repeatedly."
/>

Ask someone with $\beta=0.7$ and $\delta=1$ (no long-run discounting, for simplicity) the following, in units of 10,000 won.

- **Asked on 1 January**: "100,000 won on 1 March" vs "110,000 won on 2 March" → both are in the future, so both are discounted by $\beta$: $0.7\times 10 = 7$ vs $0.7\times 11=7.7$. The person picks 110,000 won.
- **Asked again on 1 March**: "100,000 won today" vs "110,000 won tomorrow" → today's amount is not discounted: $10$ vs $0.7\times 11 = 7.7$. The person picks 100,000 won.

The same person reversed their preference over the same two options just because the timing changed. This is **time inconsistency**. It is the structure behind the repeated "starting tomorrow" in dieting, exercise, saving and work with deadlines.

## 3. Naivety and sophistication

Behaviour differs depending on whether a present-biased person knows that their future self will also be present-biased.

<LectureTable
  title="Naive and sophisticated agents"
  headers={["Type", "Belief about future self", "Behaviour"]}
  rows={[
    ["Naive", "Tomorrow's self will follow the plan", "Keeps procrastinating; does not buy commitment devices"],
    ["Sophisticated", "Tomorrow's self will also procrastinate", "Buys commitment devices; sometimes acts early"],
  ]}
/>

O'Donoghue and Rabin's example: you must see one of four films, each better than the last, but can see only one. A naive person may keep saying "I'll see a better one next week" and miss them all. A sophisticated person, knowing their future self will procrastinate, actually decides early.

## 4. Gym memberships: evidence from the field

DellaVigna and Malmendier (2006) showed with US gym membership data that monthly members, dividing their fees by actual visits, paid about 17 dollars per visit. A 10-visit pass (10 dollars a visit) would have been cheaper. When choosing the monthly plan they expected to go often but went less. This is evidence consistent with **naive present bias**, which overestimates future self-control.

## 5. Commitment devices and saving design

Sophisticated people pay for **commitment devices** that restrict their own future choices: savings products with withdrawal restrictions, contracts that forfeit money if a goal is missed, Christmas savings clubs. In the standard model there is no reason to pay for a contract that removes options, so demand for commitment devices is itself evidence of present bias.

Thaler and Benartzi's **Save More Tomorrow (SMarT)** programme used present bias and loss aversion together. Employees committed in advance to raising their saving rate not now, but from their next pay rise.

- Because the increase is in the future, present bias bites less (no sacrifice now).
- Because it comes out of the pay rise, take-home pay does not fall, so it does not feel like a loss.
- Participants could leave at any time, but because of status quo bias (Chapter 3), most stayed.

At the first firm to adopt it, participants' average saving rate rose from 3.5% to 13.6% over about 40 months.

<Callout type="warning">
Estimates of β are widely scattered, from the 0.6s to nearly 1, depending on the task and method. Experiments that use money as the reward in particular may understate present bias, because people do not consume money as soon as they receive it. Studies that measure consumption or effort (completing tasks) tend to report clearer present bias.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

A person with $\beta=0.8$ and $\delta=1$ faces a choice with a cost of 6 today (the pain of exercise) and a benefit of 7 tomorrow (health). Deciding today? $-6+0.8\times 7=-0.4<0$, so the person does not exercise. Had they made the plan yesterday, $0.8\times(-6+7)=0.8>0$, so they would have planned "I'll exercise tomorrow." They make plans but postpone carrying them out.
