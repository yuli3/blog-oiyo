## What Is Financial Management?

**Financial management** studies how a firm raises capital and how it deploys that capital, all in service of maximizing the firm's value.

- **Financial management in the broad sense**: **corporate finance** (real-asset decisions) plus **investments** (financial-asset decisions)
- **Financial management in the narrow sense**: corporate finance specifically

This series covers the narrow sense — the financial decisions firms make.

---

## The Three Pillars of Financial Decision-Making

A firm's financial activity is a cycle: raise capital in the financial markets → invest it in real assets → generate larger cash flows and return value to investors. Along the way, financial managers make three kinds of decisions.

### 1. The investment decision
Deciding which real assets to invest in. This determines the **size and composition of the firm's assets** (the asset side of the balance sheet).

### 2. The financing decision
Deciding how to raise the funds needed for those investments. This determines the **size and mix of debt and equity** (the liabilities-and-equity side of the balance sheet).

### 3. The dividend decision
Deciding whether cash generated from operations should be kept as **retained earnings** for reinvestment or paid out to shareholders as **dividends**. Broadly speaking, this is itself a form of financing decision, since retained earnings are internally generated financing.

## Securities Markets and Financial Assets

### Real assets vs. financial assets
- **Real assets**: a broad category covering visible physical assets plus human capital
- **Financial assets**: securities such as stocks, bonds, and derivatives — conceptually, **claims on the cash flows that real assets generate**

### What securities markets do
1. **Connect** suppliers of capital with those who need it
2. Make the **separation of ownership and management** possible for firms
3. **Allocate resources** efficiently across the economy

## The Firm's Goal: Maximizing Firm Value

In financial management, the firm's goal is to **maximize firm value** — the total value of its assets.

```
Firm value = Σ (present value of future cash flows)
           = market value of equity + market value of debt
⇒ Maximizing firm value = maximizing shareholder wealth = maximizing the share price
```

### Why "value maximization," not "profit maximization"
Treating profit maximization as the goal creates three problems.

1. **Accounting profit is arbitrary** — profit varies depending on accounting treatment.
2. **It ignores the timing of cash flows** — when a cash flow arrives matters just as much as its amount (the time value of money — covered in Chapter 2).
3. **It ignores differences in risk** — profit maximization says nothing about how uncertain those future profits are.

Value maximization is the more defensible goal because it accounts for **cash flow, timing, and risk** together.

## Shareholders and Managers: The Agency Problem

When ownership (shareholders) and management (managers) are separated, decision-making authority is delegated from the **principal** (shareholders) to the **agent** (managers). When their interests diverge, this creates the **agency problem**.

**Common forms**
- Managers consuming perquisites or pursuing empire-building growth for its own sake
- Short tenure horizons pushing managers toward short-term profit
- Complacency and excessive risk aversion

**Mechanisms that mitigate it**
- Shareholders elect a **board of directors** to monitor management indirectly
- Incentive compensation such as **stock options** to align interests
- The threat of **takeovers (M&A)** creating pressure to replace underperforming management
- Competition in the market for managerial talent

**Agency costs** = the costs of dealing with this problem
→ monitoring costs + bonding costs + residual loss

## Legal Forms of the Firm

Corporate law generally recognizes several legal forms of business organization — for example, Korea's Commercial Act distinguishes the stock corporation, general partnership, limited partnership, and limited liability company. These forms differ in owners' **liability limits** and **tax treatment**. Modern corporate finance is concerned primarily with the publicly traded **stock corporation**.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. Financial management covers **raising and deploying capital**; in the narrow sense, it means corporate finance.
2. It centers on three decisions — **investment, financing, and dividends**.
3. A firm's goal is not profit but **maximizing firm value** (equivalently, shareholder wealth, equivalently, the share price).
4. Value maximization accounts for **cash flow, timing, and risk** together.
5. Separating ownership from management creates the **agency problem**, which is mitigated through the board, incentive pay, and the threat of takeover.
