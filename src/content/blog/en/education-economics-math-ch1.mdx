# Mathematics for Economics — The Derivative Is the Grammar of the Margin

This course is not an introduction to calculus. It is for people who have seen derivatives at university and want to pin down again **which derivative is which economic quantity** in demand, cost and profit equations. Integration is in [chapter 2](/en/education-economics-math-ch2/), constraints in [chapter 3](/en/education-economics-math-ch3/), dynamics in [chapter 4](/en/education-economics-math-ch4/) and matrices in [chapter 5](/en/education-economics-math-ch5/).

## 1. The margin is not the average

With demand $Q=100-2P$, quantity falls 2 units for each 1-won rise in price. Because the function is linear, this slope of $-2$ is the same everywhere, not just at one point. If total cost is $\mathrm{TC}=10+6Q+Q^2$ (in units of 10,000 won), then

<FormulaBox
  label="Marginal cost"
  formula="MC = dTC/dQ = 6 + 2Q"
  description="At Q=4, MC=14. Average cost ATC=TC/Q=10/Q+6+Q is 12.5 at Q=4. The margin and the average diverge."
/>

At $Q=4$, the cost of making one more unit is 140,000 won, not the average so far of 125,000 won. That is why the profit rule of section 2 uses the margin rather than the average.

Elasticity is a derivative with the units removed.

<FormulaBox
  label="Point elasticity"
  formula="\varepsilon = (dQ/dP)\cdot(P/Q)"
  description="With Q=100-2P and P=20, Q=60 and ε=-2×(20/60)=-2/3. Since |ε|<1, a price rise at this point increases revenue."
/>

Revenue is $\mathrm{TR}=P\cdot Q=20×60=1200$. At a price of 21, $Q=58$ and $\mathrm{TR}=1218$, higher. This matches the calculation for the inelastic range.

## 2. Profit maximization looks at the first and second order together

With $\pi=\mathrm{TR}-\mathrm{TC}$, $\mathrm{TR}=40Q$ and $\mathrm{TC}=10+6Q+Q^2$, we get $\pi=34Q-Q^2-10$. $d\pi/dQ=34-2Q=0$ gives $Q=17$, and $d^2\pi/dQ^2=-2<0$, so it is a maximum. It is the same point where $\mathrm{MC}=6+2Q$ meets the price of 40.

<Callout type="warning">
Solving only the first-order condition can also pick up a minimum. If $\mathrm{TC}$ is in a range of decreasing marginal cost, MC falls and can meet MR a second time. That point is close to a profit minimum. Do not memorize equations without the graph.
</Callout>

The chain rule applies directly to composite production. If $Q=F(K(t))$, then $dQ/dt=F'(K)\cdot K'(t)$. When capital changes over time, the change in output is the product of the marginal product and investment. This connects to the optimization of chapter 3.

## Check your understanding

What is the optimal quantity for a competitive firm with $\mathrm{TC}=100+20Q+Q^2$ (in units of 10,000 won) and $P=80$? $\mathrm{MC}=20+2Q=80$ gives $Q=30$. Then $\mathrm{ATC}=100/30+20+30≈53.3$, so profit per unit is about 26.7 and total profit 800 (8 million won). Choose the point where $P=\mathrm{MC}$, not the point of minimum average cost.
