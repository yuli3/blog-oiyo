# Human resource management — without strategic alignment, HR remains administration

[Principles of management](/en/education-business-ch1/) groups planning, organizing, leading, and controlling together. A plan cannot become practice unless the people systems support it. This chapter is about **aligning those systems**. Motivation and perception are separate organizational-behavior topics; later chapters examine job design, hiring, and rewards in detail.

## 1. Administration becomes strategic when the question changes

Personnel administration keeps hiring records and payroll running. Strategic HRM first asks **which employee behaviors the business needs**, then connects hiring, appraisal, and rewards to those behaviors. If people are treated only as a cost, training becomes an easy budget cut. If every people program is treated as an investment, programs without a plausible return accumulate. Neither framing is a substitute for testing the underlying assumptions.

Ulrich's strategic partner, administrative expert, employee champion, and change agent are descriptions of HR roles. Memorizing the four labels does not make HR a strategic partner. A business discussion also needs evidence about turnover, time to fill positions, and capability gaps.

## 2. Vertical and horizontal alignment work together

Vertical alignment runs from business strategy to required capabilities to HR policy. A cost-leadership strategy may emphasize reliable processes and retention of operational skill. A differentiation strategy needs room for experimentation and learning; an appraisal system that automatically penalizes unsuccessful experiments works against it.

Horizontal alignment asks whether HR practices reinforce the same behaviors. Suppose recruitment selects people for innovation, but attendance accounts for 70% of their appraisal. Those employees may adapt to the appraisal or leave. The functions are sending contradictory signals.

## 3. Treat alignment as a testable proposition, not a slogan

Consider two hypothetical business units that both aim to innovate. Unit A assigns 70% of its appraisal score to attendance and cost reduction. Unit B rewards documented experiments, collaboration, and customer validation. If they produce 12 and 18 new-product proposals respectively, B has a clearer **connection between appraisal criteria and strategy**. Proposal counts alone cannot establish that B's HR system is better: team size, market opportunities, and proposal quality may differ.

In practice, state the behaviors the strategy requires, then inspect what appraisal and rewards actually reinforce. Examine not only the **number of proposals but their validation and adoption rates**, retention of critical staff, and capability gaps after hiring. Before comparing outcomes across a policy change, check whether the business environment or workforce composition also changed. This is a way to test an alignment hypothesis, not an experiment that automatically proves the policy caused the outcome.

<Callout type="warning">
“Strategic HR” alone does not explain business performance. A severe market downturn can overwhelm well-aligned HR practices. If the business strategy itself is wrong, alignment can entrench the wrong behavior more quickly. That boundary needs to be considered alongside the design of any HR system.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

Seven of a differentiation unit's ten appraisal criteria reward attendance and cost cutting. New-product proposals fall from 12 a year to 3, even though recruitment adverts promise an innovative workplace. Where is the misalignment? The appraisal criteria conflict with the differentiation strategy: vertical alignment has broken down. The figures make the conflict worth investigating; they do not, on their own, prove that appraisal caused the decline.
