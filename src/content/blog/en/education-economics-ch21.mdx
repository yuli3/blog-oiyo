# Macroeconomics — A Recession Has One Name, but Shocks Come from Two Directions

The fact that output has fallen below potential does not by itself tell us what to do. Depending on whether aggregate demand or aggregate supply has fallen, prices move in opposite directions and the right policy differs. This chapter uses the AD-AS model of [Chapter 8](/en/education-economics-ch19/) as a diagnostic tool for business cycles.

A supply shock puts policymakers in a dilemma. Raising aggregate demand restores output but pushes prices up further, and if expected inflation rises, the Phillips curve of [Chapter 9](/en/education-economics-ch20/) shifts up and the cost grows. Cutting aggregate demand controls prices but deepens the recession. If the shock is temporary and expectations are anchored, "looking through" it is also an option. In the 1970s expectations followed inflation up, inflation became entrenched, and it could only be brought down at the cost of a deep recession.

## 3. What causes fluctuations: two views

**Real business cycle (RBC) theory** sees technology (productivity) shocks as the main source of fluctuations. Prices are flexible, and business cycles are the result of people responding optimally to changes in productivity. Since even recessions are efficient responses, there is little role for stabilization policy. The 1982 paper by Kydland and Prescott is the starting point.

**New Keynesian economics** explicitly models price and wage stickiness and imperfect competition. Aggregate demand shocks then move output, recessions are inefficient and monetary policy can raise welfare. The dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models used by central banks today combine the RBC methodology (optimizing households and firms, rational expectations) with New Keynesian rigidities.

## 4. Propagation: how small shocks become large fluctuations

Recent research focuses less on the shocks themselves than on **amplification and persistence**.

- **Financial accelerator**: when asset prices fall, collateral values shrink, borrowing becomes harder, investment falls and asset prices fall further. This is the same structure as crisis amplification in [Financial Economics Chapter 5](/en/education-economics-ch38/).
- **Labour market search**: after jobs are destroyed it takes time to find new ones, so unemployment persists ([Chapter 20](/en/education-economics-ch31/)).
- **Hysteresis**: when the long-term unemployed lose skills, cyclical unemployment turns into structural unemployment and potential output itself falls.

<Callout type="warning">
Do not apply the prescription for an average business cycle to this particular one. The 2008 crisis was a finance-driven demand shock; the 2021–2022 surge in prices combined supply disruptions, energy prices and a strong recovery in demand. "It's a recession, so cut rates" is a sentence that has not checked the direction of the shock. In real data several shocks arrive at once, so look at expected inflation, wages and commodity prices as well as the signs of output and prices.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

In one year output falls by 2% and prices rise by 3%. Which shock dominated, and what happens if policy responds with demand expansion? Output and prices moved in opposite directions, so an adverse supply shock dominated. Raising aggregate demand to restore output would push prices up by more than 3%, and if expected inflation follows, a larger cost must be paid later. A single demand policy cannot return both variables to where they were.
