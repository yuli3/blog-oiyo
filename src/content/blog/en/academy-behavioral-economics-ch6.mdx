# Behavioral Economics — Put a Label on Money and the Same Money Is Spent Differently

In the standard model, money is **fungible**. 100,000 won received as salary and 100,000 won won in a lottery, 100,000 won in a food account and 100,000 won in a travel account, are all part of the same budget constraint. Thaler called **mental accounting** the way people put money into different "accounts" by source, purpose and timing, and spend each account by different rules.

## 2. Transaction utility: the pleasure of a good deal

Thaler split the value of a purchase into two parts.

<FormulaBox
  label="Acquisition utility and transaction utility"
  formula="\text{Total utility} = \underbrace{v(\bar{p}) - v(p)}_{\text{acquisition utility}} + \underbrace{v(p^{*}) - v(p)}_{\text{transaction utility}}"
  description="p is the price paid, p̄ the price equivalent to the value of the item to me, and p* the reference price (the price I expect to be fair)."
/>

A friend offers to fetch a beer for someone lying on a beach and asks how much they would pay. When the beer was to come from a fancy resort hotel, the median willingness to pay was 2.65 dollars; from a small grocery store, 1.50 dollars (Thaler 1985). The beer drunk and the place it is drunk are the same, so acquisition utility is the same. The hotel's reference price is higher, so the same price feels less like a "rip-off."

Transaction utility explains why sales and labels such as "X% off the list price" are so powerful. People buy things they do not need when transaction utility is large.

## 3. Hedonic editing: segregate or integrate?

Using the shape of the value function from Chapter 2, total value depends on whether outcomes are viewed together or separately.

- **Segregate gains**: the gain domain is concave, so $v(50)+v(50) > v(100)$. Giving gifts in several instalments brings more pleasure than giving them all at once.
- **Integrate losses**: the loss domain is convex, so $v(-100) > v(-50)+v(-50)$. Paying one combined bill hurts less than paying several charges.
- **Separate a small gain from a large loss**: a small gain mixed into a large loss is barely felt. Treated separately as a "silver lining," it is worth much more (a cash rebate after buying a car).

With the estimate from Chapter 2 ($\alpha=0.88$), $v(50)+v(50)\approx 62.5$ and $v(100)\approx 57.5$. Splitting the same 100 into two brings about 9% more pleasure.

## 4. The sunk cost effect

Costs already incurred and unrecoverable should not affect future choices. In practice, the more people have already paid, the more they want to keep using the project or item.

Arkes and Blumer (1985) randomly divided buyers of season tickets to a university theatre into groups paying full price, a 13% discount and a 47% discount. In the first half of the season, the full-price group attended more performances. The same performances, similar people — they went more just because they had paid more. The effect faded in the second half of the season.

In mental accounting terms, buying a ticket records a loss in a "theatre account." Attending closes the account and offsets the loss; not attending confirms the loss. Combined with the loss aversion of Chapter 2, people keep making low-value choices to avoid confirming a loss. Failed large projects by firms and governments that continue "because of everything we have already invested" have the same structure.

## 5. Income targets and narrow periods

Camerer et al. (1997) reported that New York taxi drivers quit early on busy days when their hourly earnings are high. This is the opposite of the standard labour supply model (work more when wages are high). One interpretation is that drivers use a daily income target as a reference point and stop once they reach it. Later reanalyses by Farber and others found the effect to be small or limited to some drivers, and the debate continues.

<Callout type="warning">
Taking sunk costs into account is not always an error. If there are reasons that affect future value — reputation (quitting loses trust), learning (going further reveals the value) or self-signalling (I am someone who finishes things) — continuing can be rational. The error is when past spending itself becomes the basis for the decision.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

You bought a 100,000-won concert ticket and on the day there is a downpour. If the value of attending (net of the inconvenience of getting there) is 60,000 won, should you go? The 100,000 won already paid will not come back whether you go or not, so the comparison is between "60,000 won gained by going" and "0 won by not going." The net value is positive, so you should go. If the net value were −20,000 won, you should not go, and "I don't want to waste the 100,000 won" is not a reason.
