## The three parts of an argument

An argument isn't just a list of opinions — it's a set of reasons offered to make you accept a conclusion. The conclusion is the **claim**. What supports the claim is the **evidence**. And the idea that makes the argument work, even though it's never stated outright, is the **assumption**.

Take the sentence "This app has a lot of users, so it's a good app." The claim is "it's a good app," and the evidence is "it has a lot of users." The hidden assumption is "an app with a lot of users is generally good."

## A quick reference table

| Element | Question it answers | Example |
|------|------|------|
| Claim | What am I being asked to believe? | This policy is necessary |
| Evidence | Why does the author say so? | It cuts costs |
| Assumption | What idea is being taken for granted? | Cost-cutting is an important standard |
| Counterargument | What could be wrong with this? | What about effects beyond cost? |

Once you can make this split, both writing and speech become much easier to read. Even a forcefully worded sentence gets easier to examine once you break it into its structural parts.

## Why assumptions matter

Assumptions are the hidden pillars of an argument. Even when the evidence is true, a weak assumption can topple the conclusion. "A celebrity endorsed it, so it must be trustworthy" assumes that fame and reliability go together — but if the celebrity has no relevant expertise, that assumption is weak.

To find an assumption, ask: "What would have to be taken for granted for this evidence to actually support this conclusion?"

## A short exercise

Let's break down "This lecture has a lot of views, so it must be worth learning from." The claim is that it's worth learning from; the evidence is that it has a lot of views; the assumption is that a highly viewed lecture is a valuable one. Now we can ask whether view count is really a good indicator of learning value.

## Key takeaways

- The claim is the conclusion you're being asked to accept.
- The evidence is the reasons or data offered to support the claim.
- The assumption is the idea that must be implicitly accepted for the argument to hold.
- Finding the assumption helps you spot the weak points in claims that sound plausible on the surface.
