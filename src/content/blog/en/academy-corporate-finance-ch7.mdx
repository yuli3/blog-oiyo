## The Long-Term Financing Decision

**Long-term financing** raises capital through financial instruments with maturities of a year or more. Equity and corporate bonds are the two main forms, and the market where they trade is the **capital market**.

As firms grow, they face decisions that tie up capital for extended periods — capital expenditure, R&D, mergers and acquisitions, and international expansion. These long-term investments are hard to fund with short-term borrowing alone, so stable long-term capital is needed.

Choosing among long-term financing tools requires weighing at least two considerations.

1. The potential impact on control of the firm
2. The potential impact on the stability of the financial structure

Equity carries no repayment obligation, but it can dilute ownership and shift control. Debt doesn't dilute ownership, but it adds the burden of interest and principal repayment.

## Common Stock and Preferred Stock

**Common stock** has no maturity date, and — barring liquidation — carries no claim to repayment of principal. Common shareholders receive dividends that vary with the firm's performance and vote at shareholder meetings.

The main forms of common stock include:

| Type | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Common stock | The basic share class, carrying voting rights and a residual claim |
| Non-voting common stock | Shares that receive dividends in exchange for limited voting rights |
| Par-value stock | Shares issued with a stated par value |
| No-par stock | Shares issued without a stated par value |

**Preferred stock** has priority over common stock for dividends and in the distribution of remaining assets. It usually carries no voting rights, but because it typically pays a fixed dividend, it has characteristics that sit between debt and equity.

The detailed types of preferred stock include:

| Type | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Voting preferred stock | Preferred stock that, unlike most preferred shares, carries voting rights like common stock |
| Cumulative preferred stock | Unpaid dividends accumulate and must be paid in later years |
| Non-cumulative preferred stock | Unpaid dividends do not carry forward |
| Redeemable (callable) preferred stock | Preferred stock the company can redeem under agreed terms |
| Convertible preferred stock | Preferred stock with the right to convert into common stock |

The price gap between discounted non-voting preferred shares and common shares can be interpreted as a voting-right premium.

## Types of Corporate Bonds

A **corporate bond** is a debt security a firm issues to raise long-term capital. Investors lend money to the company, and the firm takes on an obligation to pay agreed interest and repay principal.

### Interest payment structure

| Type | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Coupon bond | Pays interest periodically until maturity |
| Zero-coupon bond | Pays no interest until maturity; issued at a discount and repays principal at maturity |

### Repayment structure

| Type | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Callable bond | Carries a provision letting the firm repay principal and interest before maturity |
| Sinking-fund bond | Requires the firm to periodically set aside funds for repayment at maturity |
| Perpetual bond | A hybrid security that pays interest indefinitely with no maturity date |

A perpetual bond has bond-like characteristics because it pays interest, and equity-like characteristics because it has no maturity — making it a **hybrid security**.

## Equity-Linked Corporate Bonds

Attaching equity-related rights to an ordinary bond changes its financing cost, its dilution effect, and its appeal to investors.

| Type | Core structure | Financial effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Convertible bond (CB) | Carries the right to convert the bond into common stock | Converting turns debt into equity |
| Bond with warrants (BW) | Carries the right to purchase a set number of new shares | Exercising the warrant brings in additional cash |
| Exchangeable bond (EB) | Carries the right to exchange for shares the firm already holds | Exchanged using existing shares, without issuing new ones |

Convertible bonds let a firm raise capital at a lower cost than issuing common stock outright. Investors earn interest income while retaining the option to realize a gain by converting if the share price rises.

With bonds with warrants, the right to repayment of principal and interest is separate from the right to purchase new shares. Exercising the warrant brings in additional cash equal to the new shares issued, but it can dilute existing shareholders.

## Issuance Methods and Capital Increases

Securities issuance varies by who the buyers are and who bears underwriting risk.

| Method | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Private placement | Sold through private negotiation to fewer than 50 investors |
| Public offering | Issued to an unspecified group of investors |
| Best-efforts underwriting | The issuing firm bears the risk of any unsold balance |
| Standby underwriting | The underwriter bears the risk of any unsold balance |
| Firm-commitment underwriting | The underwriter buys the entire issue and resells it at its own risk |

A **seasoned equity offering** (rights issue) increases paid-in capital through an inflow of new cash from investors.

| Type of offering | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Rights offering to existing shareholders | New shares are allocated to current shareholders and employee stock ownership plans |
| Third-party allotment | Shares are allocated to specific institutional investors or business partners |
| General public offering | Existing shareholders' preemptive rights are set aside and shares are offered to the general public |
| Shareholder-priority public offering | Shares are first offered to existing shareholders, with any unsubscribed shares then offered to the public |

Existing shareholders are often given preemptive rights because new shares are typically issued at a discount to the market price, and allocating them to the general public first would dilute existing shareholders' ownership and wealth.

A **bonus issue** increases share capital without any actual cash inflow, by converting reserves on the capital account into paid-in capital. Stock dividends and new shares issued upon conversion of convertible bonds can also increase paid-in capital in form, without a cash inflow.

## The Concept of Working Capital

**Net working capital** is current assets minus current liabilities.

```
Net working capital = current assets - current liabilities
```

Working capital management is decision-making about holding current assets and financing them in the short term. It covers:

1. Day-to-day operating activity aimed at growing revenue
2. Activity aimed at maintaining adequate cash
3. Purchasing raw materials and managing inventory

The goal is to maximize firm value while maintaining the ability to meet short-term obligations — that is, liquidity.

---

## The Operating Cycle and the Cash Conversion Cycle

The **operating cycle** is the period from when raw materials arrive to when accounts receivable are collected in cash.

```
Operating cycle = inventory period + accounts receivable collection period
```

The **cash conversion cycle** is the period from when cash goes out to pay for raw materials, to when cash comes back in from selling the finished product.

```
Cash conversion cycle = operating cycle - accounts payable credit period
                       = inventory period + receivable collection period - payable payment period
```

Efficient working capital management means shortening this cash conversion cycle: collecting receivables quickly, turning over inventory fast, and paying what's owed as late as the credit terms allow.

## Working Capital Financing Policy

Current assets can be split by their nature.

| Category | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Permanent current assets | The baseline level of current assets a firm must always hold |
| Temporary current assets | Current assets that fluctuate with seasonal changes in production or sales |

As a basic rule, assets and liabilities should be financed under a **maturity-matching principle**: assets needed long-term should be financed with long-term capital, and assets needed temporarily should be financed with short-term capital.

Short-term financial policy broadly splits into aggressive and conservative approaches.

| Policy | Characteristics | Advantage | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aggressive policy | Minimizes current assets, relies heavily on current liabilities | Lower financing costs, higher profitability | Weaker liquidity |
| Conservative policy | Holds more current assets, relies more on long-term capital | More stable liquidity | Higher long-term cost of capital |

A flexible short-term financial policy holds more current assets and uses less current liabilities; a restrictive policy does the opposite.

## Accounts Receivable Management and Financing

**Accounts receivable** are the unpaid portion of sales, such as trade receivables and notes receivable. They make up a large share of working capital, which is why managing them matters.

Key considerations in receivables management include:

1. **Credit standards**: the criteria for deciding which customers can buy on credit
2. **Credit period**: how long customers are allowed before payment is due
3. **Cash discount policy**: discounts offered to encourage early payment

There are also financing tools built around accounts receivable.

| Tool | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Trade credit insurance | Pays out if a buyer defaults on its obligation |
| Factoring | Selling receivables to a third party for immediate cash |
| Receivables-backed loan | Short-term financing secured by receivables as collateral |

Working capital management is a balance between profitability and liquidity. Holding too much liquidity leaves idle funds; holding too little raises the risk of insolvency.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. Long-term financing raises capital for growth investment through instruments with maturities of a year or more, chiefly equity and corporate bonds.
2. Common stock carries voting rights and a residual claim, while preferred stock has priority for dividends and asset distribution.
3. Corporate bonds come in many forms depending on their interest structure, repayment terms, equity-linked rights, and issuance method.
4. Net working capital is current assets minus current liabilities, and the goal is to balance firm value with liquidity.
5. Shortening the cash conversion cycle means reducing the inventory and receivables collection periods while appropriately extending the payables payment period.
