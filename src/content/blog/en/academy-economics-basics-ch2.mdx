# Introduction to Economics — A Change in Price and a Shift in the Curve Are Different Events

The law of demand is not "people buy less when things are expensive." It is a conditional statement: **other things being equal**, when a good's own price rises, the quantity demanded of it falls. If income changes at the same time, that condition is broken.

## 1. Movements along the curve and shifts of the curve

A change in the good's own price is a movement to another point on the same demand curve (a change in quantity demanded). Income, the prices of substitutes and complements, expectations and preferences shift the curve itself. The same applies to supply: input prices and technology move the supply curve, while the selling price changes the quantity supplied.

In an interview, "demand for housing fell because interest rates rose" is closer to saying that borrowing costs changed the relative price of housing (a shift in demand). "Transactions fell because house prices rose" is a movement along the same demand curve. Mixing the two in one sentence reverses the policy prescription.

With demand $Qd=100-2P$ and supply $Qs=10+P$, equilibrium is $P=30$, $Q=40$. If higher income shifts demand to $Qd=112-2P$, then $P=34$, $Q=44$: price and quantity rise together. If a supply shock makes supply $Qs=4+P$, then $P=32$, $Q=36$: price rises and quantity falls, which distinguishes it from a demand shift.

## 2. Elasticity is a unit-free response

<FormulaBox
  label="Price elasticity of demand"
  formula="|\varepsilon| = |(\Delta Q/Q) / (\Delta P/P)|"
  description="Using the midpoint (arc) elasticity gives the same value whichever point you start from. Above 1, demand is elastic."
/>

If price falls from 10 won to 9 won and quantity rises from 100 to 120, the midpoints are a price of 9.5 and a quantity of 110, so $|ε|=(20/110)/(1/9.5)≈1.73$. When demand is elastic, a price cut raises total revenue: from $10×100$ = 1,000 won to $9×120$ = 1,080 won. In an inelastic range, the same cut reduces revenue.

When supply is inelastic, most of a demand shift goes into price. That is the first diagnosis of housing and commodity booms. In the long run supply is more elastic, so the price effect of the same demand shock is smaller.

## 3. A price ceiling turns shortage into rationing

If equilibrium is 30 won and a price ceiling is set at 20 won, $Qd=60$ and $Qs=30$: a shortage of 30. Queues, black markets and lower quality become the rationing devices in place of price. The intuition that consumer surplus will rise is true only for those who actually get to buy. Subtract the losses of those who cannot and the fall in producer surplus, and a deadweight loss remains.

<Callout type="warning">
An upward-sloping demand curve (a Giffen good) is possible as a counterexample to the law, but only if the income effect outweighs the substitution effect and the good is inferior. Rarity of observation does not overturn the definition. Conversely, "if observed price-quantity points slope upward, demand slopes upward" is an identification error that reads a shift in supply as demand.
</Callout>

Consumer surplus is the sum of willingness to pay minus price, and producer surplus is the sum of price minus marginal cost. The proposition that competitive equilibrium maximizes the sum of the two holds only under **perfect competition, no externalities and perfect information**. Chapters 5 and 6 cover where these assumptions break down.

## Check your understanding

When the price of coffee rose from 4,000 to 5,000 won, sales fell from 200 to 180 cups a day. Does the price rise increase the shop's revenue in this range? The midpoint elasticity is $|ε|=(20/190)/(1000/4500)≈0.47$, so demand is inelastic and revenue rises from 800,000 won to 900,000 won. If customers can leave in the long run or switch to other drinks, this elasticity grows.
