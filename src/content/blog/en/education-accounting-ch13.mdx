Managerial accounting is the accounting information managers use to **plan, decide and control**. The course runs through decisions (chapters 1–3), planning and budgets (chapter 4), performance evaluation (chapters 5–6), long-term investment (chapter 7) and strategic cost management (chapter 8). How costs are calculated was covered in [Cost Accounting](/en/academy-cost-accounting-ch1/). This first chapter sets out **relevant costs**, the starting point of every decision analysis.

## 2. The two conditions for relevance

A cost is relevant to a decision only if it meets **both** conditions.

1. **It occurs in the future.** Money already spent cannot be recovered whatever you choose.
2. **It differs between alternatives.** An amount that is the same whichever alternative is chosen does not affect the comparison.

## 3. An equipment replacement decision

Company BI is weighing whether to keep a machine bought four years ago or replace it with a new one. Ignore the time value of money (long-term investment is covered in [chapter 7](/en/academy-managerial-accounting-ch7/)).

  title="Equipment replacement data (unit: ₩10,000)"

<FormulaBox
  label="Comparing relevant costs over four years"
  formula="\begin{aligned}\text{Keep} &= 300 \times 4 = 1{,}200 \\ \text{Replace} &= 900 - 100 + 150 \times 4 = 1{,}400\end{aligned}"
  description="In ₩10,000. Replacing costs ₩2 million more over four years, so keeping the existing machine is better."
/>

The existing machine's ₩4 million carrying amount is a **sunk cost** and enters neither side. Replacing it would record a ₩3 million loss on disposal, but that loss merely reveals a cost paid four years ago; keeping the machine turns the same ₩4 million into depreciation expense over time. By contrast, the **₩1 million receivable if sold now** is given up by keeping the machine, so it is relevant.

## 4. Incremental analysis and qualitative factors

Gathering only the relevant items to see the difference between two alternatives is **incremental analysis**. For the decision above:

- Incremental cost of replacing: new machine ₩9 million
- Incremental benefits of replacing: disposal proceeds ₩1 million, operating cost savings of $150 \times 4 = 600$ (₩6 million)
- Net effect: $700 - 900 = -200$ (−₩2 million)

Look at factors beyond the numbers too. If the new machine improves quality and reduces returns, or the old machine carries a high risk of breakdown, the conclusion could change. Relevant cost analysis sets out precisely what can be expressed in numbers and so provides a baseline for such judgements.

<Callout type="warning">
Being swayed by sunk costs is the most common error. The thought "we've already spent ₩4 million, so we must get our money's worth" distorts decisions. Opportunity costs, on the other hand, are easily left out because they never appear in the books. In a decision to use a warehouse yourself, forgetting the rent you could earn by leasing it out makes own use look free. Allocated common fixed costs are also usually unavoidable and therefore not relevant ([chapter 2](/en/education-accounting-ch16/)).
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

Company BJ wants to dispose of 1,000 obsolete parts left in inventory (carrying amount ₩20,000 each) in one of two ways. Sold as scrap, they fetch ₩3,000 each. Modified at a cost of ₩5,000 each, they can be sold for ₩10,000 each. Which is better, and how is the ₩20 million carrying amount treated?

The ₩20 million carrying amount is a sunk cost and irrelevant to both alternatives. Selling as scrap brings a net inflow of $1{,}000 \times 3{,}000$ = ₩3 million; modifying and selling brings $1{,}000 \times (10{,}000 - 5{,}000)$ = ₩5 million. Modifying is ₩2 million better. Both show a loss in the books, but modifying is the one that reduces it.
