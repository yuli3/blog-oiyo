## Moving together doesn't mean one causes the other

Causal reasoning is the process of judging why something happened. The most common trap here is mistaking correlation for causation. Just because two things happen together doesn't mean one causes the other.

For instance, ice cream sales and swimming accidents might rise together. But ice cream isn't the cause of the accidents — hot weather is more likely influencing both at once.

## Major traps in causal judgment

| Trap | Description | Question to ask |
|------|------|------|
| Third variable | Another factor explains both | Is there a hidden factor? |
| Reverse causation | The direction of cause and effect is flipped | Could the "effect" actually be causing the "cause"? |
| Selection bias | The observed group is skewed | Who was included, and who was left out? |
| Single-cause thinking | Explaining a complex problem with one cause | Are there other causes at play? |

To claim causation, you need timing, an actual relationship between the two, other explanations ruled out, and a plausible mechanism for how one affects the other.

## Good causal questions

When you hear "A caused B," ask four things: Did A come before B? Do A and B actually move together? Have other explanations been ruled out? Is there an explained process for how A affects B?

These questions are useful not only in academic research but also in analyzing business performance, evaluating study methods, and judging everyday habits.

## Example: study time and grades

Just because a student who studies longer gets better grades doesn't mean time alone is the cause. Study methods, prior ability, understanding of lessons, sleep, and exam difficulty can all play a role. So instead of asking only "how long," you also need to ask "in what way."

## Key takeaways

- Correlation means two things move together; causation means one actually influences the other.
- Third variables, reverse causation, and selection bias all cloud causal judgment.
- Checking timing and ruling out alternative explanations are at the core of causal reasoning.
- Explaining a complex phenomenon with a single cause weakens your judgment.
