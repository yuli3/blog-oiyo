# Business Strategy — Industry Structure, Resources, and Portfolio Choices

Strategy connects the customers a firm serves, the value it offers, and the activities needed to deliver that value. A list of strengths or a promising market is not yet a strategy: the choices must fit together and survive comparison with alternatives.

This chapter separates industry-level analysis, firm resources, activity links, and portfolio decisions. **All company numbers below are fictional teaching assumptions**, not market observations or recommended investment allocations. A framework helps organize evidence; it does not guarantee that competitors cannot respond.

---

## 1. From SWOT lists to testable choices

SWOT distinguishes internal strengths and weaknesses from external opportunities and threats. Crossing the categories can generate options, but it does not establish causation or rank those options. A claimed strength needs a relevant customer, a competitor benchmark, and evidence that its value exceeds the cost of maintaining it.

### (1) Cross-category options, not automatic prescriptions

The examples below are hypotheses to investigate. An SO option is not always aggressive marketing, and a WT option is not always an exit.

Compare the options using demand evidence, expected cash flows, implementation capacity, and the responses competitors could make. Do not turn four populated boxes into a claim that the strategy has been validated.

## 2. External Analysis: Porter's 5-Forces Model

Porter's Five Forces examines how industry structure affects profitability. It is not a fixed ranking of external conditions over internal efficiency, nor a prediction of every firm's profit. The relevant industry boundary, substitutes, switching costs, and the firm's position must be specified.

<LectureTable
  title="Analysis of 5 Competitive Forces"
  headers={["Force", "What to investigate"]}
  rows={[
    ["Rivalry", "Growth, differentiation, fixed costs, exit barriers, and the basis of competition"],
    ["Entry", "Scale, capital, switching costs, regulation, and likely incumbent responses"],
    ["Substitutes", "Other ways to meet the same need, their price/performance, and switching costs"],
    ["Supplier power", "Concentration, alternative inputs, switching costs, and contract conditions"],
    ["Buyer power", "Purchase concentration, differentiation, information, and integration options"],
  ]}
/>

For example, suppose annual input purchases are 200,000 USD and 60% of that spending is exposed to a supplier's 20% price increase. With quantities, input mix, exchange rates, and the period unchanged, extra purchasing cost is **200,000 × 0.60 × 0.20 = 24,000 USD**. A supplier's market share alone cannot supply the 60% exposure assumption. Inventory timing and selling-price changes are also needed before connecting this cost to profit.

## 3. Internal Analysis: VRIO and Value Chain

### (1) VRIO is a cumulative argument

For a specified resource, ask whether it is **valuable**, **rare**, **costly to imitate or replace**, and supported by an **organization able to capture its value**. A rare resource that customers do not value is not automatically an advantage. The table follows the cumulative decision path in Virginia Tech's 2025 analysis tool; it is a model interpretation, not a performance guarantee.

<LectureTable
  title="Cumulative VRIO conditions"
  headers={["Conditions established", "Model interpretation"]}
  rows={[
    ["V = No", "Competitive disadvantage"],
    ["V = Yes; R = No", "Competitive parity"],
    ["V/R = Yes; I = No", "Temporary competitive advantage"],
    ["V/R/I = Yes; O = No", "Potential advantage not fully exploited; organizational changes needed"],
    ["V/R/I/O = Yes", "Basis for sustained competitive advantage, subject to continued relevance"],
  ]}
/>

The earlier questions cannot be skipped when interpreting a later Yes. Organization includes decision rights, incentives, coordination, and the processes needed to use the resource. Demand and rivals can change, so rarity and imitation costs must be checked over time. The four labels are not four independent competitive positions.

### (2) Value-chain analysis connects activities

Value-chain analysis separates strategically important activities to ask where lower cost or greater customer value originates. The links matter too: improving one department's cost while raising costs elsewhere may leave the firm worse off. Suppliers and distribution channels form part of the wider value system.

<LectureProcess
  title="Fictional activity-link hypothesis"
  steps={[
    { label: "Purchasing", description: "Specify component quality rather than choose only the lowest purchase price." },
    { label: "Operations", description: "Test whether that specification reduces defects and rework." },
    { label: "Service", description: "Check whether fewer failures reduce repair cost and improve the customer experience." },
  ]}
/>

Suppose an improved component adds 2 USD per unit but reduces expected rework and repair cost by 3 USD. On those assumptions, the linked activities save 1 USD per unit before implementation costs. That saving is a hypothesis to test with defect and service records, not an observed result or proof of a sustainable advantage.

## 4. Business Portfolio Management: BCG Matrix

A growth-share matrix classifies business units by **market growth** and **relative market share**. Relative share compares a unit with its largest competitor in the same market, period, and measurement basis. It is not the unit's share of its own company's budget.

<FormulaBox

  formula="Relative market share = unit market share ÷ largest competitor's market share"
  description="If the unit is the market leader, compare it with the largest other competitor, not with itself. Use matching units or revenue measures."
/>

The horizontal axis in this diagram increases to the right. Classification follows the labeled axes, not a memorized left/right position. For the fictional table below only, high growth means at least 10% annual unit-volume growth, and high relative share means at least 1. These boundaries are teaching assumptions, not universal industry cutoffs.

<LectureTable
  title="Four fictional units, with the share calculation visible"
  headers={["Unit and market growth", "Relative share", "Classification"]}
  rows={[
    ["A: 12%", "20% ÷ 10% = 2", "Star"],
    ["B: 3%", "30% ÷ 15% = 2", "Cash cow"],
    ["C: 15%", "8% ÷ 40% = 0.2", "Question mark"],
    ["D: 1%", "5% ÷ 25% = 0.2", "Dog / pet"],
  ]}
/>

The framework prompts questions about investment, cash generation, and whether to develop, maintain, or leave a business. It does **not** establish a fixed 40/40/15/5 budget split. Actual allocation also needs cash-flow forecasts, investment returns, dependencies between units, and implementation constraints. Low growth and low share alone do not prove that closing a unit creates value; high share does not prove profitability.

<Callout type="note">
  **Separate classification from a decision.** A Star label, a VRIO checklist, or a filled SWOT table is not a recommendation by itself. Record the evidence, the assumptions that could fail, and the activities required to act on the conclusion.
</Callout>

## 5. Worked review questions

### (1) A share is not an allocation

**Question:** A unit has 5% market share and its largest competitor has 8%. Market growth is 12%. Apply the fictional cutoffs above. Does the result tell you to invest 15% of the company's budget?

**Solution:** Relative share is 5 ÷ 8 = **0.625**, below 1. With growth above 10%, it is a Question mark in this example. No budget percentage follows. The firm must assess the cost of gaining share, expected cash flows, rivals' responses, and the opportunity cost of other investments.

### (2) Organization cannot replace the other conditions

**Question:** A firm has excellent training and reporting systems, but its resource is widely available to competitors. Does O = Yes imply sustained advantage?

**Solution:** No. If the resource is valuable but not rare, the cumulative path indicates competitive parity. Organization helps the firm exploit a resource; it cannot make a common resource rare by itself. If value has not been established either, even parity is not supported by the argument.
