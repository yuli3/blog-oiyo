# Microeconomics — Consumers Equalise Marginal Utility per Won

If [Introduction to Economics, Chapter 3](/en/academy-economics-basics-ch3/) introduced the tangency condition, this chapter derives it, looks at the cases where it fails (corner solutions), and computes the two channels through which a price change alters demand (the substitution and income effects). Where the demand curve of Chapter 2 comes from is the conclusion of this chapter.

## 1. The budget constraint and indifference curves

A consumer with income $M$ facing prices $P_x$ and $P_y$ has the **budget constraint** $P_x x + P_y y \le M$. The slope of the budget line, $-P_x/P_y$, is the rate of exchange set by the market.

An **indifference curve** joins bundles that give the same utility. If preferences are monotone (more is better) and convex (mixtures are preferred to extremes), indifference curves slope downward, are convex to the origin and never cross. The absolute value of their slope is the **marginal rate of substitution (MRS)**: how much $y$ the consumer is willing to give up for one more unit of $x$.

<FormulaBox
  label="Marginal rate of substitution"
  formula="MRS_{xy} = -\frac{dy}{dx}\Big|_{u=\bar u} = \frac{MU_x}{MU_y}"
  description="Along an indifference curve the change in utility is MU_x dx + MU_y dy = 0, so the slope is the ratio of marginal utilities."
/>

## 2. Deriving the optimality condition

The Lagrangian for maximising utility $u(x,y)$ subject to the budget constraint is $\mathcal{L}=u(x,y)+\lambda(M-P_x x-P_y y)$. The first-order conditions are:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=\lambda P_x,\qquad \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=\lambda P_y$$

Dividing one by the other gives the optimality condition.

<FormulaBox
  label="Interior-solution condition"
  formula="MRS_{xy}=\frac{MU_x}{MU_y}=\frac{P_x}{P_y}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{MU_x}{P_x}=\frac{MU_y}{P_y}=\lambda"
  description="Marginal utility per won must be equal. If one is larger, moving spending towards that good raises utility. λ is the extra utility from one more won of income (the marginal utility of income)."
/>

With $MU_x=10$, $MU_y=4$, $P_x=2{,}000$ won and $P_y=1{,}000$ won, marginal utility per won is $1/200$ for $x$ and $1/250$ for $y$. Taking 1,000 won away from $y$ and spending it on $x$ raises utility. Because marginal utility diminishes, the gap narrows as spending shifts, and the equality is restored.

## 3. Cobb–Douglas demand

If $u=x^{a}y^{1-a}$, the first-order condition gives $\frac{a\,y}{(1-a)\,x}=\frac{P_x}{P_y}$; solving it together with the budget constraint yields the demand functions.

<FormulaBox
  label="Cobb–Douglas demand functions"
  formula="x^*=\frac{aM}{P_x},\qquad y^*=\frac{(1-a)M}{P_y}"
  description="A fixed share of income (a, 1−a) is spent on each good. The own-price elasticity is −1, the income elasticity 1 and the cross elasticity 0."
/>

With $u=xy$ ($a=1/2$), $M=12$ won, $P_x=2$ won and $P_y=1$ won, $x^*=3$, $y^*=6$ and utility is 18. Here $\lambda=MU_x/P_x=y/2=3$. If income rises to 13 won, $x=3.25$ and $y=6.5$, so utility becomes about 21.1 — an increase of about 3, consistent with $\lambda$ being the marginal utility of one won of income. The general interpretation of the Lagrange multiplier is in [Mathematics for Economists, Chapter 3](/en/education-economics-math-ch3/).

## 4. Corner solutions: when the equality fails

Depending on preferences, the optimum can lie on an axis.

- **Perfect substitutes** ($u=x+y$): indifference curves are straight lines. If $P_x<P_y$, the consumer buys only $x$. There is no interior tangency where the MRS (= 1) equals the price ratio.
- **Perfect complements** ($u=\min(x,y)$): indifference curves are L-shaped. The consumer always chooses the kink where $x=y$, a fixed ratio regardless of prices. The MRS is not defined there.

At a corner solution we can have $MU_x/P_x > MU_y/P_y$ with $y=0$. The first-order conditions become inequalities (the Kuhn–Tucker conditions).

## 5. Two channels of a price change: the Slutsky decomposition

When the price of $x$ rises, two things happen at once: $x$ becomes relatively more expensive, so the consumer shifts towards $y$ (the **substitution effect**), and the same income buys less (the **income effect**).

In the example above, if $P_x$ rises from 2 to 4 won, the new demand is $x=12/(2\times 4)=1.5$. Let us split the total effect of $-1.5$. To let the consumer afford the original bundle (3, 6) at the new prices, income must be compensated to $M'=4\times 3+6=18$ won. With this compensated income, demand is $x=18/8=2.25$.

- Substitution effect: 3 → 2.25, **−0.75**
- Income effect: 2.25 → 1.5, **−0.75**

There are two ways to compensate. **Slutsky compensation** lets the consumer afford the original bundle; **Hicks compensation** keeps the consumer on the original indifference curve. For small price changes the two coincide. When solving a problem, first check which compensation it uses.

The same decomposition applies to labour supply. A higher wage makes leisure more expensive, so people work more (substitution effect), but it also raises income, so people want more leisure (income effect). If the income effect dominates at high wages, the labour supply curve bends backwards. [Public Finance, Chapter 4](/en/academy-public-finance-ch4/) applies this logic to income tax.

<Callout type="warning">
Utility is ordinal: only the ranking matters. $u=xy$ and $u=\ln x+\ln y$ represent the same preferences and have the same MRS. "Utility has doubled" makes sense only when cardinal utility is assumed separately, as in choice under risk. And when goods can be bought only in whole units, or are rationed, the equality of marginal utility per won becomes an inequality.
</Callout>

Even without knowing the utility function, we can test choice data for consistency. If bundle B was chosen when A was affordable, then A must not be chosen when B is affordable (the weak axiom of revealed preference, WARP). Data that violate it cannot be rationalised by stable preferences.


## 6. Duality: maximising utility and minimising expenditure are two faces of one problem

So far we solved "the most utility from a given income". Turning it around — "the cheapest way to reach a given utility" — lands on the same tangency. The value functions of the two problems are the **indirect utility function** $V(P_x,P_y,M)$ and the **expenditure function** $e(P_x,P_y,u)$. For $u=xy$:

<FormulaBox
  label="Value functions for u = xy"
  formula="V=\frac{M^2}{4P_xP_y},\qquad e=2\sqrt{P_xP_y\,u},\qquad x^h=\sqrt{\frac{P_y\,u}{P_x}}"
  description="V plugs the optimal bundle into utility; e inverts V with respect to M. x^h is Hicksian (compensated) demand, holding utility fixed."
/>

Differentiating the value functions returns the demands, with no need to re-solve the optimisation — that is what duality is for.

<FormulaBox
  label="Roy's identity and Shephard's lemma"
  formula="x^*=-\frac{\partial V/\partial P_x}{\partial V/\partial M},\qquad x^h=\frac{\partial e}{\partial P_x}"
  description="For u=xy, Roy's identity gives (M²/4P_x²P_y)/(M/2P_xP_y)=M/2P_x, the Marshallian demand; Shephard's lemma gives √(P_y u/P_x), the Hicksian demand."
/>

In the example of section 5 ($M=12$, $P_y=1$), when $P_x$ rises from 2 to 4, Hicksian compensation puts the substitution effect at $x^h=\sqrt{18/4}\approx2.12$, i.e. $-0.88$ — slightly larger than the Slutsky figure of $-0.75$. The gap vanishes as the price change gets smaller.

### A. Measuring the cost of a price rise in money

There are three ways to put a money value on the consumer's loss from a price rise.

/>

For a normal good, $EV\le\Delta CS\le CV$ always holds (for a price rise). The gaps come from the income effect; the smaller it is, the closer the three measures. Cost-benefit analysis uses the area under Marshallian demand because the gap is usually small; for goods that take a large share of income, such as housing, computing CV or EV directly is more accurate.

## Check your understanding

**Problem 1.** With $u=xy$, $M=24$, $P_x=3$ and $P_y=2$, find the optimal bundle and $\lambda$.

**Solution.** $x^*=24/(2\times3)=4$ and $y^*=24/(2\times2)=6$. $\lambda=MU_x/P_x=y/3=2$, equal to $MU_y/P_y=x/2=2$: marginal utility per won is equalised across the two goods.

**Problem 2.** For the same consumer, $P_x$ rises from 3 to 6. Find the compensating variation (CV), the equivalent variation (EV) and the loss of consumer surplus, and check their order.

**Solution.** Initial utility is $V=24^2/(4\times3\times2)=24$ and final utility $24^2/(4\times6\times2)=12$. $CV=e(6,2,24)-24=2\sqrt{288}-24\approx9.94$ and $EV=24-e(3,2,12)=24-2\sqrt{72}\approx7.03$. The loss of consumer surplus is $\int_3^6 12/P\,dP=12\ln2\approx8.32$. So $EV<\Delta CS<CV$, the order expected for a normal good.
