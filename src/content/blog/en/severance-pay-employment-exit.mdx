### Introduction: More Than Just a Goodbye Check

Severance pay — the lump-sum payment employers offer departing employees — represents more than compensation for lost income. It's recognition of your tenure, a bridge to your next chapter, and, if handled strategically, the seed money for long-term financial security.

Unlike some countries where severance is legally mandated for all departures, severance in the US is largely discretionary — determined by company policy, employment contracts, and negotiation. That makes understanding the norms and levers of severance critically important. The difference between a poorly negotiated exit and a well-managed one can be tens of thousands of dollars.

<h2>2. Three Strategies to Maximize Your Exit Package</h2>

<h3>① Timing Your Departure Strategically</h3>
If you're being offered a package, timing matters. Departing right after a bonus pays out means more cash in your pocket before you leave. In layoff scenarios, companies typically calculate severance based on base salary — but your most recent W-2 earnings, including bonuses, can sometimes be used as the basis for negotiation. Ask explicitly how the calculation is made and whether bonuses factor in.

<h3>② Know What Else You're Owed</h3>
Severance is separate from other compensation you're entitled to:
- **Accrued but unused PTO**: Most states require employers to pay this out upon termination. Know your balance.
- **Prorated bonus**: If you're mid-performance period, some contracts include a prorated payout clause.
- **Vested equity**: Any vested RSUs, stock options, or ESPP shares are yours regardless of departure. Review your equity grant agreements carefully.

<h3>③ Roll Your Severance into an IRA or 401(k)</h3>
Severance is taxed as ordinary income in the year received. However, if you're also receiving a 401(k) distribution from a former employer, rolling it directly into a Traditional IRA or a new employer's 401(k) avoids immediate taxation. The rollover must be completed within 60 days. This isn't applicable to the severance payment itself — but structuring your broader exit well can defer tens of thousands in taxes.

## 3. Important Considerations Before You Sign

1. **The 21-day review period**: Under the OWBPA (Older Workers Benefit Protection Act), employees 40+ must be given at least 21 days to review a severance agreement and 7 days to revoke after signing. Younger employees have no federally mandated review period, but taking time to review with an attorney is always worthwhile.
2. **What you're waiving**: Most severance agreements include a release of claims — meaning you give up the right to sue the company for anything related to your employment. Understand what you're signing away before accepting.
3. **COBRA health coverage**: After losing employer-sponsored health insurance, you have 60 days to elect COBRA continuation coverage. Factor this cost (often \$500–\$800/month for an individual) into your financial bridge plan.

## Conclusion: The Next Chapter Starts Here

Your severance package, handled well, becomes the first financial brick of your next chapter. Whether it's a career pivot, entrepreneurial launch, or simply a needed pause — knowing the numbers, understanding your rights, and making tax-smart moves turns your departure into the foundation of a stronger future.
