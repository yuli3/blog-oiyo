# A company-wide WACC can reject worthwhile lower-risk investments

[Chapter 4](/en/education-finance-ch4/) covers CAPM and the cost of equity; [Chapter 6](/en/education-finance-ch6/) covers weighting equity and after-tax debt costs. Here we examine when those inputs do not match a project's risk.

## 1. A regression beta of 1.2 is an estimate from one sample

Assume a hypothetical risk-free rate of 3% and market risk premium of 5%. A beta of 1.2 estimated from five years of weekly returns gives $r_E=3\%+1.2×5\%=9\%$. With market-value debt weight $D/(D+E)=40\%$, pretax debt cost $r_D=6\%$, and tax rate $T_c=25\%$, the company WACC is **7.2%**. The tax rate is an example input, not a statement of current Korean tax law.

  description="D/V=40% differs from D/E=40%. This example assumes the interest tax shield can be used."

If a two-year daily-return estimate instead gives beta 0.9, the same inputs imply $r_E=7.5\%$ and WACC 6.3%. This is a hypothetical sensitivity comparison, not an observed result. Beta measures sensitivity to market returns, not total stock-price volatility.

For a given business risk, debt leverage changes equity risk. Remove the capital-structure effect from comparable firms' equity betas, then adjust for the division's target leverage. Check whether treating debt beta as zero is appropriate. One raw regression estimate is not a definitive cost of capital.

## 2. A 7.2% company rate can reject a lower-risk positive-NPV project

Suppose a lower-risk division has beta 0.7 and **no debt**. Its discount rate is $3\%+0.7×5\%=6.5\%$. A hypothetical project costs 100 today and pays 107 in one year, giving IRR 7.0%. At the risk-appropriate rate, NPV is $107/1.065-100≈0.47$. At the company's 7.2%, it is $107/1.072-100≈-0.19$, so the same project is rejected. All cash amounts use the same arbitrary unit.

This example has one initial outflow followed by a positive inflow. Do not rank mutually exclusive projects or cash flows with multiple sign changes solely by IRR. Match **free cash flow to the firm (FCFF) with a risk- and financing-appropriate WACC, and free cash flow to equity (FCFE) with equity cost**. Match currency and nominal versus real assumptions too.

The lower-risk division is unlevered to make the financing assumption explicit. With debt, do not simply call 6.5% its WACC: recalculate leverage-adjusted equity cost and after-tax debt cost.

<Callout type="warning">
Adding country or size premia mechanically can count the same risk twice. Sparse comparables or private-company data increase estimation uncertainty. Two decimal places describe display precision, not estimation accuracy.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

**Problem.** Calculate the company's WACC and value the unlevered lower-risk project costing 100 and paying 107 in one year at both 6.5% and the company rate. Is the difference between IRR and the discount rate an NPV amount?

**Solution.** $0.6×9+0.4×6×0.75=7.2\%$. Divisional NPV is about +0.47; company-rate NPV is about −0.19. The 7.0% IRR is 0.5 percentage points above the divisional rate and 0.2 points below the company rate. These rate differences are not cash amounts. Correct arithmetic cannot rescue a discount rate mismatched to risk.
