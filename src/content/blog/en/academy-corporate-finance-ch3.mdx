## The Concept of Return

Investment performance is better compared using **rate of return** than raw dollar amounts, because it lets you compare assets with different investment sizes on a common basis.

Return captures both the capital gain from price movement and any cash income received while holding the asset.

```
Rate of return = (ending price - beginning price + cash income during the holding period) / beginning price

If the cash income is a dividend:
Stock return = capital gain rate + dividend yield
```

To look at performance across multiple periods at once, use the **holding period return (HPR)**.

```
HPR = (1 + R1)(1 + R2) ... (1 + Rn) - 1
```

For financial products that pay interest multiple times a year, the stated rate and the actual compounding effect can differ. The compound return actually realized over one year is called the **effective annual rate**.

## Expected Return and Risk

Future returns aren't certain, so we consider the possible outcomes and their probabilities. **Expected return** is a probability-weighted average.

```
E(r) = Σ [return in each state × probability of that state]
```

Risk is how far the actual future return might deviate from the expected return. In financial management, this is usually measured with **variance** and **standard deviation**.

```
Variance = Σ [(return - expected return)^2 × probability]
Standard deviation = the square root of variance
```

The higher the standard deviation, the more likely actual returns are to deviate substantially from the expected return — meaning greater investment risk.

### Attitudes toward risk

| Type | Decision rule | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Risk-averse | Given equal risk, prefers the higher expected return; given equal expected return, prefers the lower risk | The standard assumption for investors |
| Risk-seeking | Given equal expected return, prefers greater risk | A speculative disposition |
| Risk-neutral | Considers only expected return, ignoring risk | A theoretical benchmark |

Standard models in financial management generally assume investors are risk-averse. So taking on greater risk requires a higher expected return as compensation.

## Portfolio Expected Return

A **portfolio** is a combination of two or more assets held by an investor. For a portfolio of two assets, A and B, the portfolio's return is the weighted average of the two assets' returns.

```
rp = WA × rA + WB × rB

E(rp) = WA × E(rA) + WB × E(rB)
```

The expected return of a portfolio is simple — multiply each asset's expected return by its weight and sum.

Risk is where it gets interesting. Portfolio risk isn't just the weighted average of the individual assets' risks — it also depends on how the assets move relative to each other.

---

## Portfolio Risk and Correlation

The variance of a two-asset portfolio is:

```
Var(rp) = WA^2 Var(rA) + WB^2 Var(rB) + 2 WA WB Cov(rA, rB)
        = WA^2 σA^2 + WB^2 σB^2 + 2 WA WB σA σB ρAB
```

The key ingredients here are **covariance** and the **correlation coefficient**.

| Concept | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Positive covariance | The two assets' returns tend to move in the same direction, on average |
| Negative covariance | The two assets' returns tend to move in opposite directions, on average |
| Correlation coefficient | A standardized measure of how closely the two assets co-move |

The lower the correlation, the greater the benefit of diversification. Combining assets that move in perfect lockstep doesn't reduce risk much, but combining assets that move differently can lower the volatility of the portfolio as a whole.

---

## Diversification and the Two Kinds of Risk

A portfolio's **diversification effect** grows under two conditions.

1. As the number of assets in the portfolio increases
2. As the correlation between those assets falls

But diversification doesn't eliminate all risk. Total risk splits into two components.

| Category | Meaning | Can diversification remove it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unsystematic risk | Risk specific to an individual firm or industry | Yes |
| Systematic risk | Risk from common factors like the business cycle, interest rates, inflation, or market-wide shocks | No |

In financial theory, the risk investors are compensated for is **systematic risk** — the risk that diversification cannot remove. The logic is that the market doesn't pay extra for risk you could have diversified away but chose to keep.

---

## Beta and Market Risk

An individual asset's systematic risk is measured by its **beta (β)**. Beta captures how sensitively an asset's return moves, on average, when the market portfolio's return changes.

```
βj = Cov(rj, rM) / Var(rM)
```

Beta has a few basic properties.

1. A portfolio's beta is the weighted average of its component assets' betas.
2. The risk-free asset has a beta of 0.
3. The market portfolio has a beta of 1.

A beta above 1 identifies an **aggressive stock** — more sensitive than the market; a beta below 1 identifies a **defensive stock** — less sensitive than the market.

## The CML and CAPM

The **Capital Market Line (CML)** describes the relationship between risk and expected return for efficient portfolios built from the risk-free asset and risky assets. Here, risk means the standard deviation of the entire portfolio.

```
Expected return of an efficient portfolio
= risk-free rate + risk premium
= risk-free rate + the market price of risk × the quantity of risk
```

The standard model explaining an individual security's expected return is the **Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)**. CAPM describes the equilibrium relationship between systematic risk and expected return; plotted, it's the **Security Market Line (SML)**.

```
E(rj) = rf + [E(rM) - rf] × βj

rf: the risk-free rate
E(rM) - rf: the market risk premium
βj: the asset's systematic risk
```

CAPM is used in security analysis, evaluating fund manager performance, and estimating the discount rate or cost of equity in capital budgeting.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. Return captures both capital gains and holding-period cash income such as dividends.
2. Expected return is a probability-weighted average; risk is measured with variance and standard deviation.
3. Portfolio risk depends not just on individual asset risk, but on the covariance and correlation between assets.
4. Diversification reduces unsystematic risk but cannot eliminate systematic risk.
5. CAPM describes the relationship between beta-measured systematic risk and expected return as `E(rj) = rf + [E(rM) - rf] × βj`.
