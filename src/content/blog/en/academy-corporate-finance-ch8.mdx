## Where International Financial Management Starts

In domestic corporate finance, we typically evaluate a project's cash flows, risk, and cost of capital in a single home currency. But once a firm earns revenue abroad, imports raw materials, or invests in a foreign subsidiary, a new variable enters the picture: the **exchange rate**.

**International financial management** studies how to value a firm and manage the risk created by exchange-rate movements when cash flows cross borders.

Three questions sit at the core of international financial management:

1. How are exchange rates determined in the foreign exchange market?
2. What kind of risk does exchange-rate movement create for a firm's cash flows and value?
3. What cash flows and discount rate should be used to evaluate the NPV of a foreign investment?

---

## The Foreign Exchange Market and Exchange Rates

The **foreign exchange market** is where one country's currency is exchanged for another's. Foreign exchange trading happens mostly over the counter rather than on a centralized exchange.

The **exchange rate** is the rate at which one country's currency converts into another's.

```
Examples:
$1 = ₩1,100
$1 = ¥123
```

How you quote a rate can change how the same move reads. If the dollar price of one dollar in local currency rises, the dollar is strengthening against the local currency, and the local currency is weakening against the dollar.

---

## Spot and Forward Transactions

Foreign exchange transactions are broadly split into spot and forward transactions based on when delivery happens.

| Type | Meaning | Rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spot transaction | Currency is delivered at the time of the contract, or within a very short window | Spot rate |
| Forward transaction | A contract made now to deliver or receive currency at a specific future date | Forward rate |

In a forward transaction, actual delivery happens in the future, but the exchange rate that applies is agreed on today. This makes forward contracts useful for reducing uncertainty about future exchange-rate movement.

Say the dollar rate for the euro moves like this:

| Term | Rate | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spot | $1.1405/€ | Current exchange rate |
| 1-month forward | $1.1413/€ | Rate for future delivery |
| 3-month forward | $1.1429/€ | The longer the term, the higher the forward rate |
| 1-year forward | $1.1448/€ | Possible forward premium on the euro |

Forward rates differ from spot rates because they reflect the interest-rate gap between the two currencies, expected future exchange rates, and market supply and demand.

## Theories of Exchange-Rate Equilibrium

Five major theories explain where exchange rates settle.

| Theory | Core idea |
| --- | --- |
| Purchasing power parity (PPP) | Two currencies' spot exchange rate moves toward equalizing real purchasing power |
| Fisher effect | Inflation expectations get reflected in each country's nominal interest rate |
| International Fisher effect | The interest-rate gap between two countries gets offset by a change in the future spot rate |
| Interest rate parity (IRP) | The gap between two countries' nominal interest rates matches the forward premium or discount |
| Expectations hypothesis | The forward rate equals the market's expected value of the future spot rate |

### Purchasing power parity

Purchasing power parity holds that exchange rates adjust so that the same good has equal real purchasing power across countries. If one country's prices rise faster than another's, its currency is expected to weaken accordingly.

### The Fisher effect and the international Fisher effect

The Fisher effect holds that a nominal interest rate is made up of a real interest rate plus expected inflation.

```
Nominal interest rate ≈ real interest rate + expected inflation rate
```

The international Fisher effect extends this logic to exchange-rate changes between countries. A currency with a higher nominal interest rate reflects higher inflation expectations, implying that currency may weaken over time.

### Interest rate parity

Interest rate parity holds that the interest-rate gap between two currencies shows up as the difference between the forward rate and the spot rate. If risk-free arbitrage is possible, the hedged return should be the same no matter which currency you invest in.

```
Interest-rate gap between two countries
≈ forward premium or discount
```

## What Currency Risk Means

**Currency risk (exchange risk)** is the risk that a firm's cash flows or value change because of exchange-rate movement.

For example, if a Korean firm expects to receive dollar-denominated revenue in three months, a weaker dollar against the won means less revenue once converted. Conversely, a firm that owes a dollar-denominated debt faces a burden if the dollar strengthens.

Currency risk isn't just an accounting presentation issue. Exchange rates can affect revenue, cost, the amount recovered from a foreign investment, debt repayment, and competitiveness, all at once.

---

## Hedging Currency Risk

**Hedging** protects cash flows from unfavorable price movement. In international financial management, hedging currency risk mainly relies on the forward market and the spot foreign exchange market.

### Hedging with the forward market

A firm expecting to receive foreign currency in the future can lock in the local-currency amount it will receive by selling forward. A firm that needs to pay foreign currency in the future can lock in the local-currency amount it will pay by buying forward.

| Firm's position | Risk | Basic hedge direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Future foreign-currency receivable | Foreign currency weakens | Sell forward |
| Future foreign-currency payable | Foreign currency strengthens | Buy forward |

### Hedging with the spot market

Combining the spot market with short-term money markets can also lock in a future foreign-currency cash flow at today's rate. For example, borrowing foreign currency against a future receivable and immediately converting it at the spot rate reduces exposure to exchange-rate movement.

The goal of hedging isn't predicting the exchange rate correctly — it's reducing uncertainty the firm can't comfortably absorb, so the core business's cash flow stays stable.

## International Capital Budgeting

Foreign investment is fundamentally evaluated on the same NPV basis as domestic investment.

```
NPV = present value of cash flows recoverable to the parent company - initial investment
```

If a project's NPV is positive, it increases firm value and can be accepted. But international capital budgeting is more complex than domestic capital budgeting.

Factors to consider include:

1. Whether foreign cash flows can actually be remitted back to the parent company
2. What exchange rate to use to convert local-currency cash flows
3. How to reflect currency risk in the discount rate
4. How to account for local taxes, remittance restrictions, and political risk
5. Whether the foreign project's risk differs from the firm's existing domestic business

The core point is clear: international capital budgeting should use the **actual cash flows recoverable to the parent company**, not local accounting profit, and should apply a discount rate that properly reflects the foreign project's risk.

---

## Connecting Back to Domestic Capital Budgeting

The capital-budgeting principles from Chapter 4 still apply to international investment.

1. Evaluate on a cash-flow basis.
2. Reflect only incremental cash flows.
3. Account for the time value of money through the discount rate.
4. Use a discount rate that matches the project's risk.
5. NPV connects most directly to an increase in firm value.

The difference is the exchange rate. The same foreign-currency cash flow has a different present value in local-currency terms depending on the exchange rate. That's why international financial management has to manage currency risk alongside the project's own operating risk.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. The foreign exchange market is where different countries' currencies are exchanged, and the exchange rate is the rate at which two currencies convert.
2. A spot transaction delivers currency immediately; a forward transaction locks in today the rate for a future delivery.
3. The equilibrium theories of exchange rates include purchasing power parity, the Fisher effect, the international Fisher effect, interest rate parity, and the expectations hypothesis.
4. Currency risk is the risk that exchange-rate movement changes a firm's cash flows or value, and it can be hedged through the forward and spot foreign exchange markets.
5. International capital budgeting should calculate NPV using the actual cash flows recoverable to the parent company and a discount rate that reflects the foreign project's risk.
