# Macroeconomics — The Solow Model Shows That Saving Cannot Raise the Growth Rate Permanently

The previous chapters dealt with the short run, in which output rises and falls around its potential. From this chapter on, we ask why potential output itself differs across countries and grows over time. By showing that capital accumulation alone cannot keep raising income per person, the Solow model reveals that the source of long-run growth is technology. The stability of differential equations is covered in [Economics Math Chapter 4](/en/education-economics-math-ch4/).

## 1. The dynamics of capital per worker

Let the production function per worker be $y=f(k)$, with diminishing marginal product of capital. Each year a fraction $s$ of output is saved and invested, capital depreciates at rate $\delta$ and the number of workers grows at rate $n$. The change in capital per worker is

<FormulaBox
  label="The fundamental equation of the Solow model"
  formula="\Delta k = s\,f(k) - (\delta + n)\,k"
  description="The first term is investment per worker; the second is the investment needed to keep capital per worker constant (replacing worn-out capital plus equipping new workers). The k* at which the two are equal is the steady state."
/>

With $f(k)=\sqrt{k}$, $s=0.2$ and $\delta+n=0.1$, the steady state solves $0.2\sqrt{k}=0.1k$, so $\sqrt{k}=2$, that is $k^*=4$ and $y^*=2$.

When $k$ is below the steady state, investment per worker exceeds required investment and $k$ rises; when it is above, $k$ falls. Wherever it starts, the economy converges to $k^*$.

## 2. The saving rate changes the level, not the growth rate

If the saving rate rises to 0.3, the steady state solves $0.3\sqrt{k}=0.1k$, giving $k^*=9$ and $y^*=3$. Output per worker is 50% higher. But once the new steady state is reached, output per worker stops growing again. Because the marginal product of capital diminishes, each additional unit of capital adds less output, until investment only just covers what is required.

A higher saving rate raises growth only during the transition. If the saving rate becomes 0.3 at $k=4$, capital per worker grows by $0.3\times 2/4-0.1=5\%$ in the first year, and this rate falls towards 0 as $k$ approaches 9.

### A. Closed form and convergence speed: how long does the transition take?

With $f(k)=k^{\alpha}$ and labour efficiency growing at rate $g$ (measuring $k$ per effective worker), the steady state has a closed form.

<FormulaBox
  label="Cobb-Douglas steady state"
  formula="k^*=\left(\frac{s}{n+g+\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}},\qquad \frac{\partial\ln y^*}{\partial\ln s}=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}"
  description="In the example above (α=0.5, g=0) the elasticity is 1, so a saving rate 1.5 times higher gives y* 1.5 times higher (2 → 3). With a capital share of α=1/3 the elasticity is 0.5: doubling the saving rate raises y* only by √2 ≈ 1.41."
/>

Linearising the fundamental equation in $\ln k$ around the steady state shows that the gap closes at a constant rate.

<FormulaBox
  label="Convergence speed and half-life"
  formula="\frac{d\ln y}{dt}\approx-\lambda\,(\ln y-\ln y^*),\qquad \lambda=(1-\alpha)(n+g+\delta),\qquad t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{\lambda}"
  description="In the example above λ = 0.5 × 0.1 = 5%, a half-life of about 14 years. With a capital share of 1/3 and n+g+δ = 6%, λ = 4% and the half-life is about 17 years. The gap with empirical estimates (around 2% a year) is taken up in chapter 22."
/>

Computing the actual path year by year for the economy whose saving rate rises from 0.2 to 0.3: output per worker starts at 2, closes half the gap around year 12 (the linear approximation says 14; the economy moves faster when it is far from the steady state), and after 60 years is 2.95, still just short of 3. Consumption drops to 1.4 in the first year, regains its original 1.6 in year 7, and then rises towards 2.1. The cost of a policy that raises saving is concentrated in the early years. Empirical estimates of convergence speed and the human-capital correction continue in [chapter 22](/en/education-economics-ch33/).

## 3. The Golden Rule: the capital stock that maximizes consumption

Saving more raises output but can lower consumption. Steady-state consumption per worker is $c^*=f(k^*)-(\delta+n)k^*$, and the condition that maximizes it is

<FormulaBox
  label="The Golden Rule condition"
  formula="f'(k_{gold}) = \delta + n"
  description="Consumption is highest where the marginal product of capital equals the cost of maintaining capital. In the example above, 1/(2√k) = 0.1, so k_gold = 25; the saving rate that achieves it is 0.5, and consumption is 2.5."
/>

Saving rates of 0.2 (consumption 1.6) and 0.3 (consumption 2.1) both leave less capital than the Golden Rule. In this economy a higher saving rate raises long-run consumption, but early in the transition consumption falls by as much as saving rises. In the year the saving rate rises from 0.2 to 0.3, consumption is $0.7\times 2=1.4$, below the original 1.6. The current generation pays the cost and future generations reap the benefit. Conversely, an economy with more capital than the Golden Rule can raise consumption both now and in the future by saving less.

## 4. Convergence and technological progress

The model predicts that countries with less capital grow faster. But only countries with similar saving rates, population growth and technology converge to the same steady state (**conditional convergence**). **Absolute convergence**, in which all countries converge to the same income, does not show up well in the data. The data fit conditional convergence better.

If labour-augmenting technology progresses at rate $g$ per year, output per worker keeps growing at rate $g$ in the steady state. In the Solow model, technology is the only source that explains **sustained** growth in income per person. Yet the rate of technological progress is given from outside the model.

Growth accounting splits output growth by factor. In $g_Y=\alpha g_K+(1-\alpha)g_L+g_A$, the remainder $g_A$ after subtracting the contributions of capital and labour is called the **Solow residual** (total factor productivity growth). The residual is a measurement, not an explanation. What drives technological progress is the subject of the endogenous growth theory in [Chapter 12](/en/education-economics-ch23/).

<Callout type="warning">
"Saving more raises the growth rate permanently" is not a conclusion of the Solow model. Nor should the investment rate in the national accounts be read directly as the model's $s$, or the model be taken to mean that all countries must converge to the same income. What the model predicts is conditional convergence among countries with similar structures.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

**Problem 1.** An economy with $f(k)=\sqrt{k}$ and $\delta+n=0.1$ saves 20% of output. Find steady-state output and consumption per worker. What happens if population growth rises so that $\delta+n=0.2$?

**Solution.** $k^*=4$, $y^*=2$ and $c^*=0.8\times2=1.6$. With $\delta+n=0.2$, $0.2\sqrt{k}=0.2k$ gives $k^*=1$ and $y^*=1$: output per worker halves.

**Problem 2.** An economy with capital share $\alpha=1/3$ and $n+g+\delta=0.06$ is at half its steady-state income. How many years does it take to close half the gap? If the country doubles its saving rate, by what factor does steady-state income rise?

**Solution.** $\lambda=(1-1/3)\times0.06=0.04$, so the half-life is $\ln2/0.04\approx17$ years. The saving-rate elasticity is $\alpha/(1-\alpha)=0.5$, so steady-state income rises by $2^{0.5}\approx1.41$. That is why saving alone struggles to explain income gaps of many times between countries.
