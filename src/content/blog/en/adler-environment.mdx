### Who's Actually Around You?

Motivational speaker Jim Rohn put it bluntly:
**"You are the average of the five people you spend the most time with."**

Think about it. Take your closest friends, coworkers, family — average out their income, their hobbies, their way of talking, their way of thinking — and the result looks strikingly close to you. We absorb the people around us without realizing it, and we drift toward becoming like them. It's part instinct, part survival strategy.

Adlerian psychology pushes this a step further. Yes, environment shapes us — but it also insists we face an uncomfortable fact: **we're the ones who chose that environment in the first place.** Today we're looking at one of the most powerful — and most difficult — ways to change yourself: resetting the environment of your relationships.

---

### 1. Mirror Neurons: We Copy Each Other

Neuroscience has a concept called **[mirror neurons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror_neuron)** — brain cells that fire almost as if we're performing an action ourselves, just from watching someone else do it. Yawns are contagious; so are emotions, attitudes, and habits.

Spend your days around people who constantly complain, and you'll start seeing the world negatively without noticing. Spend time with people who keep challenging themselves and achieving things, and you'll pick up that same "I can do this too" energy. You can't out-willpower your environment — humans are creatures of context. Cross a river and an orange tree turns into a bitter one; where you belong changes what you're made of.

### 2. The Trap of Familiarity: Why We Can't Cut Ties

A friend who's holding you back. A partner who dismisses you. A coworker with nothing to teach you. You know, logically, that you should distance yourself — and yet you don't. Why?

Adler traces it back to **the comfort of the familiar.**

Even friends who do nothing but complain feel comfortable to be around. They validate each other's failures — "the world is just broken" — and that's oddly soothing. Walking away from that group to chase something better is frightening. You're afraid of hearing "you've changed," and you're afraid of the tension that comes with adapting to somewhere new.

In other words, we're not **trapped** in a bad environment — we're **choosing to stay** because change is frightening.

### 3. Changing Your Environment Isn't Betrayal

Plenty of people treat changing their environment as a kind of betrayal. They feel guilty: "How could I abandon an old friend?"

But look at it coldly. Is it a betrayal to grow and move toward a bigger world? Or is it a betrayal of yourself to stay locked in an old shell, letting your own potential rot?

A real friend will cheer your growth. If someone leaves because you changed, they may never have valued you for who you are — only for the comfortable, half-failing state you used to be in. Relationships shouldn't be stagnant water. Like a flowing stream, they should shift and circulate naturally as you grow.

### 4. Find the Five People Who Will Grow You

Actively seek out the people you want to resemble — people a step ahead of you, people who give off positive energy. If no one like that is around you right now, find a mentor through books, talks, or communities instead.

**"Change yourself, and the people you meet change. Change the people you meet, and your life changes."**
That's simply true. Rebuild your five people. It's the single most reliable investment you can make in your future.

### 5. Closing Thought: Be Someone's Good Environment

One last thing worth remembering: you, too, are one of someone else's "closest five."

Are you raising the average for the people around you, or dragging it down?

If you want to meet good people, you have to become a good person first. When you're growing and shining, good people will gather toward that light. Stop blaming your environment — choose it instead. And become a good environment for someone else.
