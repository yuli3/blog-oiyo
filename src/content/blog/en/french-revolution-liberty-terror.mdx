## Why 1789?

Late 18th-century France was a society of extreme inequality, divided into three Estates:

**First Estate (clergy)**: 0.5% of the population, owned 10% of the land, exempt from most taxes
**Second Estate (nobility)**: 1.5% of the population, monopolized top offices, largely tax-exempt
**Third Estate (everyone else — 98%)**: Bourgeoisie, artisans, peasants — bearing the full tax burden

In 1788–89, back-to-back crop failures caused bread prices to explode. Parisian workers spent 80–90% of their wages on bread alone. The powder keg was primed.

---

## 1789: Revolution Begins

In May, Louis XVI convened the Estates-General to address a fiscal crisis. Third Estate delegates, demanding equal voting rights and being refused, broke away to form the **National Assembly**.

On July 14, armed Parisian crowds stormed the **Bastille prison**. Only 7 prisoners were inside — but the symbolism was electrifying. July 14 remains France's national holiday.

In August, the **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen** proclaimed: "All men are born free and equal in rights."

<Callout type="tip">
**The irony of "all men"**: The Declaration meant men literally. Women's rights activist Olympe de Gouges published a "Declaration of the Rights of Woman and the Female Citizen" in 1791. She was guillotined in 1793. The revolution's ideals were selectively applied.
</Callout>

## The Reign of Terror: Revolution Devours Its Children

The monarchy was abolished in 1792 and the Republic proclaimed. In January 1793, Louis XVI was guillotined — shocking European monarchies into a coalition war against France.

Facing foreign invasion and internal counter-revolution, the **Committee of Public Safety** under Maximilien Robespierre launched the **Reign of Terror** (1793–94).

Approximately 17,000 were officially executed; total deaths may have exceeded 40,000. The purge that began targeting counter-revolutionaries turned inward, as revolutionaries denounced each other as traitors.

Robespierre declared: "Terror without virtue is disastrous; virtue without terror is powerless." In July 1794, the revolution claimed him too — he was arrested and guillotined.

---

## Enter Napoleon

After the Terror, the unstable Directory (1795–99) failed to stabilize France. In 1799, young General **Napoleon Bonaparte** seized power in a coup, promising order.

By 1804, he had crowned himself Emperor. A republic born from killing a king had produced a new one.

---

## The Revolution's Lasting Legacy

Despite its bloody trajectory, the French Revolution permanently altered the world:

- **Liberty, Equality, Fraternity**: This tricolor ideal inspired democratic movements worldwide
- **Equality before the law**: Abolished aristocratic legal privileges; foundation of modern rule of law
- **Nationalism**: The concept of a "nation" of citizens replaced subjects of a monarch
- **Secularism**: Church-state separation advanced dramatically

The revolution's ideas spread through 19th-century Europe in wave after wave of reform and revolution. Democracy, human rights, and constitutionalism — concepts we take for granted — were born and baptized in blood on the streets of Paris in 1789.
