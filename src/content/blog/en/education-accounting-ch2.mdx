Once the adjusted trial balance of [chapter 3](/en/education-accounting-ch3/) is complete, financial statements are prepared. They are not tables to be read separately; they interlock so that a number in one statement is the starting point of another. This chapter confirms the components set by K-IFRS 1001 *Presentation of Financial Statements* (IAS 1) and follows the links with one company's numbers.

## 1. A complete set of financial statements

K-IFRS 1001 defines a complete set of financial statements as the following five components. Figures for the preceding period are presented alongside for comparison.

  title="Components of financial statements and their roles"

  rows={[
    ["Statement of financial position", "At the end of the reporting period", "What do we own and whom do we owe how much?"],
    ["Statement of comprehensive income", "Reporting period", "How much did we earn (profit + other comprehensive income)?"],

    ["Statement of cash flows", "Reporting period", "Where did cash come from and where did it go?"],
    ["Notes", "—", "What policies, estimates and details lie behind the numbers?"],

/>

The notes are not an appendix but part of the financial statements. They contain **material accounting policy information**, estimation uncertainty, contingent liabilities, related party transactions and more. Under the amendment effective from 2023, entities disclose "material accounting policy information" instead of "significant accounting policies". The aim is to describe the entity's own judgements rather than copy sentences from the standard.

### A. The two parts of the statement of comprehensive income

The statement of comprehensive income is divided into **profit or loss** and **other comprehensive income**. Other comprehensive income covers gains and losses not yet realized or different in nature from operating performance, such as a revaluation surplus on property, plant and equipment, fair value changes on equity instruments measured at fair value through other comprehensive income, and translation differences on foreign operations. Their sum, total comprehensive income, is the change in equity for the period other than transactions with owners.

### A. Income statement

Profit is $2{,}000 - 1{,}500 = 500$ (₩10,000). Assume there is no income tax and no other comprehensive income.

### B. Statement of changes in equity

### C. Statement of cash flows

<LectureTable
  title="Statement of cash flows (direct method, unit: ₩10,000)"
  headers={["Activity", "Detail", "Amount"]}
  rows={[
    ["Operating", "Receipts from customers 1,800 − operating payments 1,500", "+300"],

Dividends paid are classified as financing. K-IFRS 1007 (IAS 7) allows dividends paid to be classified as either financing or operating, provided the choice is applied consistently.

### D. Statement of financial position

Closing receivables are $300 + 2{,}000 - 1{,}800 = 500$. Closing assets are cash ₩9 million, receivables ₩5 million and land ₩6 million, or ₩20 million; there are no liabilities, and equity is also ₩20 million. At the start, too, assets of $500 + 300 + 600 = 1{,}400$ matched equity of $1{,}000 + 400 = 1{,}400$. If the equation does not hold, a number is missing somewhere, so the equation also serves as a check after the statements are prepared.

## 3. Three links

<LectureTable
  title="Links between the statements"
  headers={["Start", "Link", "End"]}
  rows={[
    ["Profit in the income statement 500", "Increase in retained earnings in the statement of changes in equity", "Retained earnings of 800 in the statement of financial position"],
    ["Net change in cash in the cash flow statement +400", "Added to opening cash", "Cash of 900 in the statement of financial position"],
    ["Sales in the income statement 2,000", "Difference of 200 from collections of 1,800", "Increase of 200 in receivables in the statement of financial position"],
  ]}
/>

The third link shows why profit and cash diverge. Profit is ₩5 million, but operating cash flow is ₩3 million. The ₩2 million difference is the increase in receivables not yet collected. Reconciling this difference backwards, starting from profit, is the indirect method of [chapter 11](/en/academy-accounting-basics-ch4/).

<Callout type="warning">
A rise in profit does not mean cash rises by the same amount. A company whose receivables and inventory pile up quickly can run out of cash while reporting profits and fail despite being profitable. Conversely, a rise in cash does not mean operations improved: in this example, the ₩1 million of financing cash came from a share issue. Do not draw conclusions from a single statement.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

In the following year, the company in section 2 makes sales of ₩24 million (all on credit), collects ₩20 million of receivables and pays operating expenses of ₩17 million in cash. There are no other transactions. What are profit, operating cash flow, closing receivables and closing cash?

Profit is $2{,}400 - 1{,}700 = 700$, i.e. ₩7 million. Operating cash flow is $2{,}000 - 1{,}700 = 300$, i.e. ₩3 million. Closing receivables are $500 + 2{,}400 - 2{,}000 = 900$ (₩9 million), and closing cash is $900 + 300 = 1{,}200$ (₩12 million). The ₩4 million difference between profit and operating cash flow is exactly the ₩4 million increase in receivables.
