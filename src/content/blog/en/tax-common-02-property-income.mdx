# Everyday Taxes: Ownership and Income

Part 1 covered taxes paid when assets move. This installment addresses: 1) taxes on owning real estate (property tax and comprehensive real estate holding tax) and automobiles, which are due even when you simply hold them; and 2) the differences among the three major national taxes that arise when you earn money (income and sales): value-added tax, income tax, and corporate tax.

---

## 1. Real Estate Holding Taxes: Property Tax vs. Comprehensive Real Estate Holding Tax

These are taxes imposed simply because you own real estate. The owner as of June 1 each year (the tax base date) pays them.

### The Magic of the Tax Base Date (June 1)
If you plan to sell a home, you must complete the sale and transfer title **before June 1** to avoid that year’s property tax and comprehensive real estate holding tax (in practice, the earlier of the final-payment date and the title-transfer registration date is used). Conversely, if you plan to buy a home, purchasing **after June 2** is a sensible choice to avoid paying that year’s holding taxes.

## 2. Automobile Tax (Local Tax)

If you own a vehicle, you pay this tax every year. It is assessed based on engine displacement (cc).
- It is imposed not according to a vehicle’s value, but on the logic that **“the greater the engine displacement, the greater the impact on environmental pollution and road wear.”**
- *Contradiction:* A 100-million-won electric vehicle (such as a Tesla) has no engine displacement and pays only “130,000 won (a flat amount)” under the non-commercial passenger-car standard, whereas an old 20-million-won Sonata (2.0, 2000cc) pays more than 500,000 won. This tax therefore continually raises controversy over regressive treatment of wealth.
- If you prepay the entire year’s amount in January each year, you receive a benefit that discounts a certain percentage.

## 3. Value Added Tax (VAT)

This is the “VAT 10%” printed at the bottom of a receipt when we eat at a restaurant or drink coffee.

- **Concept:** A tax levied at a rate of 10% on newly created value (value added) at each stage of producing and distributing goods.
- **Indirect tax:** The person who eats the meal (the final consumer) bears the cost in practice, but the restaurant owner (the business operator) reports and pays the tax to the tax office. Because the taxpayer and the person responsible for payment differ, it is an indirect tax with low tax resistance.
- **Exemption:** VAT is not added to every item. Items closely tied to ordinary life—basic necessities (unprocessed agricultural, livestock, and fishery products), healthcare, educational services (private academies), and public transportation—are entirely exempt from VAT (zero won).

## 4. Income Tax and Corporate Tax

These form the core foundation of South Korea’s national tax revenue when individuals and companies earn money.

### Income Tax
- **Concept:** A tax collected on income earned by a **natural person (individual)** during one year. It includes employment income (salary), interest/dividend income, business income, pension income, capital gains, and miscellaneous income.
- **Tax rate:** It uses a progressive structure in which the rate rises as income increases. It starts at 6% and can reach 45% for very high earners (49.5% including local income tax).
- Comprehensive income tax is generally reported and paid each May, combining all income from the preceding year.

### Corporate Tax
- **Concept:** A tax collected on the net profit (based on net income for the period) earned by a **company (corporation)**, a legal person rather than an individual.
- **Rate difference:** The top income-tax rate approaches 45%, while the top corporate-tax rate is only 24%. Because of this tax difference, when a sole proprietor’s business becomes so successful that they face a tax burden, they may accept the administrative burden and “convert to a corporation” to receive the corporate tax rate.

<Callout type="important">
**Summary of tax-saving common sense** <br/>
“The corporation’s money is not my money.” <br/>
After a sole proprietor pays income tax on revenue deposited into the account, the owner may spend it freely. A corporation is different: net profit remaining after 24% corporate tax is strictly the money of the ‘stock company.’ If the CEO (representative director) takes that money out freely, it immediately constitutes **‘embezzlement.’**
</Callout>
