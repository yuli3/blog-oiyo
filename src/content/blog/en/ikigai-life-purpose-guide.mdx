Okinawa has one of the highest concentrations of centenarians anywhere in the world. When researchers asked residents the secret to their longevity, they didn't reach for diet or exercise first — they mentioned **ikigai (生き甲斐).** Ikigai is a Japanese word for "a reason to be alive" — a reason to get out of bed in the morning, a reason to keep going.

## What ikigai means

**生き (iki)**: life, being alive
**甲斐 (gai)**: worth, value, what makes something worthwhile

Taken literally, ikigai is "that which makes life worth living." It doesn't have to be a grand mission. Brewing morning coffee, watering the flowers in the garden, looking after a grandchild — people who find their ikigai in these small, everyday things tend to live long, healthy lives.

## The Western four-circle model

In the West, ikigai is often mapped as the overlap of four circles. (It's more career-centered than the original Japanese concept, but it's a genuinely useful framework.)

### The four circles

| Circle | The question it asks |
|----|------|
| **What you love (Passion)** | What makes you lose track of time? |
| **What you're good at (Profession)** | What do you do better than most people? |
| **What the world needs (Mission)** | What does the world need from you? |
| **What you can be paid for (Vocation)** | What do you receive compensation for? |

### What the overlaps mean

| Overlap | Name | What it feels like |
|--------|------|--------|
| Love + Skill | **Passion** | Enjoyable, but doesn't pay |
| Skill + Pay | **Profession** | Pays the bills, but feels hollow |
| Pay + World's need | **Vocation** | Meaningful, but not something you love |
| World's need + Love | **Mission** | Meaningful, but doesn't pay |
| **The center** | **Ikigai** | All four are satisfied |

## 7 questions for finding your ikigai

### Question 1: What absorbed you as a child?
Your happiest childhood moments — the activities where you lost track of time.

### Question 2: What makes time disappear?
Flow — the state of being so focused you forget time is passing.

### Question 3: What kind of difficulty are you willing to endure?
When you love what you're doing, you tolerate the hard parts too. Which hard parts feel worth it to you?

### Question 4: What do people come to you for help with?
The things friends and colleagues specifically ask you for.

### Question 5: What makes you angry, consistently?
Anger is often connected to a deep value: "this should be different."

### Question 6: If your life became a book, what would it be about?
The story from your own life that you'd want to pass on to others.

### Question 7: What do you need to do before you die?
If you had full permission to "do nothing else," what would you do?

## Common misconceptions about ikigai

### Misconception 1: "Ikigai has to be a grand mission"

Japanese researchers of ikigai actually point the other way — toward small, everyday joys.
- Walking the dog every morning
- Greeting the neighbors
- Tending a small garden

### Misconception 2: "Career = ikigai"

Ikigai can be a career, but it can just as easily be a hobby, a relationship, or service to others.

### Misconception 3: "It never changes"

Ikigai shifts with life stage. What gave your 30s meaning may not be what gives your 70s meaning.

## The research on ikigai and well-being

In the Okinawa research, people with a strong sense of ikigai showed:
- Lower rates of heart disease
- Lower stress levels
- Higher life satisfaction
- Longer-preserved cognitive function

**A Japanese study (Ohsaki Study, 2008)**: People who reported having ikigai had a lower mortality risk than those who didn't.

## Bringing ikigai into everyday life

### Start small

Before chasing some grand mission, look for the moment today when you felt most alive.

### Keep an ikigai journal

Each evening, write down: "When did I feel most alive today?" Patterns tend to emerge after about three months.

### Increase your flow activities

Build more time-disappearing activities into your everyday life.

**A quick way to check whether you already have an ikigai**: ask yourself, "Do I have a reason to get up tomorrow morning?" If something comes to mind immediately, you already have one.
