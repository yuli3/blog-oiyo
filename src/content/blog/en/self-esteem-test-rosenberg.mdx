## What Is Self-Esteem?

Self-esteem is your overall evaluation of, and sense of value about, yourself. Psychologist Morris Rosenberg defined self-esteem as **the degree of positive or negative attitude a person holds toward themselves as a person of worth**.

Self-esteem is generally made up of two components. One is **self-efficacy** — the belief that you're capable of doing things well. The other is **self-worth** — the sense that you deserve love and respect simply for being who you are. Healthy self-esteem forms when both of these are in balance.

<Callout type="note">
Self-esteem is different from narcissism or arrogance. People with healthy self-esteem realistically recognize both their strengths and their limitations, while still fundamentally accepting themselves.
</Callout>

## How Self-Esteem Shapes Your Life

### Patterns Linked to Low Self-Esteem

Low self-esteem tends to create a recurring set of struggles. It can lead to overreacting to criticism or failure, or seeking excessive approval from others. It can also cause people to avoid new challenges, undervalue their own accomplishments, and struggle to set boundaries in relationships. These patterns tend to reinforce each other, creating a cycle that further erodes self-esteem.

### Characteristics of Healthy Self-Esteem

With healthy self-esteem, people tend to interpret failure as an opportunity to learn, and while they listen to others' opinions, they still trust their own judgment. They set boundaries in relationships and express their needs and feelings clearly.

## Practical Ways to Build Self-Esteem

### Things You Can Start Today

- **Notice self-criticism**: recognizing your inner critical voice is the first step. Replace "why can't I even do this?" with "that was hard this time — I'll try it differently next time."
- **Track small wins**: writing down three things you did well each day strengthens self-efficacy.
- **Clarify your values**: acting on what genuinely matters to you raises your sense of self-worth.

### Longer-Term Strategies

- **Practice setting boundaries**: saying no and setting limits is a core "muscle" for self-esteem. Start practicing in small, low-stakes situations.
- **Self-compassion**: practice speaking to yourself the way you would speak to a close friend. Kristin Neff's research on self-compassion shows that it can serve as an even more stable psychological foundation than self-esteem.

<Callout type="tip">
Self-esteem doesn't grow through achievement or external validation alone. It starts with unconditional self-acceptance. Take a moment to examine the belief that "I'm only worthy if I'm perfect."
</Callout>

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Is low self-esteem a personality flaw?**
A: No. Self-esteem is shaped significantly by childhood experiences, social environment, and repeated experiences of success and failure. It's a psychological trait that can change.

**Q: Is there a way to raise my self-esteem quickly?**
A: Consistent practice tends to be more effective than any quick fix. Repeatedly practicing self-compassion, values-based action, and boundary-setting in daily life can produce noticeable change within a few months.
