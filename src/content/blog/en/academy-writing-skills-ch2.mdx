## Is Shorter Always Better?

Short sentences aren't automatically good. But the sentences that cause the most trouble in beginner writing are almost always too long. When several ideas tangle together in one sentence, readers lose track of where to focus.

The goal of editing a sentence isn't shortening it for its own sake — it's making the meaning sharper. A sentence that needs its length can stay long; a sentence that adds nothing should go, even if it's short.

## One Sentence, One Main Idea

Sentences sprawl most often because they're carrying more than one main idea. Stringing together "and," "but," and "so" again and again is a fast way to let a sentence run on.

Weak version:

> This campaign targets new customer acquisition, and it also includes incentives designed to raise the repurchase rate among existing customers, and the messaging will run differently across channels.

Edited version:

> This campaign's primary goal is acquiring new customers. We're also offering separate incentives to encourage repeat purchases from existing customers. Messaging will vary by channel to match each customer segment.

The content is nearly identical, but the reading effort drops sharply.

## Finding What You Can Cut

Concise sentences start with cutting. Watch especially for habitual filler phrases.

| Common padding | Tightened |
| --- | --- |
| in the process of doing | while doing |
| it can be said that | (state it directly) |
| has a very significant impact on | significantly affects |
| a wide variety of different | a wide variety of |
| in advance, ahead of time | in advance |

Cutting isn't about making writing lighter for its own sake — it's about setting priorities for meaning.

## Turning Abstract Words into Concrete Ones

Words like "improve," "optimize," "strengthen," and "streamline" are convenient but vague. Without a concrete action or result attached, readers can't tell what's actually changing.

For example, "We will improve the customer experience" is far less clear than "We will cut checkout from four steps to two." Abstract words point at a direction; concrete words show the execution.

## Cutting Passive Voice and Nominalizations

Sentences get stiff when they lean too heavily on passive voice and noun forms instead of verbs.

| Stiff | Natural |
| --- | --- |
| A review was conducted | We reviewed it |
| A decision has been made | We decided |
| Implementation of the plan will be pursued | We will implement the plan |
| Sharing of the report was completed | We shared the report |

Restoring a real subject and a real verb puts motion back into a sentence. The effect is especially strong in reports and emails.

## Read It Out Loud

One of the best editing techniques is reading your sentences aloud. If you run out of breath, if the meaning doesn't land on a single pass, or if the same word keeps repeating, that's a signal to revise.

Reading aloud places your writing inside the reader's actual time. A sentence that felt smooth in your head can snag the moment it's read for real.

## Key Takeaways

- The goal of sentence editing isn't writing short for its own sake — it's making meaning sharper.
- Keep one main idea per sentence whenever possible.
- Cutting habitual filler phrases raises the density of your writing.
- Abstract words need a concrete action or result attached to be understandable.
- Reducing passive voice and nominalizations while restoring verbs makes sentences feel natural.
