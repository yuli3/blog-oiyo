# Introduction to Economics — A Review Ends Not With a Formula List but With Knowing Which Assumptions to Switch On

Chapter 10 does not rewrite the micro and macro formulas. That approach is the same as flashcards, the low-quality format this course chose to drop. Instead, it fixes **which chapter to open when a question comes in**, and the boundaries where the introduction must hand over to a full subject course.

## 1. The assumptions to switch on for each type of question

The same $\mathrm{MR}=\mathrm{MC}$ gives $P=\mathrm{MC}$ under competition and $P>\mathrm{MC}$ under monopoly. Writing the equation without stating the market structure makes it impossible to calculate the deadweight loss. With the same $Y=C+I+G+\mathrm{NX}$, adding intermediate goods is double counting.

With demand $P=100-Q$ and $\mathrm{MC}=20$, the competitive quantity is 80 and the monopoly quantity 40, so the DWL is $0.5×40×40=800$ won. It is the same number as in chapter 5. There is no new formula to learn in chapter 10. The number comes back to confirm that, rather than a summary table, **once you pick the structure, the calculation follows**.

## 2. What the introduction does not answer — handed over to subject courses

Consumer theory beyond interior solutions belongs to [Microeconomics](/en/education-economics-ch1/), expectations and IS-LM comparative statics to [Macroeconomics](/en/education-economics-ch11/), identification and regression to [Econometrics](/en/education-econometrics-ch1/), risk allocation to [Financial Economics](/en/education-financial-economics-ch1/), the family of comparative-advantage models to [International Trade](/en/education-economics-ch34/), and the trilemma to [International Finance](/en/education-international-finance-ch3/). One introductory course does not replace every undergraduate subject.

<Callout type="warning">
Do not hard-wire a multiplier of 5, a deposit multiplier of 10 or P = MC into an answer as defaults. All three are upper bounds or benchmarks resting on special assumptions. What an interviewer wants is whether those assumptions fit the data at hand. If the assumptions are not visible, it is not an intermediate review.
</Callout>

A current account surplus, GDP growth and currency depreciation are each pieces of different identities. Reading one indicator as a national report card is the typical failure of an introductory course. Compressed into one sentence, chapters 7–9 say: "State what is being measured first, and do not mix mirror relationships with causation."

## 3. Common mistakes — the boundaries of definitions

Opportunity cost is the single best alternative forgone. Sunk costs are left out of future decisions. A shift in demand and a change in quantity demanded are different events. A monopolist's MR is not P. Intermediate goods are not added directly to GDP. A rise in won/dollar is a depreciation of the won. Comparative advantage exists even without an absolute advantage. A positive externality leads to underproduction and a negative one to overproduction. At the same MPC, the tax-cut multiplier is smaller than the spending multiplier.

Do not memorize these sentences as flashcards; remember them together with the counterexamples each chapter showed by calculation. A correct answer with no calculation is not the pass mark for this course.

## Check your understanding

Is the diagnosis "we cut rates but lending did not rise, so the multiplier is wrong" correct? No. Chapter 8's leakages $c$ and $e$, credit demand and the liquidity trap come first. It means the numerator of the multiplier is close to zero, not that the arithmetic of $1/(1-\mathrm{MPC})$ is wrong. An answer that does not say which assumption was switched off is wrong.
