# Microeconomics — An Observed Price and Quantity Is an Intersection, Not a Demand Curve

If [Introduction to Economics, Chapter 2](/en/academy-economics-basics-ch2/) separated a shift of a curve from a movement along it, this chapter turns that distinction into a problem of **identification**. The prices and quantities recorded in data are points on neither the demand curve nor the supply curve; they are where the two met. We need to know which curve moved before we can interpret those points.

## 1. Solving the equilibrium in general form

Linear demand $Q_d=a-bP$ and supply $Q_s=c+dP$ ($b,d>0$) meet at:

<FormulaBox
  label="Equilibrium of a linear market"
  formula="P^*=\frac{a-c}{b+d},\qquad Q^*=\frac{ad+bc}{b+d}"
  description="If the demand intercept a rises by 1, price rises by 1/(b+d) and quantity by d/(b+d). The steeper the supply curve (the smaller d), the more of a demand increase goes into price."
/>

With demand $Q_d=100-2P$ and supply $Q_s=10+P$, $P^*=90/3=30$ won and $Q^*=40$.

- If higher income moves demand to $Q_d=112-2P$, then $P^*=34$ won and $Q^*=44$. Price and quantity **rise together**. By the formula, $a$ rose by 12, so price rises by $12/3=4$ and quantity by $12\times 1/3=4$.
- If higher input costs move supply to $Q_s=4+P$, then $P^*=32$ won and $Q^*=36$. Price rises and quantity **falls**.

Price alone does not reveal the cause. If quantity rose with price, a demand shock dominated; if quantity fell, a supply shock dominated. When both curves move at once, the sign of one variable depends on their relative sizes and becomes ambiguous.

## 2. Why you cannot draw a demand curve by joining the dots

Suppose you plot a product's yearly prices and sales and the points slope upward. It is tempting to conclude that "people buy more when the price is higher", but if supply stayed put while demand grew every year, the points trace out the **supply curve**. To estimate a demand curve you need variation that moves supply while leaving demand unchanged (for example, weather shifting the supply of a crop). This is the **identification problem** of econometrics; its standard tool, instrumental variables, is covered in [Econometrics, Chapter 4](/en/education-econometrics-ch4/).

"Housing demand fell because interest rates rose" describes an event that shifted the demand curve through the cost of borrowing. "Transactions fell because house prices rose" may be a movement along the same demand curve, or the result of reduced supply. Misreading the cause leads to the opposite policy.

## 3. A price ceiling creates a shortage and changes how goods are rationed

If the market above (equilibrium price 30 won) is given a ceiling of 20 won, quantity demanded is $100-40=60$ and quantity supplied is $10+20=30$: a shortage of 30. What price no longer rations is rationed instead by queues, lotteries, connections, black markets and lower quality.

The **deadweight loss** from quantity falling from 40 to 30 is the triangle between the demand and supply curves from 30 to 40 units. At a quantity of 30, consumers are willing to pay $P=50-Q/2=35$ won and producers' marginal cost is $P=Q-10=20$ won.

<FormulaBox
  label="Deadweight loss of a price ceiling"
  formula="DWL=\tfrac{1}{2}\times(35-20)\times(40-30)=75"
  description="Those 10 trades would have been worth more to consumers than they cost to produce, yet they disappear. Counting the time spent queuing, the true loss is larger."
/>

"Lower prices benefit consumers" holds only for the 30 people who actually buy. The losses of those who cannot buy and the cost of queuing must be subtracted to find the net effect on consumers as a whole. A price floor (a minimum wage, a guaranteed crop price) does the opposite and creates excess supply.

<Callout type="warning">
Upward-sloping observed points are not a counterexample to the law of demand. A demand curve that genuinely slopes upward — a Giffen good — requires an inferior good whose income effect overwhelms the substitution effect, which is rare (Chapter 4). When data slope upward, first check whether supply was actually held fixed.
</Callout>

The claim that competitive equilibrium maximises total surplus holds only under perfect competition, no externalities and perfect information. Chapters 5–10 relax these assumptions one by one.

## Check your understanding

Demand $Q_d=80-P$, supply $Q_s=20+P$. The equilibrium is $P=30$ won and $Q=50$. With a ceiling of 20 won, demand is 60 and supply 40, a shortage of 20. At a quantity of 40 the demand price is 40 won and the supply price 20 won, so the deadweight loss is $\tfrac{1}{2}\times 20\times 10=100$.
