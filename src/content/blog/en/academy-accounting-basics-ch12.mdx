A budget turns the business plan into numbers. It works out in advance how much will be sold next year, how much must be made to do so, how much material must be bought and when cash will run short. A budget is a planning tool and also the benchmark for performance evaluation, compared later with actual results. Variance analysis was covered in [Cost Accounting chapter 10](/en/academy-cost-accounting-ch6/).

## 1. The structure of the master budget

A master budget links several budgets like a chain. The starting point is always the **sales budget**, because the quantity to be made depends on the quantity to be sold.

## 2. The sales and production budgets

Company BP expects to sell 1,000 units in Q1, 1,200 in Q2 and 1,500 in Q3. The selling price is ₩100,000 per unit. Finished goods at each quarter-end are kept at 20% of the next quarter's sales, and finished goods at the start of Q1 are 200 units.

## 4. The cash budget

The cash budget looks at **the timing of cash, not profit**. BP collects 60% of sales in the quarter of sale and 40% in the next quarter. Collections in Q1 of the previous year's Q4 receivables are ₩35 million. Raw materials are paid 50% in the quarter of purchase and 50% in the next, and ₩10 million is still owed for the previous Q4's purchases. Q1 cash payments for wages and expenses are ₩45 million, opening cash is ₩5 million and the minimum cash balance is ₩10 million.

In Q1 there is a surplus and no borrowing is needed. In the quarter just before sales rise, such as Q3, production and material purchases increase first and cash flows out ahead of collections, so even a profitable company may need short-term borrowing. The cash budget shows this timing in advance.

## 5. Static and flexible budgets

A **static budget** is prepared for one planned level of activity. A **flexible budget** recalculates the cost budget for the actual level of activity. If Q1 planned 1,000 units but 1,100 were actually sold, it is natural that variable costs exceed plan. When evaluating performance, compare with a flexible budget for 1,100 units to separate the volume effect from the effect of cost control.

## 6. Budgeting approaches and behavior

- **Incremental budgeting:** adds a percentage to last year's budget. Simple, but inefficiencies carry forward.
- **Zero-based budgeting:** justifies every activity again from zero. Thorough, but time-consuming.
- **Rolling budget:** adds a new quarter each time one ends, so it always looks 12 months ahead.
- **Participative budgeting:** staff take part in preparing the budget. Acceptance is high, but slack creeps in easily.

<Callout type="warning">
Evaluating performance against budgets creates **budgetary slack**: managers try to budget revenue low and costs high so targets are easy to hit. And where unspent budget at year-end means a smaller budget next year, needless spending bunches up. This is the tension of using a budget as both a planning tool and an evaluation standard. It is reduced with devices such as relative evaluation, ex-post adjustment of targets and penalties for slack.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

Company BQ expects to sell 2,000 units in April, 2,500 in May and 3,000 in June. Finished goods at each month-end are kept at 30% of next month's sales, and inventory at the start of April is 500 units. What is production in April and May? If 70% of sales are collected in the month of sale and 30% the following month, and the selling price is ₩40,000 per unit, what are May's collections?

April production is $2{,}000 + 2{,}500 \times 30\% - 500 = 2{,}250$ units and May production $2{,}500 + 3{,}000 \times 30\% - 750 = 2{,}650$ units. May collections are 70% of May's ₩100 million of sales, ₩70 million, plus 30% of April's ₩80 million, ₩24 million: ₩94 million in total.
