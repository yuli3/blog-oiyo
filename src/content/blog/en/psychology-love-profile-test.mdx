## Your Complete Romantic Profile

Most relationship conflicts aren't about what couples argue about — they're about *how* each person is wired. Two people can deeply love each other and still struggle because they speak different emotional languages, carry different attachment histories, and handle disagreement in incompatible ways.

This integrated profiler combines three clinically grounded frameworks into a single 28-question test:

1. **Attachment Style** — how securely or anxiously you bond (Bowlby & Ainsworth, 1950s–70s)
2. **Love Language** — how you give and receive love (Gary Chapman, 1992)
3. **Conflict Style** — how you approach disagreement (Thomas-Kilmann model)

Understanding all three together gives you a far more complete picture than any single assessment. Couples who understand their combined profiles report fewer misunderstandings, faster repair after conflict, and stronger emotional safety.

The test has three sections (A, B, C). Answer each statement based on how you *actually* behave in relationships — not how you'd like to behave. Honest answers produce more useful results.

## Section A: The Four Attachment Styles

Attachment theory, developed by John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth, holds that our early experiences with caregivers create an internal "working model" of relationships that we carry into adulthood.

| Style | Core Pattern | In Relationships |
|---|---|---|
| **Secure** | Trust comes naturally; comfortable with intimacy and independence | Can ask for needs, repair conflict, tolerate partner's absence |
| **Anxious** | Fears abandonment; craves reassurance | Worries about partner's feelings, prone to protest behaviors |
| **Avoidant** | Values independence; discomfort with closeness | Pulls away when things deepen, suppresses emotional needs |
| **Disorganized** | Simultaneous desire for and fear of closeness | Contradictory behavior; often linked to unresolved early trauma |

<Callout type="note">
Attachment styles are not fixed diagnoses. Research consistently shows that secure attachment can be developed through intentional relationships, therapy, and self-awareness — regardless of your early history.
</Callout>

## Section B: The Five Love Languages

Gary Chapman's framework identifies five primary ways people express and receive love. Mismatches — when partners speak different love languages — account for much of the "I show love but they don't feel it" problem.

| Language | What it sounds like | What hurts if missing |
|---|---|---|
| **Words of Affirmation** | "I'm so proud of you." "Thank you for everything." | Criticism, silence, unexpressed appreciation |
| **Acts of Service** | Making coffee, handling logistics, helping without being asked | Broken promises, neglected responsibilities |
| **Receiving Gifts** | A small thoughtful token; remembering a preference | Forgotten occasions, impersonal gifts |
| **Quality Time** | Full attention; device-free conversations | Distracted presence; partner always busy |
| **Physical Touch** | Handholding, hugs, sitting close | Extended physical distance, coldness |

## Section C: Five Conflict Styles

Based on the Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument, your conflict style reflects how you balance **assertiveness** (getting your needs met) and **cooperativeness** (meeting others' needs).

| Style | Description | When it helps | When it hurts |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Collaborate** | Win-win; high assertive + high cooperative | Complex problems needing buy-in | Too slow for low-stakes issues |
| **Compromise** | Middle ground; partial satisfaction | Time pressure; equal power | Neither side fully satisfied |
| **Accommodate** | You yield; low assertive + high cooperative | Preserving relationship over issue | Builds resentment over time |
| **Avoid** | Withdraws from conflict | Genuinely trivial issues | Leaves core issues unresolved |
| **Compete** | Win-lose; high assertive + low cooperative | Emergencies needing quick decisions | Damages trust and safety |

## How to Use Your Combined Profile

**Example 1 — Anxious + Quality Time + Avoid:**  
You fear abandonment and crave connection, feel loved through undivided time together, but avoid conflict. When your partner seems distant, you'll feel unloved but won't say so — building resentment silently. The insight: surface your needs before they become pressure.

**Example 2 — Avoidant + Acts of Service + Compete:**  
You love by doing, prefer independence, and tend to win arguments. When stressed, you pull away and become controlling. The insight: recognize that your partner may need words and closeness, not just solutions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Is one attachment style healthier than the others?**  
A: Secure attachment leads to more satisfying relationships on average, but all styles are adaptive responses — they developed because they worked at some point. Any style can be shifted toward security with awareness and effort.

**Q: What if my partner and I have very different love languages?**  
A: Different languages are the norm, not the exception. The key isn't matching languages — it's learning to "speak" your partner's language intentionally, even when it doesn't come naturally to you.

**Q: My conflict style seems to change depending on the situation. Is that normal?**  
A: Absolutely. Most people use different styles in different contexts. The test reveals your *default* or most habitual mode, especially under stress.
