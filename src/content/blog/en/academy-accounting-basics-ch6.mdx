As a company grows, it delegates decision rights to divisions. Once authority is delegated, someone must be held responsible for the results and evaluated. Responsibility accounting divides the organization into **responsibility centers** and measures each center's performance on the items it **can control**. Budgets are covered in [chapter 4](/en/academy-accounting-basics-ch12/) and prices for internal transactions between divisions in [chapter 6](/en/academy-managerial-accounting-ch6/).

Under the **controllability principle**, managers are evaluated only on items they can control. Items the division head cannot influence, such as common costs allocated by head office or exchange rates, are excluded from the manager's evaluation. They may be included when looking at the division's own economic performance: evaluating the manager and evaluating the division serve different purposes.

Division X has sales of ₩100 million, operating profit of ₩10 million and invested assets of ₩50 million: a 10% margin, turnover of 2 and ROI of 20%.

## 3. The suboptimization problem of ROI

A new project is proposed for division X: an investment of ₩10 million earning ₩1.5 million a year in operating profit (ROI 15%). The company's cost of capital (minimum required return) is 12%.

- From the company's view: in this performance-measurement example, accounting ROI of 15% exceeds the 12% required return, so the investment increases current-period residual income. Value creation over the investment's whole life needs a separate cash-flow assessment.
- From the division head's view: with the project, ROI falls from 20% to $(1{,}000 + 150)/(5{,}000 + 1{,}000) ≈ 19.2\%$. A division head evaluated on ROI will want to reject it.

When the division's interest and the company's interest diverge, it is called **suboptimization**. A high-ROI division has a stronger incentive to reject investments that, as in this example, raise current-period RI but lower its ROI. There is also an incentive not to replace old (heavily depreciated) assets with new ones.

## 4. Residual income and EVA

**Residual income (RI)** is operating profit minus the minimum required return on invested assets.

$$
\text{RI} = \text{Operating profit} - \text{Invested assets} \times \text{Required return}
$$

Division X's current RI is $1{,}000 - 5{,}000 \times 12\% = 400$ (₩4 million). Adding the project raises it to $1{,}150 - 6{,}000 \times 12\% = 430$ (₩4.3 million). Unlike the falling divisional ROI, RI highlights an incremental ₩300,000 of performance in this example, reducing the incentive to reject the investment. But RI is an amount, which makes divisions of different sizes hard to compare.

### a. Current-period performance and whole-life investment value

Accounting ROI uses operating profit, not cash flow. Depreciation, revenue recognition and the measurement of invested assets can change annual ROI and RI for the same investment. **An increase in this year's RI does not by itself establish a positive net present value (NPV).** Assess an investment separately using incremental cash flows over its life, collection dates, residual value and a discount rate appropriate to its risk. These inputs are absent from this chapter's examples, so their NPV cannot be determined.

This does not mean RI and NPV are theoretically unrelated. Discounted RI can be related to NPV when profit, carrying amounts and cash flows are linked consistently over the investment's full life and capital charges are applied. [ACCA's performance-measurement article](https://www.accaglobal.com/uk/en/student/exam-support-resources/fundamentals-exams-study-resources/f5/technical-articles/performance-measurement.html) shows that a positive-NPV investment can have low initial ROI and RI and be rejected. One year's RI therefore does not automatically guarantee goal congruence.

**Economic value added (EVA)** is a form of residual income that deducts from after-tax operating profit the invested capital multiplied by the **weighted average cost of capital (WACC)**. Its hallmark is adjusting accounting figures toward economic substance, for example by capitalizing research and development.

<Callout type="warning">
All financial measures are vulnerable to easy ways of raising short-term profit. Cutting R&D, training and equipment maintenance improves this year's ROI and EVA but erodes future competitiveness. That is why the balanced scorecard ([chapter 8](/en/academy-managerial-accounting-ch8/)) emerged, which looks at non-financial measures — customers, internal processes, learning and growth — alongside financial ones. Also, if invested assets are measured at net carrying amount, ROI rises automatically as depreciation accumulates.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

Division Y's operating profit is ₩24 million, its invested assets ₩160 million and its required return 10%. How do ROI and residual income change if a project with an investment of ₩20 million and annual operating profit of ₩2.6 million is added? Under which measure would the division head accept the project?

Current ROI is 15% and residual income $2{,}400 - 16{,}000 \times 10\% = 800$ (₩8 million). The project's ROI is 13%. After adding it, ROI falls to $2{,}660 / 18{,}000 ≈ 14.8\%$, while residual income rises by ₩600,000 to $2{,}660 - 18{,}000 \times 10\% = 860$ (₩8.6 million). Under these current-period measures, RI creates an incentive to accept and ROI an incentive to reject. These calculations alone cannot determine the investment's whole-life NPV or the actual acceptance decision.
