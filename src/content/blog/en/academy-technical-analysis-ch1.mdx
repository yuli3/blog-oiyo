## Overview: What Does a Chart Tell You?

Two people look at the same chart. One says "time to buy," the other says "it's about to fall." The difference isn't the data — it's whether they know the *grammar* of reading a chart.

Analyzing a company's earnings and financials is **fundamental analysis**. Drawing trading decisions from the movement of price and volume themselves is **technical analysis**.

<Callout type="info">
**Goal of this lesson**: Understand the three premises of technical analysis and grasp Dow Theory and the concept of "trend" — the root of all chart analysis.
</Callout>

---

## 1. The Three Premises of Technical Analysis

Technical analysis rests on three assumptions.

| Premise | Meaning |
|---------|---------|
| **The market discounts everything** | Earnings, rates, sentiment — all information is already in the price |
| **Prices move in trends** | Once established, a direction tends to persist |
| **History repeats itself** | Investor psychology recurs, so patterns reappear |

In other words, instead of "why is it rising," you focus on **"what flow is it in right now."** Not the interpretation of news, but the price movement that results from it.

---

## 2. Fundamental vs. Technical Analysis

| Aspect | Fundamental | Technical |
|--------|-------------|-----------|
| Object | Earnings, financials, industry | Price, volume, charts |
| Core question | "What to buy" | "When to buy/sell" |
| Time horizon | Mid-to-long term | Short-to-mid term |
| Weakness | Poor at timing | Risk of ignoring fundamentals |

<Callout type="tip">
**The two are complements, not rivals.** Picking a good company (fundamental) and entering at a good time (technical) are most powerful when used together.
</Callout>

## 3. Dow Theory — The Root of All Chart Analysis

Dow Theory, formulated by Charles Dow in the late 19th century, is the starting point of modern technical analysis. Its core idea: **the market moves as three overlapping trends.**

- **Primary trend**: the major direction lasting a year or more (bull/bear market)
- **Secondary trend**: corrections of weeks to months against the primary
- **Minor trend**: ripples of a few days, closer to noise

### The Six Tenets (summary)

1. The averages discount everything
2. The market has three trends
3. The primary trend has three phases (accumulation → public participation → distribution)
4. The averages must confirm each other
5. **Volume confirms the trend**
6. A trend persists until a clear reversal signal appears

Tenet 6 is key — it's where the saying **"the trend is your friend"** comes from.

---

## 4. The Three Directions of Trend

A trend is ultimately defined by how highs and lows connect.

| Trend | High/low pattern | Response |
|-------|------------------|----------|
| **Uptrend** | Higher highs & higher lows (HH·HL) | Buy the pullback |
| **Downtrend** | Lower highs & lower lows (LH·LL) | Sell into bounces |
| **Sideways (range)** | Highs and lows within a band | Buy bottom, sell top |

```
Uptrend:   /\    /\
          /  \/    \/   ← lows (HL) keep rising
        /
```

The simplest signal that an uptrend has broken is **the previous low (HL) being taken out**. Knowing just this gives you a clear stop-loss reference.

---

## 5. The Right Mindset Toward Technical Analysis

<Callout type="warning">
Technical analysis is **not fortune-telling — it's probability management.** No indicator is right 100% of the time. **Limiting losses (risk management)** when a signal fails matters more than reading the chart.
</Callout>

- Don't trust a single indicator — judge by a **combination of signals**
- Plan your **exit (stop-loss/take-profit) before entering**
- Use only the indicators that fit your timeframe (swing, day, long-term)

---

## Key Takeaways

1. Technical analysis assumes "the market discounts everything, and prices move in trends."
2. Fundamental (what) and technical (when) analysis are complements.
3. Dow Theory's core: **a trend persists until a reversal signal appears.**
4. A trend is defined by the flow of highs and lows; breaking the prior low is the simplest reversal signal.
5. A chart is a probability and risk-management tool, not a crystal ball.
