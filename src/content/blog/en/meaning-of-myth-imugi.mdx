In Korean folklore, the imugi is remembered as a great serpent, or a being close to a dragon, in the stage before becoming a dragon. It is said to live in water, deep ponds, rivers, or mountain pools, and people believed that if it endured for a long time, it could rise to the heavens and become a dragon. But if it failed to obtain a yeouiju, if its true identity was revealed to humans, or if it missed the appointed moment, it could not become a dragon. Its form and conditions differ by tradition, but the core of the imugi is enormous potential that has not yet been completed.

## Core Myth — Representative Episodes

A common structure in imugi tales is the story of a being that lives in deep water and ascends as a dragon after completing a long span of time. When people saw a large pond or a whirlpool in a river, they imagined that an imugi lived there. Water is a place that holds invisible depth and the possibility of transformation, and the imugi is a power within that depth that has not yet received its name.

In some stories, the imugi must obtain a yeouiju in order to become a dragon. In other stories, it ascends only when a person says, "It is a dragon," while it fails if someone says, "It is a snake." The conditions vary by tradition, but it is interesting that transformation is not completed through personal effort alone. It is also connected to external recognition, words, and timing.

The imugi is also a figure of failure. Many stories describe it nearly becoming a dragon, only to be frustrated at the final moment. Yet that failure is not merely an object of ridicule. The imugi shows the anxiety felt when an immense possibility has not yet found its own form, and the danger faced by a being that has waited for a long time at the final threshold.

## Reading as an Archetype — From a Jungian Perspective

From a Jungian perspective, the imugi is an archetype of the **unfinished transformer** and the **shadow of potential**. It is already no ordinary snake, but it is not yet a dragon either. In modern terms, it resembles a person who is about to debut after long training, or the uneasy state just before a qualifying exam, promotion, or creative presentation.

The imugi archetype says that the last stage of growth may be the most dangerous. The fact of having endured for a long time does not guarantee completion. The right moment, a name, recognition, and self-control must come together. For this reason, the imugi is not a failed dragon, but the tension itself carried by a being just before completion.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q. Is an imugi a dragon or a snake?**

A. It differs by tradition, but it is usually understood as a great serpent before becoming a dragon, or as an unfinished dragon.

**Q. Why does an imugi fail to become a dragon?**

A. Because it fails to meet conditions such as obtaining a yeouiju, completing the required time, receiving the right words from a person, or observing a taboo. The details differ from story to story.
