Khonsu is a god in Egyptian mythology associated with the moon, time, travel, and healing. In Thebes, he forms the Theban triad as the son of Amun and Mut, and he is depicted as a child god with a side-lock of hair or as a figure bearing the lunar disk. His name is connected with "to travel," calling to mind the character of the moon as it moves across the night sky.

## Core Myth — Representative Story

Khonsu's core is the lunar cycle and the mobility of the night. If the sun god Ra symbolizes the center of daytime, Khonsu is the god who measures the hours of night and helps people find their way in darkness. The moon waxes and wanes, and that change teaches humans the passage of time and the rhythm of recovery.

Khonsu was also known as a god of healing. In later traditions, a story tells of Khonsu's divine statue being sent to a distant region to heal a sick princess. This scene shows that the moon god was regarded not merely as a celestial symbol, but as a power that restores balance to body and mind within darkness.

## Reading as Archetype — A Jungian View

From a Jungian perspective, Khonsu is the archetype of the **moon child** and the **night traveler**. Rather than a completed king, he is closer to a young energy that moves according to changing cycles. In modern terms, he resembles scenes of long recovery, nighttime care, uncertain journeys, and a growing young person finding their own rhythm.

The key to reading Khonsu is that time continues to flow even in darkness. Even when things are not as clear as daylight, the moon provides direction and rhythm. For that reason, the Khonsu archetype symbolizes gradual recovery rather than quick solutions, the patience to cross the night, and an attitude of trusting the changing rhythms of body and mind.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q. Is Khonsu a moon god?**

A. Yes. Khonsu is an important Egyptian moon god associated with the lunar cycle, movement through the night, and the flow of time.

**Q. Why is Khonsu depicted as a child?**

A. Because in the Theban triad he was regarded as the son of Amun and Mut. The child's side-lock expresses his identity as a young god.
