## Why post-processing matters

Post-processing adjusts brightness, color, and framing for an intended output. You may use a camera-rendered JPEG directly or develop RAW yourself. Not every photograph needs strong edits; documentary work also requires preserving the original and factual context.

Post-processing = the digital version of "developing and printing in a darkroom."

---

## RAW vs. JPEG

This is the most important foundational choice you'll make.

| Comparison | RAW and JPEG |
|------------|--------------|
| **File size** | RAW depends on pixel count, bit depth, and compression. JPEG depends on pixel count and quality settings. |
| **Compression** | RAW may be uncompressed, lossless, or lossy depending on camera/settings. JPEG is usually lossy. |
| **Room to edit** | RAW generally offers more latitude; JPEG is constrained by its already rendered data. Neither can recover unrecorded information. |
| **White balance** | RAW permits redevelopment from sensor data; JPEG adjustments work on rendered colors and can introduce artifacts. |
| **Brightness adjustment** | RAW latitude depends on the sensor, capture conditions, and remaining data. JPEG latitude also depends on the applied tone mapping. |
| **Camera processing** | RAW contains less finalized sensor data and metadata; processing varies by camera. JPEG has white balance, tone, and compression applied. |
| **Immediate delivery** | RAW normally needs development software. JPEG can be shared directly. |

**Choose by purpose**: RAW offers latitude for color and tone edits; JPEG supports immediate delivery and file efficiency. RAW cannot restore information that was never recorded. RAW+JPEG can serve both purposes.

## A complete overview of the basic panel

### Exposure sliders

- **Exposure**: overall brightness (-5 to +5 stops)
- **Highlights**: adjusts only the bright areas (sky, whites)
- **Shadows**: adjusts only the dark areas (shade, dark skin tones)
- **Whites**: the brightest point (guards against clipping)
- **Blacks**: the darkest point (sets deep black)

**Basic editing decisions**:
1. Inspect important areas and channel clipping. Lowering Highlights cannot restore unrecorded sky detail.
2. When raising Shadows, compare noise and the intended tonal rendering.
3. Blacks and Whites set tonal endpoints; they do not always need to move down or up.
4. Compare before/after at the final output size and judge whether the subject reads better.

### Presence (texture and clarity)

- **Texture**: emphasizes mid-tone texture (skin pores, fabric weave)
- **Clarity**: emphasizes mid-tone contrast (adds crispness)
- **Dehaze**: removes haze and atmospheric fog (effective for landscapes)
- **Vibrance**: selectively boosts less-saturated colors (natural-looking saturation)
- **Saturation**: adjusts overall saturation (use with caution)

## Color correction

### Adjusting white balance

RAW files let you **freely change** white balance even after the shot.

| Adjustment axis | Change in RAW rendering |
| --- | --- |
| Lower / higher Temp | Bluer / more yellow rendering |
| Lower / higher Tint | Greener / more magenta correction |

Check neutral references and the intended mood. These are rendering adjustments, not changes to the physical light source.

### The HSL/Color panel (adjusting by color)

A powerful tool for selecting and adjusting a single color at a time.

| Goal | Method |
|---------|------|
| Make the sky bluer | Adjust Blue Hue, raise Blue Saturation |
| Improve skin tone | Fine-tune Orange Hue/Saturation/Luminance |
| Make green grass pop | Raise Green Saturation |
| Emphasize a sunset | Raise Orange/Red Saturation |

### The tone curve

Applying an S-curve fine-tunes **contrast and color rendering**.

**The basic S-curve:**

  - Raise the top (highlights) → brighter highlights
  - Lower the bottom (shadows) → darker shadows
  - **Result**: increased contrast, more punch

**A film look**: leaving the bottom of the curve slightly above pure black (rather than at 0) lifts the blacks and creates a film-like feel.

## Detail adjustments

### Noise reduction

| Noise control | What to inspect |
| --- | --- |
| Luminance | Brightness mottling versus fine texture |
| Color | Colored speckles versus small color boundaries |

Do not apply one fixed number to every original. Compare skin, hair, fabric, and shadows at 100% magnification. Reduce strength when detail turns mushy or color edges bleed, then reassess at the final output size.

### Sharpening

**Sharpening:**

- **Amount**: sharpening strength. Adjust for the original and output size; inspect edge halos and exaggerated noise.
- **Radius**: the radius over which sharpening is applied
- **Detail**: controls emphasis on high-frequency texture and edges; check whether noise is exaggerated too.
- **Masking**: sharpens only certain areas (edge detection)
- **Masking preview**: hold Alt on Windows or Option on Mac while adjusting. White areas are targeted for sharpening; black areas are protected.

> **Rule of thumb**: noise reduction and sharpening pull in opposite directions — balance is key.

## Cropping and straightening

### Cropping

- **Basic crop**: press R → crop to your desired ratio
  - Free: any ratio
  - 1:1: square (Instagram)
  - 4:3, 16:9: standard screen ratios
  - 2:3: 35mm film ratio (default)

### Straightening a horizon

If a photo isn't level, straighten it automatically or rotate it manually.

Drag the Level tool along a horizontal or vertical line → auto-correct  
Fine-tune with the Angle slider

## Exporting

| Purpose | Recommended settings |
|------|----------|
| Print | Check the lab's file, profile, and PPI requirements; choose physical size and pixel dimensions together |
| Web/social media | Check platform pixel dimensions, format, and compression; verify sRGB compatibility for ordinary SDR sharing |
| Archiving | Back up camera originals and editing settings separately; TIFF/DNG conversions alone do not replace original preservation |

Screen display depends on pixel dimensions and the viewing environment; 72dpi metadata is not a universal web-quality standard. Distinguish print PPI from a printer's ink-dot DPI.

## A sample before/after workflow

**A landscape comparison workflow**:

1. Prepare the original and a virtual copy for comparison.
2. Adjust highlights and shadows while inspecting remaining sky data and foreground noise.
3. Set tonal endpoints and check clipping in important areas.
4. Test texture, clarity, and dehaze without introducing halos or exaggerated contrast.
5. Check color edges and other subjects when adjusting blue skies or greens.
6. Compare at 100% and final output size. If the original already meets the purpose, further editing is unnecessary.

## Practice quiz — 5 questions

**Q1.** What's the biggest advantage of a RAW file?
- ① Smaller file size
- ② You can freely adjust white balance and exposure after the shot
- ③ You can share it on social media instantly
- ④ The camera optimizes it automatically

**Answer**: ②

---

**Q2.** What's the main effect of lowering the Highlights slider?
- ① Dark areas get brighter
- ② It adjusts bright-area tones within remaining data
- ③ Overall brightness drops
- ④ Color saturation decreases

**Answer**: ②

---

**Q3.** If a photo shot at high ISO shows colored speckles, which slider fixes it?
- ① Clarity
- ② Luminance Noise Reduction
- ③ Color Noise Reduction
- ④ Sharpening

**Answer**: ③

---

**Q4.** What happens when you apply an S-curve to the tone curve?
- ① The photo gets darker overall
- ② Contrast increases
- ③ Saturation increases
- ④ Noise is removed

**Answer**: ②

---

**Q5.** What should you first check when exporting for web/social media?
- ① Set every file's dpi metadata to 72
- ② Platform pixel dimensions, format, compression, and color compatibility
- ③ Always increase to print resolution
- ④ RAW never needs exporting

**Answer**: ②. DPI metadata alone does not guarantee screen size or quality.
