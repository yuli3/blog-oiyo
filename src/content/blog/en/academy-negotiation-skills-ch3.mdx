## 1. Positions and interests are not the same thing

In negotiation, a position is the demand stated out loud. An interest is the reason, need, worry, or goal behind that demand. "Please lower the price" is a position. "I'm working with a fixed budget and need to stay under it" is closer to an interest.

Fight over positions alone, and it turns into a tug-of-war over concessions. Understand the interests, and you can often solve the problem with a different set of terms entirely. That's why a good negotiator asks not just what the other side wants, but why they want it.

## 2. A comparison table

| Situation | Position | Possible interest |
|------|------|-------------|
| Deadline negotiation | "I need this by the end of the week" | Launch schedule, a report to their boss, low inventory |
| Rent negotiation | "Please lower the rent" | Cash flow, intent to stay long-term, upfront cost concerns |
| Team assignment | "I can't take this on" | Skill gap, an already-overloaded schedule, unclear role |
| Purchase dispute | "I want a refund" | Quality complaint, need to rebuild trust, need a replacement |

The same position can hide several different interests. Rather than jumping to conclusions, the right posture is to ask clarifying questions.

## 3. What good questions sound like

"Why do you think that?" can sometimes sound like an accusation. Instead, try exploratory questions like "Could you tell me more about why that condition matters to you?", "What's the part you're most concerned about?", or "Is there anything besides price that could be adjusted?"

Questions are a tool for gathering information before you try to persuade anyone. Negotiators who ask well tend to discover more options at the table.

## 4. Clarify your own interests too

It's not enough to read the other side's interests — you need to be clear on your own. Whether what you're really protecting is your reputation, your time, your costs, or your quality will shape your entire strategy. If you don't know your own interests, you end up clinging to conditions that don't actually matter.

## 5. Key takeaways

- A position is the demand on the surface; an interest is the reason behind it.
- Even when positions clash, looking at interests can reveal a different solution.
- Exploratory questions help surface hidden concerns and priorities.
- Being clear about your own interests cuts down on unnecessary stubbornness.
