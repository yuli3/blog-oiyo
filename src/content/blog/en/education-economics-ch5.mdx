# Microeconomics — Profit Is Decided at the Margin, Not on Average

If [Introduction to Economics, Chapter 4](/en/academy-economics-basics-ch4/) wrote down MR = MC, this chapter works through the two steps behind it. First, find the cheapest combination of inputs for a given output (cost minimisation); then choose output using that cost (profit maximisation). This is also where we see why a firm shuts down in the short run when $P<AVC$, not when $P<ATC$.

## 1. Production functions and isoquants

The production function $Q=F(K,L)$ gives the maximum output from capital $K$ and labour $L$. The **marginal product** $MP_L=\partial Q/\partial L$ is the extra output from one more unit of labour, holding other inputs fixed; it usually diminishes.

An **isoquant** joins input combinations that yield the same output. The absolute value of its slope is the **marginal rate of technical substitution (MRTS)** — how much capital one unit of labour can replace — and equals $MP_L/MP_K$. It is the producer's counterpart of the indifference curve and MRS in Chapter 4.

## 2. Cost minimisation

The condition for producing output $Q$ as cheaply as possible, with wage $w$ and rental rate of capital $r$, has the same structure as the consumer's optimum.

<FormulaBox
  label="Cost-minimisation condition"
  formula="MRTS=\frac{MP_L}{MP_K}=\frac{w}{r}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{MP_L}{w}=\frac{MP_K}{r}"
  description="Marginal product per won must be equal. If labour's marginal product per won is higher, cutting capital and adding labour produces the same output more cheaply."
/>

With $Q=K^{0.5}L^{0.5}$, $w=10{,}000$ won and $r=40{,}000$ won, $MP_L/MP_K=K/L=w/r=1/4$, so $L=4K$. To produce 100 units we need $KL=10{,}000$, and $4K^2=10{,}000$ gives $K=50$ and $L=200$. The cost is $1\times 200+4\times 50=400$ (in units of 10,000 won), i.e. 4 million won.

When wages rise, firms substitute capital for labour to produce the same output (the **substitution effect**). If higher costs also cut output, labour demand falls further (the **output effect**). This is why both effects matter when discussing the employment impact of a minimum wage.

**Marginal and average.** If your exam average is 70 and your next score is 60, the average falls; if it is 80, the average rises. Costs work the same way. Where MC < ATC, ATC falls; where MC > ATC, it rises. So MC crosses ATC at its minimum. In the example, solving $40+2Q=200/Q+40+Q$ gives $Q=\sqrt{200}\approx 14.1$, where ATC is about 683,000 won.

## 4. The competitive firm's short-run decision

A price-taker has $MR=P$, so it produces where $P=MC$. It then decides whether to produce at all. Fixed costs of 2 million won are paid even if the firm shuts, so the question is "do I cover variable costs?"

- $P=800{,}000$ won: $80=40+2Q$ gives $Q=20$. AVC is 600,000 won and ATC 700,000 won. Profit is 100,000 won per unit, 2 million won in total.
- $P=450{,}000$ won: $Q=2.5$. AVC is 425,000 won < 450,000 won, so the firm produces. Price is below ATC, so it makes a loss, but by producing it earns 62,500 won above variable cost, which covers part of the fixed cost.
- $P=300{,}000$ won: $P<AVC$ at every $Q$ (minimum 400,000 won), so the firm shuts down. Its loss is limited to the fixed cost of 2 million won.

So the competitive firm's short-run supply curve is the MC curve above the minimum of AVC. Chapter 6 adds these up into industry supply.

## 5. The long run and returns to scale

In the long run all inputs are variable, so there are no fixed costs. If multiplying all inputs by $t$ raises output by more than $t$ times, there are **increasing returns to scale**; exactly $t$ times, **constant returns**; less, **decreasing returns**. For $Q=K^aL^b$ these correspond to $a+b>1$, $=1$ and $<1$.

With increasing returns, long-run average cost falls (economies of scale). If it keeps falling over the whole range of market demand, supply by a single firm is cheapest. This is the **natural monopoly** of Chapter 7.

<Callout type="warning">
MR = MC is only a first-order condition. For profit to be maximised, MC must cut MR from below; where MC is falling, the crossing can be a profit minimum. And spreading fixed costs over output to compute a "unit cost", then stopping production because the price is below it, is a short-run error. Fixed costs already committed and unavoidable should not enter the decision.
</Callout>


### A. Deriving the cost function directly

Let us lift the calculation of section 2 to a general formula in output $Q$ and factor prices $w,r$. Substituting the cost-minimising condition $L=(r/w)K$ into $Q=K^{0.5}L^{0.5}$ gives the **conditional factor demands** and the **cost function**.

<FormulaBox
  label="Conditional factor demands and cost function for Cobb-Douglas"
  formula="L^c=Q\sqrt{\frac{r}{w}},\qquad K^c=Q\sqrt{\frac{w}{r}},\qquad C(w,r,Q)=2Q\sqrt{wr}"
  description="With w=1, r=4 and Q=100, L=200, K=50 and C=400, matching section 2. Differentiating the cost function with respect to w gives labour demand (Shephard's lemma): ∂C/∂w = Q√(r/w) = L^c."
/>

For the general form $Q=K^aL^b$, $C\propto Q^{1/(a+b)}$. Long-run average cost $C/Q\propto Q^{1/(a+b)-1}$ therefore falls if $a+b>1$ (increasing returns), is constant if $a+b=1$, and rises if $a+b<1$. **Returns to scale are a property of the production function, and they carry over into the shape of the cost curve.**

### B. Short-run cost lies above long-run cost

Fix capital at $\bar K=50$. Producing $Q$ then needs $L=Q^2/\bar K$ units of labour, so short-run total cost is $SRTC=4\times50+Q^2/50$. At $Q=100$, $SRTC=400$, equal to long-run cost; but at $Q=150$, $SRTC=650$, which is 50 more than the long-run cost of $2\times150\times2=600$. Capital cannot be changed, so output can only be raised with ever more expensive labour.

## Check your understanding

**Problem 1.** A competitive firm has $TC=100+20Q+Q^2$ (in ten-thousand won) and faces $P=80$. Find its optimal output and profit.

**Solution.** $MC=20+2Q=80$ gives $Q=30$. ATC is $100/30+20+30\approx53.3$, so profit per unit is about 26.7 and total profit is $80\times30-(100+600+900)=800$. The rule is $P=MC$, not $P=ATC$.

**Problem 2.** With $Q=K^{0.5}L^{0.5}$, $w=4$ and $r=1$, find the minimum cost and factor mix for an output of 50. If capital is held at that level while output rises to 80, how much more does short-run cost exceed long-run cost?

**Solution.** $C=2Q\sqrt{wr}=2\times50\times2=200$, with $L^c=50\sqrt{1/4}=25$ and $K^c=50\sqrt{4}=100$. Holding capital at 100 and producing $Q=80$ needs $L=80^2/100=64$, so $SRTC=4\times64+1\times100=356$. In the long run it would cost $2\times80\times2=320$, so the short run costs 36 more. That is why costs climb steeply when output is raised in a hurry.
