Consolidated financial statements present a parent and its subsidiaries as **a single economic entity**. Even if they are separate companies in law, when the parent can in effect use the subsidiary's resources as it wishes, the information is faithful only if all those resources appear in one set of statements. The standard is K-IFRS 1110 *Consolidated Financial Statements* (IFRS 10). Accounting for the business combination at acquisition was covered in [chapter 1](/en/education-accounting-ch8/).

## 1. The three elements of control

An investor controls an investee when it has **all three** of the following.

    ["Exposure to variable returns", "Rights to returns that vary with the investee's performance", "Dividends, changes in the value of the investment, fees, tax benefits"],

/>

A fund manager has power to direct a fund but is usually an **agent** acting for other investors and so does not control the fund. Principal or agent status is judged by the remuneration structure, removal rights and the size of the manager's own holding.

### A. When voting majority and control diverge

- **Control without a majority:** an entity holding 45% may have **de facto control** if the other 55% is spread across thousands of small shareholders and past attendance at general meetings has been low. Agreements with other shareholders and potential voting rights (substantive conversion rights or options) are also considered.
- **A majority without control:** a company in rehabilitation proceedings whose relevant activities are directed by a court-appointed administrator, or where the articles require another shareholder's consent for key decisions (possibly joint control).

Control is **reassessed** when facts and circumstances change.

## 2. Basic consolidation procedures

Consolidation adds together the financial statements of the parent and its subsidiaries line by line and then removes duplications.

1. Combine like items of assets, liabilities, income and expenses.
2. Eliminate the parent's **investment** against the corresponding **equity** of the subsidiary, and recognize goodwill and non-controlling interests.
3. Eliminate intragroup transactions, receivables and payables, and unrealized profits ([chapter 4](/en/academy-advanced-accounting-ch4/)).

Accounting policies are aligned, and if a subsidiary's reporting date differs, a difference of up to three months may be used with adjustments.

## 4. Exclusions from consolidation and separate financial statements

A parent in principle consolidates all its subsidiaries. As an exception, an **investment entity** — one that manages investments solely for investment returns and evaluates performance on a fair value basis — does not consolidate its subsidiaries but measures them at fair value. An intermediate parent meeting certain conditions may be exempt from preparing consolidated statements.

Apart from consolidated statements, a parent may prepare **separate financial statements**, in which investments in subsidiaries are measured at cost, at fair value or using the equity method. Korean listed companies publish both consolidated and separate statements.

<Callout type="warning">
Consolidated statements do not show resources that can be legally claimed. However much cash appears on consolidation, if it is tied up in a regulated foreign subsidiary the parent's creditors cannot use it. Conversely, if the parent has not guaranteed a subsidiary's debts, the parent's shareholders' loss is limited to the investment. In credit analysis, read consolidated and separate statements together, and for groups with large NCI look separately at profit attributable to owners of the parent.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

Company A holds 40% of B's ordinary shares. The other 60% is spread among more than 20,000 individual investors, and average attendance at general meetings over the past five years has been 25%. A has appointed four of B's seven directors. A also holds 55% of C, but under a shareholders' agreement C's budget, investments and appointment of management all require the consent of the second-largest shareholder (45%). Does A control B and C?

For B, A holds less than a majority, but the remaining shares are widely dispersed and, given past attendance and its record of appointing directors, A has the practical ability to direct the relevant activities. It has de facto control, and B is consolidated. For C, A holds a majority, but decisions on the relevant activities require another shareholder's consent, so A has no sole control. The two shareholders control C jointly, so it is treated as a joint arrangement (joint venture) and the equity method is considered ([chapter 5](/en/academy-advanced-accounting-ch5/)).
