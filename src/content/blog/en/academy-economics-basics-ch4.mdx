# Introduction to Economics — Profit Is Decided at the Margin, Not the Average

The production function links inputs to output, and the cost function turns those inputs into prices. Maximizing profit is not drawing the hill of total revenue minus total cost; it is comparing **the revenue and cost of making one more unit**.

## 1. In the short run, some inputs are fixed

If only labour is variable, the marginal product of labour $\mathrm{MPL}$ rises over some range and then falls. It falls "not because the workers are worse" but because they share fixed capital. In the long run capital also varies, so this short-run decline does not carry over unchanged to the long run.

<FormulaBox
  label="Profit maximization"
  formula="MR = MC"
  description="If MR > MC, making one more unit raises profit. The equality describes an interior solution; with discrete units it becomes an inequality at which you stop just before."
/>

Under perfect competition the firm is a price taker, so $\mathrm{MR}=P$. For a monopoly, the next chapter shows $\mathrm{MR}<P$. Memorizing the two as a single formula in an introductory course leaves you unable to tell market structures apart.

With fixed costs of 10 million won, $\mathrm{MC}=2Q$ (in units of 10,000 won) and a competitive price of 400,000 won, $40=2Q$ gives $Q=20$. Variable cost is $∫2Q\,dQ = Q²$ = 4 million won, total cost 14 million won, revenue 8 million won and profit −6 million won. **Even so, Q = 0 may be worse.** Stopping production loses the entire 10 million won of fixed costs. The short-run shutdown point is where $P<\mathrm{AVC}$.

## 2. MC crosses AC at its minimum

When the marginal is below the average, it pulls the average down; when it is above, it pulls it up. So $\mathrm{MC}$ passes through the minimum points of $\mathrm{ATC}$ and $\mathrm{AVC}$. Drawing a "U-shaped cost curve" without this geometry leads people to read the shutdown point and the break-even point as the same point.

Break-even is at $P=\mathrm{ATC}$ and shutdown at $P=\mathrm{AVC}$. The accounting intuition that "spreading fixed costs makes a profit" collides with the fact that fixed costs do not enter the decision. Fixed costs already paid are close to the sunk costs of Chapter 1. Only recoverable opportunity costs are economic fixed costs.

## 3. Returns to scale and long-run AC

If increasing all inputs in the same proportion raises output by more than that proportion, there are increasing returns to scale; by the same proportion, constant returns; by less, decreasing returns. The range in which long-run AC falls often goes with increasing returns to scale, but there are also causes outside scale, such as learning effects and purchasing power. A natural monopoly arises when long-run AC falls over the whole range of market demand, so that one firm produces more cheaply than two.

<Callout type="warning">
MR = MC is the first-order condition for **maximizing** profit; it does not mean profit is positive. The second-order condition requires MC to cross MR from below. If the equality holds where MC is falling, that point may be a minimum.
</Callout>

In factor markets, cost minimization means $\mathrm{MPL}/w = \mathrm{MPK}/r$. When wages rise, firms substitute capital for labour. Looking only at substitution and ignoring the output effect understates the fall in labour demand.

## Check your understanding

A competitive firm faces a price of 500,000 won, with $\mathrm{AVC}$ = 300,000 won, $\mathrm{ATC}$ = 600,000 won and $\mathrm{MC}$ = 500,000 won. What is its short-run output? Since $P=\mathrm{MC}$ and $P>\mathrm{AVC}$, it produces at that quantity. It loses 100,000 won per unit, but shutting down would lose the entire fixed cost of $\mathrm{ATC}-\mathrm{AVC}$ = 300,000 won per unit.
