# Yield Curves: Rate Expectations, Term Premia and Recession Signals

A yield curve connects rates across maturities at one valuation date. Match currency, credit risk, liquidity, embedded options and compounding conventions as far as possible. Equal credit ratings do not make different corporate bonds a single risk-free curve.

Here we focus on nominal government rates. Coupon-bond yield to maturity, spot rates discounting single dated payments, and forward rates for future intervals are different objects. Slope is observed; attributing it solely to growth, inflation or policy expectations is an additional inference.

## 2. Expectations Are Not the Whole Long Rate

In a simplified log-yield decomposition, think of a long rate as an average of expected short rates plus a term premium. Pure expectations is a restrictive benchmark that leaves out this premium. Term premia compensate for risks of holding long bonds and are estimated through models; do not assume they are always positive or constant.

Liquidity preference emphasizes compensation for longer holding periods. Segmentation and preferred-habitat explanations emphasize maturity-specific demand and supply, not literal independence of all maturity markets. A falling long yield alone cannot identify whether expected short rates or the premium changed.

## 3. Calculate Spreads and a Forward Rate

These are hypothetical annual effective yields, not live quotes. With a 10-year yield of 3.8%, a 2-year yield of 4.2% and a 3-month yield of 4.7%, 10Y−2Y is −0.4 percentage points=−40bp; 10Y−3M is −0.9 percentage points=−90bp. State dates and maturities; do not substitute one spread for another.

Separately, take risk-free annual-compounded spot rates s₁=4% and s₂=3.5%. Matching the two-year accumulation gives the one-year forward rate starting in one year:

<FormulaBox formula="(1+s_2)^2=(1+s_1)(1+f_{1,2})" />

(1.035)²/1.04−1≈0.030024, or about 3.0024%. This is a no-arbitrage pricing relation, not a guarantee of the short rate actually observed next year.

## 4. A Recession Probability Is Not a Prophecy

The New York Fed’s familiar public indicator uses the 10Y−3M spread to estimate the probability of a U.S. recession twelve months ahead. Do not treat 10Y−2Y as that model’s input. Country, sample, maturities and the target event definition matter.

A 2025 New York Fed research post demonstrates substantial uncertainty around estimated probabilities themselves. A point estimate can hide model and parameter uncertainty. A negative spread alone does not supply an arbitrary numerical probability or a trading date.

Check date and rate definitions → maturity pair → changes in both legs → possible expectations and premium explanations → model and other evidence. Steepening driven by short rates falling differs from steepening driven by long rates rising. Portfolio returns also depend on duration, credit, currency and costs.

## Check Your Understanding

**Q. The 10-year yield falls 20bp and the 2-year falls 40bp. What happens to the spread?**

Solution: Δ(10Y−2Y)=−20−(−40)=+20bp. The spread widens, but an initial −50bp becomes −30bp and remains inverted. Steepening, ending inversion and an improving economy are not identical events.
