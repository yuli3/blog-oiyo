## The Spice Imperative

In 15th-century Europe, spices — pepper, nutmeg, cinnamon — were worth their weight in gold. Without refrigeration, spices preserved food and masked spoilage. But the overland routes to Asia were blocked by the Ottoman Empire and Islamic kingdoms, each taking their cut.

The solution: find a sea route to Asia directly.

---

## Portugal's Long March South

Portugal's **Prince Henry the Navigator (1394–1460)** never sailed beyond sight of land personally, but he established a research center and systematically funded expeditions down the African coast.

In 1488, **Bartolomeu Dias** rounded Africa's southern tip — the **Cape of Good Hope**. The Indian Ocean was reachable.

In 1498, **Vasco da Gama** completed the first direct sea voyage from Europe to India — reaching Calicut (Kozhikode). Portugal began dominating the spice trade almost immediately.

## Columbus: The Accidental Discovery

Spanish-sponsored **Christopher Columbus** believed he could reach Asia by sailing *west*. He drastically underestimated Earth's size — but sailed anyway in 1492.

In October he landed in the Bahamas — and died believing he had reached Asia ("the Indies"). He never understood he had found a continent unknown to Europeans.

<Callout type="tip">
**Amerigo Vespucci**: Italian explorer Amerigo Vespucci recognized that Columbus's lands were a completely new continent. The new continents were named "America" in his honor — which is why we don't call North America "Columbia."
</Callout>

## Magellan: The First Circumnavigation

In 1519, Portuguese explorer **Ferdinand Magellan**, sailing for Spain, set out with 5 ships and 265 men to circumnavigate the globe.

In 1521, Magellan was killed in the Philippines in a skirmish with local warriors. His officer **Juan Elcano** took command, and in September 1522 brought 1 ship and 18 survivors back to Spain — completing the **first circumnavigation of the Earth**. The spherical Earth was now confirmed by actual navigation.

## The Columbian Exchange

The most profound consequence of the Age of Exploration wasn't geographic discovery — it was the **biological exchange** between hemispheres, reshaping the ecology of every continent.

**Old World → New World**: horses, cattle, pigs, wheat, rice, smallpox, measles, influenza
**New World → Old World**: potatoes, maize (corn), tomatoes, chili peppers, chocolate, tobacco, sweet potatoes

The humble potato transformed Europe. More calorie-dense per acre than any Old World crop, it fueled European population growth in the 17th–18th centuries.

In the opposite direction, Old World diseases — against which Indigenous Americans had no immunity — killed an estimated **70–90% of the native population** within a century. From roughly 50 million people in 1500, the Americas may have had only 5 million by 1600.

---

## The Dark Legacy

The Age of Exploration opened the world — and inaugurated **colonialism, the transatlantic slave trade, and the decimation of indigenous populations** on a global scale. The wealth that flowed to Europe from these conquests shaped everything from the Industrial Revolution to present-day global inequality.

The light and shadow of the Age of Exploration remain inseparably intertwined, and debated, to this day.
