## A Draft Is Raw Material, Not a Finished Product

Many people feel like they can't write because they expect a draft to come out finished. But good writing rarely arrives that way the first time. A draft is raw material where you've gotten your thinking out; revision is the process of shaping that material into something a reader can actually read.

Revising well means looking at the big problems before fixing individual sentences. Correcting a comma in a piece whose structure is broken rarely produces good writing.

## Revise from Big to Small

The order of revision matters. If you check spelling first, you'll miss the fact that the purpose and structure are still unclear.

| Step | What to check | Question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Step 1 | Purpose | Why was this written |
| Step 2 | Structure | Does the flow feel natural |
| Step 3 | Paragraphs | Does each paragraph hold one idea |
| Step 4 | Sentences | Are there long or vague sentences |
| Step 5 | Wording | Are there typos or awkward phrases |

Following this order improves writing significantly while spending less time overall.

## The Power of Letting It Sit

If you can, put your draft aside for a while after writing it. Even a few hours changes how you see the piece. Right after writing, the context is still so vivid in your head that gaps are hard to spot.

Reading it again after some time brings you closer to a reader's perspective. You'll notice missing explanations, repeated sentences, and paragraphs that suddenly veer off-topic more easily.

## Cutting Is the Strongest Edit

Revision involves more subtracting than adding. Be willing to cut repeated examples, sentences that re-explain something already said, and background that has nothing to do with the core point.

When cutting, don't get sentimental about individual sentences. Even a well-written sentence has little reason to stay if it doesn't serve the piece's purpose. The piece belongs to the reader's understanding, not to any one sentence.

## How to Get Feedback

When you show your writing to someone else, ask specific questions instead of "What do you think?"

1. What reads as the core claim?
2. Which paragraph is hard to follow?
3. Where does it feel too long or repetitive?
4. Is it clear what to do after reading this?

Specific questions get specific feedback. You don't have to act on every note, but if multiple people flag the same spot, look at it again.

## A Revision Checklist

```text
[] Does the title and first sentence show the piece's purpose?
[] Do the paragraphs flow in a natural order?
[] Does any single sentence carry too many ideas?
[] Is a concrete explanation attached after every abstract word?
[] Have repeated phrases and filler been trimmed?
[] Have you double-checked typos, numbers, and names?
```

A checklist doesn't replace writing skill, but it's a solid device for catching mistakes.

## Key Takeaways

- A draft is raw material for revision, not a finished product.
- Revise in order — purpose, structure, paragraphs, sentences, wording — from big to small.
- Reading again after time has passed brings you closer to a reader's perspective and reveals gaps.
- Cutting is the strongest edit for sharpening a piece's purpose.
- Feedback helps most when requested with specific questions.
