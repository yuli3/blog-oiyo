# Macroeconomics — To Explain the Growth Rate Inside the Model, We Must Get Past Diminishing Returns

In the Solow model of [Chapter 11](/en/education-economics-ch22/), the source of long-run growth per person is technology, but its rate of progress was given from outside. **Endogenous growth theory** brings the creation of technology inside the model. This chapter first looks at growth accounting, which measures the contribution of technology, and then at models that explain technology.

## 1. Growth accounting: the Solow residual

Writing the Cobb-Douglas production function $Y=AK^{\alpha}L^{1-\alpha}$ in growth rates gives

<FormulaBox
  label="Growth accounting"
  formula="g_Y = \alpha\, g_K + (1-\alpha)\, g_L + g_A"
  description="α is capital's share of income. What remains of output growth after subtracting the contributions of capital and labour, g_A, is the growth rate of total factor productivity (TFP), the Solow residual."
/>

If output grows by 5% a year, capital by 6% and labour by 1%, with $\alpha=0.4$, then $g_A=5-0.4\times 6-0.6\times 1=2\%$. Forty per cent of growth came from the residual. The residual mixes technological progress with rising education, better resource allocation and measurement error. That is why Abramovitz called it "a measure of our ignorance."

## 2. The AK model: without diminishing returns, growth never stops

If the production function is $Y=AK$, the marginal product of capital is constant at $A$. The Solow equation becomes $\Delta k=sAk-(\delta+n)k$, and the growth rate of capital per worker is

<FormulaBox
  label="Growth rate in the AK model"
  formula="g = sA - (\delta + n)"
  description="With A = 0.5 and δ+n = 0.05, s = 0.2 gives growth of 5% and s = 0.3 gives 10%. The saving rate changes the growth rate itself — a conclusion directly opposed to the Solow model."
/>

If $K$ is read as broad capital that includes not only machines but also human capital and knowledge, one can argue that accumulating capital creates new knowledge that offsets diminishing returns. Learning by doing (Arrow) and the externalities of human capital (Lucas) are the grounds for this interpretation.

## 3. Ideas are non-rival

Romer modelled technology as **ideas**. The key property of ideas is **non-rivalry**: one firm using a blueprint does not reduce the amount another firm can use. Creating a new idea therefore involves a fixed cost, but once it exists the cost of using it again is almost zero. This produces increasing returns to scale for the economy as a whole.

Patents provide research incentives but create the deadweight loss of monopoly ([Micro Chapter 7](/en/education-economics-ch7/)). The balance between the incentive to invent and diffusion is the design problem of the patent system. It has the same structure as the public goods problem of [Micro Chapter 10](/en/education-economics-ch10/).

## 4. Scale effects and their critique

In early versions of the Romer model, more researchers mean faster growth. Countries with large populations, or with growing numbers of researchers, should grow faster. Jones pointed out that although the number of US researchers multiplied in the late twentieth century, the growth rate did not rise. If new ideas become ever harder to find (**semi-endogenous growth**), the long-run growth rate depends on the growth of the research workforce, and saving rates and policy once again affect only levels.

## 5. Fundamental causes: institutions

If capital, human capital and technology are the **proximate causes** of growth, the debate about **fundamental causes** asks why they accumulate in some countries and not in others. Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson argue that inclusive institutions, such as secure property rights and checks on power, create the incentives to invest and innovate, and they estimated the effect of institutions using differences in settlement conditions during the colonial period.

<Callout type="warning">
The finding that TFP contributes 40% in growth accounting is "the share not explained by capital and labour," not a number that proves the effect of R&D. Moreover, if convergence is clear in the data, a pure AK model goes too far; if growth moves with saving, a pure Solow model falls short. The two views may each partly fit different periods and countries. The two models are compared in [Chapter 22](/en/education-economics-ch33/).
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

If output grows by 4%, capital by 5% and labour by 0.5%, and capital's share of income is 0.3, what is TFP growth? $4-0.3\times 5-0.7\times 0.5=2.15\%$. About 54% of growth came from the residual. In the AK model with $A=0.4$ and $\delta+n=0.06$, a saving rate of 0.25 gives growth of $0.25\times 0.4-0.06=4\%$.
