## 1. Types of difficult counterparts

Difficult counterparts show up in several forms during negotiation. Some refuse everything outright. Some apply emotional pressure. Some withhold information. Some keep delaying a decision. Treat all of them the same way, and the negotiation tends to get even more tangled.

Start by figuring out whether the behavior is strategy, anxiety, or a lack of authority. Someone who looks hardline may actually be worried about getting internal approval from their own side.

## 2. How to respond

| Situation | Response to avoid | Response that helps |
|------|----------------|------------------|
| Aggressive language | Firing back | Focus on the issue, not the tone |
| Repeated refusal | Offering an even bigger concession | Ask why they're refusing |
| Missing information | Applying pressure based on guesses | List out exactly what information is needed |
| Stalled decision | Impatient pushing | Agree on when the next decision point will be |

Difficult counterparts need structure more than strong words. Separate out what's stuck, who has the authority to decide, and what information is actually needed.

## 3. Questions that break deadlocks

Deadlocks happen when both sides keep circling the same issue. In that moment, instead of "What should the price be?", try questions like "Is there something besides price making this hard to agree on?", "What's the biggest reason this condition is difficult?", or "Is there anything small we could agree on first?"

Splitting the issue into smaller pieces gets the negotiation moving again. If a full agreement is out of reach, you can start by agreeing on principles, timeline, information sharing, or how the next meeting will run.

## 4. Sometimes pausing is the strategy

Talking longer doesn't always make things better. When emotions run high or information is missing, a break, a re-think, or a separate follow-up meeting can help. Just make sure that when you pause, you leave a condition for resuming — something like "I'll confirm the cost basis by next Tuesday and we'll pick this back up then."

## 5. Key takeaways

- Behind a difficult counterpart's behavior may be strategy, anxiety, or a lack of authority.
- In a deadlock, change the direction of your questions instead of repeating the same demand.
- Breaking an issue into smaller pieces lets you build small agreements first.
- Even when pausing, set a resumption point and name what information needs confirming.
