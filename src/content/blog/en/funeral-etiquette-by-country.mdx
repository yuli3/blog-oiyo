## Why funeral etiquette differs so much from country to country

A funeral is where a society's religion, view of death, and community culture all come together most visibly. The same underlying feeling of condolence can be expressed in completely different ways depending on where you are. Show up to a funeral abroad and act on autopilot the way you would at home, and it can easily come across as a serious breach of etiquette. Here's what matters most in each region.

---

## Korea

```
Dress: black, formal, muted colors. No bright colors or
       revealing clothing.
Condolence money: cash in a white envelope, often marked
       "賻儀" (condolence) on the front. Custom favors odd-
       numbered amounts (30,000, 50,000, 70,000, 100,000 won).
Greeting the deceased: incense offering / flower offering at
       the altar → two full bows plus a half-bow toward the
       portrait, then one mutual bow with the chief mourner.
       Religious families may substitute a moment of silence
       or a flower offering instead of bowing.
Words: "I offer my deepest condolences" (삼가 고인의 명복을
       빕니다). No loud talking or laughter.
Food: mourners are typically served food (spicy beef soup,
       sliced boiled meat, etc.); heavy drinking is frowned upon.
```

- If you don't know how to bow properly, offering a flower or bowing your head in silence is a perfectly acceptable substitute.
- Never greet the chief mourner with a casual "hello."

---

## Japan

```
Dress: formal mourning black (喪服, mofuku). Women often wear
       a black dress with pearls (a traditional symbol of tears).
Condolence money: called 香典 (kōden). Given in a special
       black-and-silver-tied envelope (不祝儀袋). Bills should
       be used, not brand-new ones — using crisp new bills
       implies you prepared for the death in advance. The
       envelope is carried wrapped in a small cloth (袱紗,
       fukusa) and presented at the reception desk.
Incense: 焼香 (shōkō) — a pinch of incense powder is raised
       to forehead height, then placed in the incense burner.
Words: "Go shūshō-sama desu" (ご愁傷さまです) — "I'm so sorry
       for your loss."
```

- In Japan, using **crisp, new bills is considered poor form** — the opposite of Korean custom, where fresh bills are common.
- The numbers 4 (死, "death") and 9 (苦, "suffering") are avoided wherever possible.

---

## China

```
Dress: white (白事) was the traditional mourning color; black
       is now common in modern cities too, often mixed with white.
Condolence money: given in a white envelope (never red — red
       envelopes are reserved for celebrations), in an odd amount.
Greeting the deceased: three deep bows from the waist (鞠躬,
       jūgōng) is standard.
Taboos: red and other bright, festive colors are strictly avoided.
```

- Customs vary significantly by region and ethnicity (Han Chinese practices differ from those of ethnic minorities, and from customs in Hong Kong or Taiwan).
- White chrysanthemums are the customary funeral flower.

## The US and the broader West

```
Sequence: a wake/viewing → the funeral service → burial (or
       cremation).
Dress: dark, understated formal wear (black, navy, gray).
       Black is not strictly required.
Condolences: giving cash is not customary. Flowers, a
       sympathy card, or a donation to a charity the family
       has named (often noted as "in lieu of flowers") are
       the standard gestures.
Words: "I'm so sorry for your loss."
Religion: church funerals follow the customs of that specific
       denomination — expect prayers, hymns, and related rites.
```

- **Handing over cash can actually be seen as inappropriate** — a card, flowers, or a donation is the expected gesture instead.
- After the service, guests often bring food to a reception at the family's home (sometimes called a "repast").

---

## The UK

Broadly similar to the US, but with a more reserved tone overall. Black clothing is more common, and "My condolences" is the typical phrase. It's also customary to attach a card with a personal message to any flowers you send.

---

## The Muslim world

```
Burial: burial happens quickly, usually within 24 hours of
       death (cremation is not practiced).
Dress: modest and conservative. Women typically cover their
       hair, arms, and legs.
Mourning: a formal mourning period of about three days
       (Ta'ziyah), during which visitors call on the family
       to offer comfort.
Taboos: flowers, music, and excessive wailing are generally
       discouraged. Visitors often offer the phrase "Inna
       lillahi wa inna ilayhi raji'un" ("To God we belong,
       and to Him we shall return").
```

- Religious observance is taken very seriously — when in doubt, the safest approach is to follow the lead of the local community and the family.

## Universal ground rules

1. Avoid loud talking, laughter, or using your phone.
2. When in doubt about color, always default to **dark and understated**.
3. Don't ask the grieving family for details about the cause of death.
4. If you're unsure of the local religion or customs, **simply follow what the people around you are doing**.

The essence of funeral etiquette isn't the specific ritual — it's **respect for the deceased and the people they left behind**. The forms differ by country, but that underlying respect is universal.
