# Business Strategy — VRIO, Value Innovation, and Strategic Trade-offs

In [Chapter 2](/en/education-business-ch2/), we separated industry structure, resources, activity links, and portfolio classification. This chapter applies those distinctions to a resource bottleneck and a proposed change in customer value. The task is to identify what must be true before a proposal can create and capture value—not to declare success because a framework has been completed.

All businesses, prices, costs, and volumes below are **fictional teaching cases**. They illustrate conditional decisions, not expected returns or observed company performance.

---

## 1. VRIO: locate the missing condition

VRIO asks whether a resource is valuable, rare, costly to imitate or replace, and supported by an organization capable of exploiting it. The questions are cumulative. A Yes for rarity does not overcome a No for value, and a Yes for organization is not another name for a core competence.

<LectureTable
  title="Evidence needed for the VRIO argument"
  headers={["Question", "What would support the answer?"]}
  rows={[
    ["Value", "Customer benefits, revenue effects, or cost reductions relevant to the chosen market"],
    ["Rarity", "Comparison with actual rivals, not a claim that our resource feels distinctive"],
    ["Costly imitation", "Time, cost, causal ambiguity, history, relationships, and possible substitutes"],
    ["Organization", "Decision rights, incentives, access, coordination, and the ability to capture value"],
  ]}
/>

The cumulative outcomes used here are: V = No, disadvantage; V = Yes but R = No, parity; V/R = Yes but I = No, temporary advantage; V/R/I = Yes but O = No, potential advantage not fully exploited; all four Yes, a basis for sustained advantage. This follows Virginia Tech's 2025 analysis tool. Other texts describe the O = No outcome differently, so the important diagnostic is the missing ability to exploit the resource, not memorizing a universal label.

Consider a fictional repair firm with a diagnostic library. Assume customer records support its value, competitor analysis supports rarity, and rebuilding the library would be costly. Only one employee can access it, dispatchers cannot use it, and rewards encourage billable hours rather than rapid resolution. Under those assumptions, **organization is the bottleneck**. Access rules, workflow integration, and incentives need attention; adding more library entries does not by itself solve that bottleneck.

The assumptions still need evidence. If rivals can obtain an equivalent tool cheaply, I may fail. If customers no longer need those repairs, V may fail. “Sustained” does not mean permanent or protected from a changing market. VRIN's N asks about non-substitutability; VRIO's O asks about organization. They are not interchangeable questions.

## 2. Value innovation: make the changes explicit

The Four Actions Framework asks what to eliminate, reduce, raise, and create relative to an industry's offering. The ERRC grid records the proposed actions. These are four complementary questions, not necessarily four steps performed in a fixed order.

<LectureTable
  title="Fictional ERRC proposal for a business-software service"
  headers={["Action", "Proposal and condition to test"]}
  rows={[
    ["Eliminate", "Remove mandatory on-site installation, if customers can deploy safely without it."],
    ["Reduce", "Offer fewer custom reports, if standard reports meet the target users' needs."],
    ["Raise", "Improve onboarding clarity and response reliability; budget for the required support."],
    ["Create", "Add a self-service setup path for smaller customers and test whether they adopt it."],
  ]}
/>

Removing features can lower cost but also destroy utility. Raising service can improve utility but add cost. A new value curve is therefore a proposal, not proof that competition has become irrelevant. The authors' strategic sequence additionally tests **buyer utility, price, cost, and adoption barriers**.

### (1) Worked profit sensitivity

Suppose the existing service has 200 monthly customers, a price of 80 USD, variable cost of 30 USD per customer, and monthly fixed cost of 8,000 USD. The proposed self-service version has a price of 60 USD, variable cost of 18 USD, and fixed cost of 9,000 USD. It targets 300 customers, but that target is not an observed demand estimate. Assume all customers pay, costs have the stated scope, and no other revenues or costs apply.

<FormulaBox
  label="Monthly operating profit in this simplified case"
  formula="Profit = (price − variable cost per customer) × customers − fixed cost"
  description="This is not cash flow or investment return. Implementation investment, financing, tax, churn, and capacity changes would need separate treatment."
/>

<LectureTable
  title="A lower-cost offer can still earn less"
  headers={["Scenario", "Monthly profit calculation"]}
  rows={[
    ["Existing service: 200 customers", "(80 − 30) × 200 − 8,000 = 2,000 USD"],
    ["Proposal: target of 300 customers", "(60 − 18) × 300 − 9,000 = 3,600 USD"],
    ["Proposal: only 220 customers", "(60 − 18) × 220 − 9,000 = 240 USD"],
  ]}
/>

The proposal's lower variable cost does not ensure higher profit. With only 220 customers it earns less than the existing service in this case. Its standalone break-even is 9,000 ÷ 42 ≈ 214.29, so **215 paying customers** cover the stated monthly costs. Covering costs is not the same as improving on the existing 2,000 USD profit; that comparison needs at least **262 customers** under the same assumptions.

Test willingness to adopt and pay, migration effort, lost custom-report utility, support workload, and whether the proposed cost assumptions hold at the target volume. Do not substitute “new market” for those tests.

## 3. Strategic positioning: which activities fit?

Cost advantage and differentiation describe relative positions; competitive scope can be broad or focused. HBS explicitly notes that low cost and differentiation can be achieved together. The trade-off arises when particular products, activities, or promises are incompatible—not merely because both words appear in a plan.

<LectureTable
  title="Generic Strategy Matrix"
  headers={["Scope", "Low Cost", "Unique Value"]}
  rows={[
    ["Broad market", "Cost leadership", "Differentiation"],
    ["Narrow market", "Cost focus", "Differentiation focus"],
  ]}
/>

For example, reducing defects may lower rework costs and improve reliability at the same time. By contrast, a long-run standardized production system may conflict with frequent bespoke orders and very short delivery promises. The analysis needs the activity link and the cost or customer-value effect, not an unsupported company label.

<Callout type="important">
  **Specify the conflict.** Which capacity, activity, incentive, or customer promise would have to change? An efficiency improvement is valuable, but competitors may copy it. It does not by itself establish a distinctive, durable position.
</Callout>

## 4. Worked review questions

### (1) Change the VRIO diagnosis

**Question:** In the repair-library case, another vendor launches an equivalent diagnostic tool that rivals can license cheaply. Assume value remains and the firm's workflow problem is solved. Does O = Yes restore sustained advantage?

**Solution:** No. An accessible equivalent challenges rarity and costly imitation or substitution. If it becomes common, the valuable resource supports parity in the simplified path. If it remains rare briefly but is cheap to reproduce, any advantage is temporary. Organization does not cancel the failed earlier conditions.

### (2) Break-even is not improvement

**Question:** The proposed software offer has 250 paying customers. Is it profitable? Does it exceed the existing service's 2,000 USD monthly profit?

**Solution:** (60 − 18) × 250 − 9,000 = **1,500 USD**. It is profitable under the stated cost assumptions but does not exceed 2,000 USD. To exceed that profit, 42 × customers − 9,000 > 2,000, so customers > 261.90; the smallest whole number is **262**. This is a decision threshold, not a forecast that those customers will arrive.

Applying VRIO and ERRC can expose assumptions and options. Neither tool ensures survival, market leadership, or a permanently uncontested market. Keep a record of the evidence that would change the decision.
