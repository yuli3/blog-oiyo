## Why and how far national taxes come first

The principle in Article 35(1) is simple: **national taxes and enforced collection costs are collected in priority to other public charges and claims.** It is a policy judgment that the state cannot run without taxes, so they rank ahead of private claims.

The problem is the exceptions. If the rule were unlimited, anyone who lent money relying on the land register could be pushed back at any time. So the article sets out five exceptions.

### The statutory date is the dividing line

Item 3 is the heart of this chapter. If a security interest was created **before the statutory date**, the security prevails over the national tax. If not, the national tax prevails.

<FormulaBox
  label="Deciding priority"
  formula="\begin{aligned}\text{Security date} < \text{Statutory date} &\Rightarrow \text{Security first} \\ \text{Security date} > \text{Statutory date} &\Rightarrow \text{National tax first}\end{aligned}"
  description="The statutory date is defined in Article 35(2). Typical examples are the filing date for taxes finalised by return, and the date the payment notice is sent for taxes the government determines or corrects."
/>

The statutory date is neither the payment deadline nor the date of default. It is closer to **the day the tax became visible to the world**. Taxes do not appear on the land register, so the law uses a point in time that someone taking security can check.

Amendments in 2022 and 2023 added item 3-2. Where property subject to a jeonse right or the like is **sold after being transferred, inherited or given**, national taxes the previous owner owed still rank ahead within a certain amount. The owner's earlier arrears follow the property even when the owner changes, which is something to check before signing a jeonse contract.

## Secondary tax liability — liability borne by persons who are not the taxpayer

When the tax cannot be fully collected from the original taxpayer's property, persons in certain relationships bear supplementary liability for **the shortfall**. There are four types.

/>

The common feature is supplementary liability for a shortfall; additional caps differ by category. Article 39’s proportional cap applies to oligopolistic shareholders and partners, not to unlimited-liability partners under item 1. Each provision’s conditions, including the date for assessing investor status, must be checked separately.

<Callout type="warning">
Article 39(2) requires the combined holding of the shareholder or qualifying member and the related persons specified by decree to **exceed 50%**, together with **dominant influence over company management**. Do not confuse qualifying status with the shares or contributions over which rights are actually exercised, used to calculate the cap. Item 3 separately covers oligopolistic members of agricultural or fisheries associations: combined contributions must exceed 50%; where an agreed profit-and-loss ratio differs from contributions, the combined agreed ratio is used instead. Do not import item 2’s management-influence condition into item 3.
</Callout>

## Property-based tax liability of holders of security by transfer (Article 42)

Whereas secondary tax liability is imposed on **persons**, this liability attaches to **property**. If a taxpayer is in arrears and has property held as security by transfer, tax can be collected from that property only when the taxpayer's other property falls short.

The statutory date appears here too. **Property that became security by transfer before the statutory date of the tax** is excluded. The structure protecting whoever took security first is the same as in Article 35.

## Tying it together in one case

Suppose A Co., Ltd. is in arrears on corporate tax.

1. A's real estate is sold at public auction. If a mortgage was created **before the statutory date**, the mortgagee is paid first (Article 35(1)3).
2. If B meets Article 39’s oligopolistic-shareholder conditions, B may answer for the shortfall within the statutory proportional cap. Article 39 excludes A if its shares are listed on the KOSPI or KOSDAQ market (Decree Article 20).
3. In a separate case, suppose B personally owes global income tax. If B’s assets excluding the company shares or interests are insufficient, and an Article 40(1) condition is met, such as no buyer despite a repeat public sale or attempted private-contract sale, the company may be liable. Paragraph 2 caps liability at company net assets × B’s proportion of the monetary amount of shares or contributions. This is not an automatic next step in collecting the company’s debt.
4. In a separate business transfer from A to C, C must comprehensively succeed to the business establishment’s rights and obligations (excluding receivables and payables) and be either related to A or acquiring the business to help A avoid tax (Decree Article 22). Liability arises for the shortfall in A’s assets against that business’s taxes finalised before transfer, capped at acquired property value.

These examples compare different original taxpayers and provisions. They are not a single collection sequence: distinguish each category’s parties, reference date, additional conditions and cap.

## What remains

- The specific types of statutory date (Article 35(2)) vary by tax and method of finalisation. Check the article directly.
- The over-50% threshold and dominant-influence requirement for oligopolistic shareholders are in Article 39 itself. Consult the implementing decree for related persons and the listed-company exclusion.
- The calculation method for item 3-2 (property transferred, inherited or given) is also in the presidential decree. It is a common question in jeonse contract practice.

The next chapter covers **amended returns, requests for correction and returns after the deadline**, the ways of undoing tax paid in error.
