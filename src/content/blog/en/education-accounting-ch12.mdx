[Chapter 3](/en/academy-cost-accounting-ch3/) and [chapter 4](/en/academy-cost-accounting-ch4/) covered job costing and process costing. Real factories often fit neither neatly. This chapter deals with **which system to use when**, and how costs go wrong when the wrong system is chosen.

## 1. Selection criteria

There are two key questions: does **resource consumption differ greatly** from one product unit to another, and is the **cost of tracing** costs to each product less than the value of that information? If both answers are yes, use job costing; if both are no, use process costing.

## 2. Operation costing

Suppose a clothing factory makes cotton shirts and silk shirts. The fabric cost differs greatly between products, but the cutting, sewing and packing operations are the same. Here **direct materials are traced by job (batch)** and **conversion costs are collected by operation and applied at a rate per equivalent unit**.

The ₩12 million of conversion cost divided by 1,500 shirts gives an operation rate of ₩8,000 per shirt, and only the fabric cost is traced by batch.

## 3. Distortion from the wrong system

If the same factory used process costing and divided all costs by total quantity, the cost per shirt would be $5{,}700 / 1{,}500 = 3.8$ (₩10,000).

<FormulaBox
  label="Distortion from averaging"
  formula="\text{Cost distortion} = \text{Average unit cost} - \text{Actual unit cost}"
  description="Cotton shirts: 3.8 − 2.3 = +1.5 (overcosted); silk shirts: 3.8 − 6.8 = −3.0 (undercosted), in ₩10,000. Cotton shirts end up carrying silk shirts' costs — a cost cross-subsidy."

Pricing on these costs would put cotton shirts on the market above competitors, losing share, while silk shirts would be sold below cost at a loss. Choosing a costing system is not an accounting technicality but the basis of pricing and product-mix decisions.

## 4. Service businesses and departmental costing

Hospitals, law firms and accounting firms are classic job-costing businesses, because the time and resources spent differ for each patient or case. Repetitive services such as call centers or bank teller transactions, by contrast, suit an average cost per transaction — the logic of process costing.

With several departments, manufacturing overhead is collected by department and **departmental rates** are used. Applying machine-intensive departments' overhead on machine hours and manual departments' overhead on labor hours is more accurate than a single plantwide rate. Allocating support department costs to production departments is covered in [chapter 8](/en/education-accounting-ch11/).

<Callout type="warning">
A more elaborate system is not always better. Tracing costs takes time and money, and if resource consumption differs little between products, simple process costing is enough. Conversely, if product variety grows and production shifts to small batches of many products while the old simple system stays in use, cost distortions accumulate and profitability judgements are turned upside down. Activity-based costing in [chapter 9](/en/academy-cost-accounting-ch5/) responds to this problem.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

Company TT makes a small part A (10,000 units) and a custom part B (500 units). Direct materials are ₩10,000 per unit for A and ₩60,000 per unit for B. Conversion costs total ₩130 million, and on a machine-hour basis A used 8,000 hours and B 2,000 hours. What are the unit costs of A and B if all costs are divided by total quantity, and if materials are traced and conversion costs applied on machine hours?

Total cost is materials of $10{,}000 + 3{,}000 = 13{,}000$ plus conversion of 13,000, i.e. ₩260 million. Divided by 10,500 units, that is about 2.48 (₩24,800) per unit. Tracing materials and applying conversion costs on machine hours gives a conversion rate of 1.3 (₩13,000) per hour. A costs $1 + 8{,}000 \times 1.3 / 10{,}000 = 2.04$ and B $6 + 2{,}000 \times 1.3 / 500 = 11.2$ (₩10,000). The simple average overcosts A by about ₩4,400 and undercosts B by about ₩87,200.
