### Introduction: Records After an Accident: A Promise for a Safer Tomorrow

The final destination of all safety laws is 'accident prevention' and 'prevention of recurrence.' If an accident occurs despite thorough prevention, what is most important next is prompt **'Reporting'** and objective **'Investigation.'** Only by clearly identifying the cause of the accident can we fill the gaps in the system and avoid repeating the same tragedy.

Today, as the last episode of the Railway Law series, we will summarize the legal procedures for accident response.

---

### 1. Mandatory Reporting of Railway Accidents (Railway Safety Act Article 61)

The first thing after an accident is to report it. Article 61 splits reporting by the size of the accident.

- **Who reports:** **Railway operators, etc.** (operators and facility managers).
- **To whom:** **The Minister of Land, Infrastructure and Transport**.
- **Major accidents (para. 1):** Accidents with many casualties and others set by Presidential Decree are reported **immediately**. (Exams often use 'within 24 hours' as a wrong answer.)
- **Other accidents (para. 2):** The operator **investigates and reports the results**.
- **How:** Set by ministerial ordinance (Enforcement Rule).

### 2. Aviation and Railway Accident Investigation Board (ARAIB) - Independent Investigation Body

So that investigations are not swayed by the operator (KORAIL) or the regulator (the Ministry), a separate independent body investigates, under the Act on Investigation of Aviation and Railway Accidents.

- **Affiliation:** **Under the Prime Minister** (Article 4(1), version in force from 2026-02-28). Older textbooks that say 'under the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport' are now wrong.
- **Independence:** It works without outside instruction or interference in investigations (Article 4(2)).
- **Size:** Up to **11 members** including the chair (Article 6(1)).
- **Notification:** Anyone involved who learns of an accident notifies the board **without delay** (Article 17). This is separate from reporting to the Minister under the Railway Safety Act.
- **Powers (Article 19):** Entering the site and inspecting related items, questioning people, requiring items to be preserved or submitted and retaining them, controlling access to the site. The Act does not use the words 'seizure' or 'search'.
- **Reports and safety recommendations:** The board publishes its report and sends it to relevant agencies (Article 25). It may issue safety recommendations, and the recipient must send an implementation plan **within 90 days** (Article 26).

### 3. Preserving the Site and Evidence (Investigation Act Article 19(2))

Once evidence is gone, finding the cause becomes impossible.

- **Rule:** Anyone asked by the board to preserve an item must not move, alter or damage it.
- **Exception:** Public interest needs and urgent rescue are exceptions. Even then, recording the original state with photos is standard practice.

### 4. Classification of Accidents (Railway Safety Act Enforcement Rule Article 1-2)

This is the most discriminating part of the exam. Railway accidents are first split by whether they relate to **running rolling stock** or **managing railway facilities**.

- **Collision:** Rolling stock hitting or touching another vehicle or obstacle. Hitting animals or birds is excluded.
- **Incidents (준사고):** Dangerous situations that nearly became accidents. They are defined separately from accidents, along with operational disruptions.

---

### Wrapping Up: Investigation is Education, Not Punishment

The purpose of a railway accident investigation is not to punish someone, but to find out 'why the accident happened' to prevent future accidents. Understanding reporting obligations and investigation procedures is the process of having the most honest and courageous attitude as a railroad professional.

**A Solid Foundation: Remember two keywords: 'report immediately' to the Minister (major accidents) and 'notify without delay' to the board, and the status of the 'Aviation and Railway Accident Investigation Board' (ARAIB). Understanding the detailed classification of accidents and incidents is key to grasping the industry's safety culture.
