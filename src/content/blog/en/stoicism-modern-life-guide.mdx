Stoicism began in Greece in the 3rd century BCE and flourished under the Roman Empire. Practiced by everyone from enslaved people to emperors, it remains — 2,300 years later — one of the philosophies most frequently cited by Silicon Valley CEOs, athletes, and therapists.

## The three great Stoics

### Marcus Aurelius (121-180 CE)
Roman emperor and philosopher. His *Meditations* was written as a private journal for himself, and it became one of the greatest works of Stoic literature ever written.

### Epictetus (50-135 CE)
A philosopher born into slavery who found inner freedom even in extraordinarily difficult circumstances.

### Seneca (4 BCE-65 CE)
Imperial advisor and writer who passed on Stoic wisdom through a rich body of letters and essays.

<Callout type="info">
**The core of Stoicism**: "Happiness comes from your inner attitude, not from external circumstances." Wisdom means not clinging to what you can't control, and focusing instead on what you can.
</Callout>

## The core principles of Stoicism

### 1. The Dichotomy of Control

Epictetus's most famous teaching.

**What you can control**:
- Your thoughts and judgments
- Your desires and aversions
- Your actions and reactions

**What you cannot control**:
- Other people's actions and words
- Weather, health, wealth
- Your reputation, status, and death

**In practice**: When you notice anxiety about something outside your control, remind yourself, "This is outside my control. I choose my response to it."

---

### 2. Virtue Is the Only Good

The Stoics held that virtue (arete) is the sole true source of happiness.

**The four cardinal virtues**:
- **Wisdom (phronesis)**: the ability to judge right from wrong
- **Courage (andreia)**: acting rightly in the face of fear
- **Temperance (sophrosyne)**: the ability to regulate desire
- **Justice (dikaiosyne)**: living fairly and rightly

---

### 3. Premeditatio Malorum — Negative Visualization

**A paradoxical but powerful practice**: imagining worst-case scenarios in advance to build gratitude for what you currently have.

**In practice**:
- "What if my health declined?" → gratitude for your health right now
- "What if I lost my partner?" → treasuring the relationship more today
- "What if I lost my job?" → rediscovering the meaning in today's work

This isn't pessimism. It's a practice for living more fully in the present, built on remembering that nothing is guaranteed to last.

---

### 4. Amor Fati — Love Your Fate

A concept Nietzsche borrowed from Stoicism: the attitude of **treating everything that happens as something you would have chosen.**

Marcus Aurelius wrote: "Love what happens to you. It is the will of the universe, and it has meaning for you too."

**In practice**: When something unfortunate happens, ask, "What is this teaching me?"

---

### 5. Memento Mori — Remember You Will Die

The practice of remembering **"you are going to die."** This isn't meant to make life feel bleak — it's meant to sharpen your sense of **how precious this present moment is.**

Marcus Aurelius wrote: "Live each day as if it were your last. But also plan for tomorrow."

---

## Daily Stoic practices

### Morning reflection

1. "What difficulties might I run into today?" (premeditatio malorum)
2. "How would I act virtuously in that situation?"
3. "What can I control today, and what can't I?"

### Evening journal

Seneca's method: ask yourself three questions each evening.
1. "What flaw did I overcome today?"
2. "What virtue did I practice?"
3. "In what way did I improve?"

### Voluntary discomfort

Occasionally choose to experience discomfort on purpose.
- A cold shower for a day
- Skipping a meal
- Eating the cheapest meal you can for a day

This proves to yourself that you can handle hard circumstances if you have to.

## Stoicism and modern psychology

Stoicism is widely regarded as a forerunner of modern **Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT).**

| Stoicism | CBT |
|-----------|-----|
| Event → judgment → emotion | Event → cognition → emotion/behavior |
| Identifying irrational judgments | Identifying automatic thoughts |
| Acting from virtue | Behavioral activation |

**Epictetus**: "People are disturbed not by events, but by their judgments about events."

**Albert Ellis (founder of CBT)**: credited Epictetus as a direct inspiration for developing REBT (Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy).

## Stoicism applied to modern situations

| Modern situation | Stoic approach |
|---------|-----------|
| Comparison anxiety from social media | The dichotomy of control: other people's lives are outside your control |
| Criticism at work | Suspend judgment: first assess whether the criticism is actually fair |
| Financial anxiety | Premeditatio malorum: imagine the worst case, then appreciate what you have now |
| Pain after a breakup | Amor fati: what is this experience teaching you? |
