King Yeomra (閻羅大王) is widely known in East Asian Buddhism and folk belief as the King of the Underworld and the judge who presides over the dead. Originating from the Indian mythological figure Yama, he evolved through Buddhism into the concepts of King Yeomra or King Yeomra the Great within the Chinese and Korean perceptions of hell. In Korea, he appears in various forms, intertwined with the faith of the Ten Kings (Siwang), the Ksitigarbha (Jijang) faith, and shamanic views of the afterlife. Therefore, rather than being a single, uniquely Korean deity, King Yeomra is a figure of the Buddhist underworld system that has come to life within the Korean folk imagination.

## Core Myth — Representative Anecdotes

King Yeomra is depicted as the entity who examines and judges the deeds of the deceased upon their arrival in the underworld. The records of the underworld contain the lifespan and deeds of every individual, and the officials and messengers of the underworld bring the deceased before him. This imagery fosters a moral imagination in which accountability for one's life does not vanish even after death.

In Korean Buddhist art and ritual, King Yeomra appears as one of the Ten Kings (Siwang). The Ten Kings are connected to the belief that the deceased undergo judgment at specific intervals after death, which is linked to rituals such as the 49-day memorial service. While the detailed systems vary by tradition, sect, and region, King Yeomra has been recognized as the representative face of underworld judgment.

In shamanic narratives, King Yeomra frequently appears as an authority figure of the afterlife. The order of the underworld is revealed in stories such as those involving the messenger Gangnim Doryeong, or the journey of Princess Bari as she traverses the paths of the afterlife and life. In these contexts, Yeomra is not merely a demon, but a ruler who governs the world of the dead through law and record.

## Reading as an Archetype — A Jungian Perspective

From a Jungian perspective, King Yeomra is the archetype of the **Judge** and the **Dark King**. He forces humans to confront the consequences of actions they may have ignored. In a modern context, this resembles scenes where avoided responsibilities return in the form of records, such as in legal proceedings, audits, evaluations, or moments of self-reflection.

The essence of the Yeomra archetype is not the punishment itself, but the confrontation. The imagination that someone is watching what I have done creates fear, but it also enables an ethical life. Thus, King Yeomra can be read as both the King of the Afterlife and an internal judge who prompts us to reflect on our actions while we are still alive.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q. Is King Yeomra a Korean deity?**

A. While his origins lie in Indian and Buddhist traditions, in Korea, he has been accepted and adapted into a Korean form through Buddhist rituals, folk beliefs, and shamanic traditions.

**Q. Is King Yeomra a demon of hell?**

A. He is closer to a judge of the underworld than a demon. While he is depicted as an entity that metes out punishment, his core function is the judgment of deeds performed during one's lifetime.
