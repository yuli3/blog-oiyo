# International Trade — Solving Comparative Advantage as a Problem of Production and Consumption

[Chapter 1](/en/education-economics-ch15/) and [chapter 2](/en/education-economics-ch34/) introduced the principle of comparative advantage and trade models. This chapter calculates the range of relative prices and the gains from trade from data on production time. The key is not absolute productivity but **how much of the other good is given up to make one more unit of a good**.

## 1. Opportunity costs from unit labour requirements

Suppose Korea needs 4 hours for one chip and 2 hours for one unit of wheat, while Australia needs 12 and 3 hours.

  title="Technology and opportunity costs"

    ["Korea", "4", "2", "2 wheat"],

Korea has higher absolute productivity in both goods, but its opportunity cost of chips is lower, so it has a comparative advantage in chips. Australia's opportunity cost of wheat is $3/12=0.25$ chips, lower than Korea's $2/4=0.5$ chips.

## 2. The world relative price must lie between the two opportunity costs

If the world price of one chip is 3 units of wheat, Korea gives up 2 wheat at home to make a chip and exchanges it for 3 wheat, gaining 1 wheat per chip. Australia would have to give up 4 wheat to make a chip at home, but through trade it only pays 3, saving 1 wheat.

<FormulaBox
  label="Terms of trade with mutual gains"
  formula="2<\frac{P_{\text{chips}}}{P_{\text{wheat}}}<4"
  description="At the boundary one country is indifferent to trade; outside the range at least one country prefers to produce for itself."
/>

## 3. Trade pushes the consumption possibility set beyond the production possibility set

If Korea has 40 hours of labour, its autarky production possibility line is $4C+2W=40$. Fully specialized in chips, it produces 10. If the world price is 3 wheat per chip, it can export 4 chips and consume 6 chips and 12 wheat. That bundle would take $4×6+2×12=48$ hours, so it lies outside the autarky production possibility set.

This is an expansion of the **consumption** possibility set, not of the production possibility set. The technology itself has not suddenly improved.

## 4. Relative wages are determined jointly with productivity and the pattern of trade

Let Korea's wage be $w_K$ and Australia's $w_A$. The cost of a chip is $4w_K$ and $12w_A$ respectively, and of wheat $2w_K$ and $3w_A$. For Korea to produce chips and Australia wheat, the following range is needed.

<FormulaBox
  label="Relative wages that sustain specialization"
  formula="\frac{12}{4}>\frac{w_K}{w_A}>\frac{3}{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad 1.5<\frac{w_K}{w_A}<3"
  description="If Korea's wage is too high relative to its productivity advantage, it loses competitiveness in chips; if it is too low, Korea produces both goods more cheaply."
/>

In the many-good model, given relative wages, a country produces the goods in which its productivity advantage is largest, and transport costs and non-traded goods move the boundary.

## Check your understanding

If the world price rises to 4 wheat per chip and sits on the boundary of the range, who gets the gains? Korea uses its 40 hours to make 10 chips, exports 4 for 16 wheat, and consumes 6 chips and 16 wheat. That bundle would take $4×6+2×16=56$ hours, even further out than at a price of 3 (48 hours). Australia is indifferent: paying 4 wheat for a chip costs the same as making it. The closer the terms of trade are to one country's opportunity cost, the more the gains go to the other country.
