# Microeconomics — Measure Responses in Ratios, Not Slopes

Chapter 2 used the slopes of curves to compute changes in price and quantity. Slopes depend on units: switching prices from won to thousands of won multiplies the slope by 1,000. **Elasticity** measures changes in percentages and removes units. This chapter computes elasticities and uses them to read revenue and tax burdens.

## 1. Point and arc elasticity

<FormulaBox
  label="Price elasticity of demand"
  formula="\varepsilon = \frac{dQ/Q}{dP/P} = \frac{dQ}{dP}\cdot\frac{P}{Q}"
  description="With demand Q = 100 − 2P and P = 20 won, Q = 60 and ε = −2 × (20/60) = −2/3. Since |ε| < 1, demand is inelastic at this point."
/>

When prices change a lot, the value depends on the starting point, so we use the **arc (midpoint) elasticity**, based on the average of the two points. If the price moves from 10 to 12 won and the quantity from 100 to 80, the midpoint is $P=11$ won, $Q=90$.

$$|\varepsilon| = \frac{20/90}{2/11} \approx 1.22$$

Demand is elastic, so raising the price lowers revenue. Indeed, revenue fell from 1,000 to 960 won.

## 2. Elasticity changes along a linear demand curve

A linear demand curve has a constant slope but its elasticity differs at every point, because $P/Q$ rises as we move up the curve. For $Q=100-2P$, the price intercept is 50 won and the quantity intercept is 100.

## 3. Elasticity and total revenue

Differentiating total revenue $TR=P\cdot Q$ with respect to quantity gives marginal revenue.

<FormulaBox
  label="Marginal revenue and elasticity"
  formula="MR = \frac{dTR}{dQ} = P\left(1-\frac{1}{|\varepsilon|}\right)"
  description="If |ε| > 1, MR > 0: cutting the price to sell more raises revenue. At |ε| = 1, MR = 0 and revenue is at its maximum. If |ε| < 1, revenue rises only when the price is raised."
/>

For the linear demand $Q=100-2P$, revenue peaks at the midpoint ($P=25$ won, $Q=50$) at 1,250 won. In Chapter 7 the conclusion that a monopolist, choosing $MR=MC>0$, **always produces on the elastic part of demand** follows directly from this formula.

## 4. What determines elasticity, and other elasticities

- **Availability of substitutes**: the more close substitutes, the more elastic. Demand for "a particular brand of cola" is far more elastic than demand for "drinks".
- **Share of spending**: the larger the share of income, the more sensitive to price.
- **Time**: demand is more elastic in the long run, when there is time to find alternatives. When petrol prices rise, people drive a little less in the short run but change cars and move house in the long run.

## 5. The less elastic side bears the tax

Take a market with demand $Q=100-P$ and supply $Q=P$ and impose a tax of 10 won per unit. Let consumers pay $P_d$ and producers receive $P_s=P_d-10$. Then $100-P_d=P_d-10$ gives $P_d=55$ won, $P_s=45$ won and $Q=45$. Relative to the pre-tax price of 50 won, consumers bear 5 won and producers 5 won, because the two elasticities are equal at the equilibrium.

<FormulaBox
  label="Splitting the burden"
  formula="\frac{\text{Consumers' burden}}{\text{Producers' burden}} = \frac{\varepsilon_s}{|\varepsilon_d|}"
  description="If supply is twice as elastic as demand, consumers bear twice as much as producers. The side that can escape by responding to price bears less."
/>

Whether the tax is levied on sellers or buyers, the equilibrium wedge in a competitive market is the same. Who hands money to the tax office (statutory incidence) differs from whose welfare falls (economic incidence). The size of the deadweight loss, its relation to the tax rate and the Ramsey rule continue in [Public Finance, Chapter 2](/en/academy-public-finance-ch2/).

<Callout type="warning">
"Firms pay the tax" looks only at statutory incidence. In perfect competition the share passed on is set by elasticities, and under monopoly, depending on the curvature of demand, the price can rise by more than the tax. Elasticity estimates also differ between the short and long run, so say which period's burden you mean.
</Callout>

## Check your understanding

The price of coffee rises from 4,000 to 5,000 won and sales fall from 200 to 180 cups. What is the midpoint elasticity? The quantity change is $20/190\approx 0.105$ and the price change $1{,}000/4{,}500\approx 0.222$, so $|\varepsilon|\approx 0.47$. Demand is inelastic, so revenue rises from 800,000 to 900,000 won. If a rival café opens nearby, elasticity in this range rises and the same price increase could reduce revenue.
